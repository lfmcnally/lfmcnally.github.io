Classicalia as a learning platform

A free learning platform for Classics: a library you can read, a practice engine that remembers you, and a classroom that runs on both. One product across Latin, Greek and Classical Civilisation, for students and teachers, from KS3 to A-level.

Today Classicalia is a collection of good things built one at a time: a marketing site, hundreds of revision pages in six templates, two vocab testers, three quiz engines, a dashboard app with its own sidebar, a teacher app, a tools page and an admin area. Each works. Together they read as separate sites, because each one has its own navigation, its own layout and its own idea of what "progress" means.

The way out is to stop thinking of the site as pages and start thinking of it as three products that share one account, one content model and one design: Revise, Practise and Teach. Subject and level stop being places you navigate to and become a setting that follows you everywhere. Every piece of content becomes a typed item in one library with one reader. Every practice activity becomes a mode of one engine with one progress model. Every teacher action happens in one workspace that can set any library item or practice scope to any class.

What Classicalia is

Revise

One library of everything readable. Browsable by subject, level and topic; searchable; every item in the same reader.

  • Notes (the revision pages)
  • Set texts, with the parallel reader
  • Reading guides and workbooks
  • Videos
  • Exam skills and past-question banks
  • Vocabulary and grammar reference

Practise

One engine, one memory. Everything that asks a question and remembers the answer.

  • Vocabulary (any list)
  • Grammar and endings
  • Content questions (Class Civ)
  • Set-text vocabulary and comprehension
  • Games as a mode, not a separate place
  • Smart practice across every subject you take

Teach

One workspace for a teacher's classes, running on the same library and engine.

  • Classes with join codes
  • Set work: any library item, any practice scope, any test
  • Results and insights across all of it
  • Classroom tools: live Do Now, visualiser, printables
  • Teacher resources: mark schemes, schemes of work

Underneath the three sits one account: your subjects and levels, your progress across all of them, your classes, your friends. And one home that is a front door when signed out and your progress page when signed in.

What this is not: a set of course pages. A student taking GCSE Latin and GCSE Classical Civilisation has one library view, one practice page, one progress page and one list of set work. The course is a filter on each of them, never a place with its own layout.

The shell: one frame for everything

Signed out, a white top bar: Revise, Practise, Teach, search, sign in. Signed in, a navy left rail with the same items plus Home and Progress, and a top bar for search and account. The content area is identical in both. Nothing else on the site has its own navigation. Tabs appear only inside a screen (a class page, a reader) and sidebars only for a document's own contents.

The starfield and the drawn wordmark remain the brand, used where brand belongs: the signed-out home, the About page, printed covers. Everywhere else the surfaces are light and quiet, so the content and the student's own progress are the colour on the page.

Revise: one library

Every readable thing on the site becomes an item with the same metadata: type, subject, level, exam board, topic, length, status. Items are shown with one card everywhere: in the library, in search, on a student's home, in a teacher's set-work picker. Subject carries a colour, used as a thin stripe and a label, so a three-subject site is navigable by eye.

The library opens filtered to your subjects and levels. Change the filter and the same screen shows GCSE Latin, or everything, or just set texts. A hand-built hub per exam component is replaced by one screen that any filter can produce, which is also what makes honest coverage automatic: an item that is not written is not in the library.

The citadel from the east · photo

Key facts

Type a fortified, palace-centred citadel; the leading centre of the Mycenaean world. Built roughly the 17th to 11th century BC, at its height c.1350–1200 BC. Location a hilltop in the north-east Peloponnese commanding the Argive plain.

Why it matters

Homer calls it "rich in gold" (Iliad 11.25), and the gold from the shaft graves bears that out. Its commanding position controlled the plain below, which helps explain its power. It was abandoned around 1200–1100 BC as the wider Bronze Age world collapsed.

Exam focus

What evidence does Mycenae give for the wealth of its rulers? Give two features of the citadel that show it was built for defence. How far does the archaeology support Homer's picture?

Practise: one engine, one memory

Today there are two vocab testers, three quiz pages, a grammar gym, two endings drills, a practice test and three games, with four different ideas of saving. The platform has one practice engine with modes: vocabulary, grammar, content questions, set-text vocabulary, and games as a presentation of the same questions. Every answer goes into one mastery store, so one map describes a student across subjects, and a teacher's homework, a live Do Now and a student's own revision are the same activity with different scopes.

The Practise page opens with the one thing that matters: Smart practice, a session built from the student's weakest and overdue items across every subject they take. Below it, the ways to choose for yourself.

Teach: one workspace

A teacher's classes, set work, results and tools in one place, built on the same library and engine their students use. Setting work is one action with one picker: choose anything in the library (a note, a text, a guide, a video), any practice scope (a vocabulary range, a topic, a set text's words), or a test (written here or taken from the bank), add a class and a date. Results is one list of everything set, each row opening the existing detail. Insights sit on the class overview and every insight ends in a button that sets something.

Classroom tools (the live Do Now console, the visualiser, the printables) are a tab in Teach, not a separate public page. Teacher resources (mark schemes, schemes of work, slide decks) are library items visible only to verified teachers, in the same reader.

The design language

A reading room rather than a night sky. The current site puts a 230-pixel navy hero with a starfield on every page, which is a marketing device, and sets body text at 14px in cards of many shapes. The platform keeps the brand (the drawn wordmark, the navy, the constellation) for brand moments and gives everything else a calm product surface built from a small set of rules.

Subject colour is the organising device. It appears as a stripe on every item card, a label on every page, the fill of every progress ring and bar. A student who takes two subjects can tell them apart across the whole site without reading. Navy is reserved for chrome and the one primary action on a screen. Progress states are the only other colours, with one meaning each.

Two faces, one for reading (headings, notes, Latin and Greek text) and one for the interface. Base size 16 to 17px; reading measure about 65 characters; every label in the same small uppercase style. The drawn Classicalia wordmark is the only piece of lettering outside these two faces.

Rules

  • One shell. Rail or top bar, identical content area. No page has its own navigation.
  • One card. Every item of content, every practice scope and every piece of set work is shown with the same card: subject stripe, type, title, meta, progress.
  • One reader. Contents on the left, body in the middle, "practise this" and "next" on the right. Set texts, videos and workbooks change the body only.
  • One primary action per screen, in navy. Everything else is a quiet button.
  • Light surfaces. The starfield appears on the signed-out home, About and print covers. Nowhere else.
  • Phone first for students. Bottom bar, one column, tap targets of 44px, and nothing that only works on hover.
  • Print is a feature. Notes, set texts, workbooks and results print cleanly from the reader.

Getting there

Each phase ships on its own and the site works throughout. The order puts the content model first because everything else is built on it.

1

The library index. Describe every real piece of content in one manifest (type, subject, level, topic, URL, written or not). Build Revise and search on top of the existing pages. The subject and level picker arrives here.

Nothing is rewritten yet; the pages get a library around them.
2

The shell. One header and rail on every page, rendered from one definition; the account state visible everywhere; dead links gone.

The first time the site feels like one product.
3

The reader. One template for notes, guides, set texts, workbooks and videos; existing pages migrated into it; print built in.

Six templates become one.
4

The engine. One practice engine and mastery store behind every mode; Smart practice across subjects; Progress across subjects; games on the same questions.

Four ways of saving become one.
5

Teach. The class workspace, the single set-work picker, the single results list, join codes, teacher verification.

Everything a teacher can set is anything in the library or engine.
6

Addresses and hygiene. Clean URLs at the root, redirects for everything old, a build step that generates the site from the manifest so it stays consistent.

The end of /version2/.

Decisions this needs

  • Three products (Revise, Practise, Teach) with subject and level as a global setting, rather than pages per course.
  • A product shell (rail when signed in) instead of the marketing header on every page.
  • Subject colours as the site's organising device.
  • One reader and one practice engine, which means retiring the existing templates and testers as the content migrates.
  • Moving to clean URLs at the root with redirects.
  • A build step driven by a content manifest.
