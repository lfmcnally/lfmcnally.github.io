The verb sum

sum, es, est: "I am", "you are", "he is", and why the noun that goes with it is nominative.
Try it →
Lesson 1.5Foundations

The verb "to be"

sum means "I am". It is irregular: it does not follow the pattern of porto, so it has to be learned on its own. It is also the commonest verb in Latin.

PersonLatinEnglish
IsumI am
you (one)esyou are
he, she, itesthe is
wesumuswe are
you (more than one)estisyou are
theysuntthey are
The endings are mostly the ones you know: es, es-t, su-mus, es-tis, su-nt. Only sum itself ends in -m instead of -o.

The noun after sum

sum does nothing to anyone, so it has no object. The noun that goes with it names the subject again, so it takes the same case as the subject, the nominative. This noun is called the complement.

femina dea est.
The woman is a goddess.
dea is nominative, like femina: they are the same person. Not deam.
the subjectthe complement: nominative too
servi sumus.
We are slaves.
No separate word for "we": sumus has it. servi is nominative plural to match.
  • After sum the noun is nominative, never accusative.
  • In English, "a" usually sounds best before the complement: "he is a messenger".
  • est or sunt at the start of a sentence often means "there is" or "there are": est nuntius, "there is a messenger".

Translating sentences with sum

  • Find the part of sum and its person, usually at the end.
  • Translate the subject, then "is" or "are", then the complement.
  • If there is only one nominative, the subject is inside the verb: nuntius est, "he is a messenger".
amici nuntii non sunt.
The friends are not messengers.
non sits before the verb, as with any other verb.

Watch out

Common mistakes
  • The complement is nominative: "the woman is a goddess" is femina dea est, not deam.
  • est is "is" and sunt is "are": check whether the subject is singular or plural.
  • es is "you are", for one person. It is not "he is".
