Prepositions with the accusative

ad, in, per, circum, contra: small words that say where, and put the noun after them in the accusative.
Try it →
Lesson 1.6Foundations

What a preposition does

A preposition is a small word in front of a noun that says where or how: "to the house", "through the garden". After these five, the Latin noun is always in the accusative.

servus ad villam ambulat.
The slave walks to the house.
ad takes the accusative: villam, not villa.
the prepositionits noun, in the accusative
  • The preposition and its noun make a phrase. Translate them together.
  • An accusative after a preposition is not the object of the verb. It is simply the case the preposition takes.
  • Most of these five describe movement: going to, into, through or around something.

The five prepositions

LatinEnglishExample
adto, towardsad villam: to the house
ininto, ontoin hortum: into the garden
perthrough, alongper insulam: through the island
circumaroundcircum villam: around the house
contraagainstcontra dominum: against the master
in has a second meaning, "in" or "on", with a different case. That is in Chapter 2. For now, in with the accusative means movement: "into" or "onto".

Translating the phrase

  • Find the preposition, then the noun after it in the accusative.
  • Keep the pair together in English: "into the garden".
  • Then the rest of the sentence as usual: subject, verb, object.
servi contra dominum pugnant.
The slaves fight against the master.
puellae in hortum ambulant.
The girls walk into the garden.
in with the accusative is "into", not "in".
nuntius epistulam ad dominum portat.
The messenger carries the letter to the master.
Two accusatives with two jobs: epistulam is the object, and dominum is accusative because of ad.

Watch out

Common mistakes
  • in with the accusative is "into" or "onto", not "in".
  • A noun straight after a preposition belongs to the preposition, not to the verb.
  • circum is "around" and contra is "against": they are easy to mix up.
