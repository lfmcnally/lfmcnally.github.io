The dative and the indirect object

Giving, owing and preparing: the dative for the person something is given to, or done for.

Direct and indirect objects

In "the master gives the slave food", the food is what is given: the direct object, in the accusative. The slave is the person it is given to: the indirect object, in the dative.

dominus servo cibum dat.
The master gives food to the slave.
cibum is what is given; servo is who gets it.
the indirect object, in the dativethe direct object, in the accusative
  • The indirect object is the person something is given, brought or owed to.
  • Translate the dative with "to" or "for": "gives food to the slave". English can also leave the word out: "gives the slave food".
  • A dative can also say who something is done for: cenam dominae parat, "she prepares dinner for the mistress".

The 1st declension dative, -ae, looks like the genitive and the nominative plural. The 2nd declension dative, -o, looks like the ablative. A verb of giving is the best clue.

libertus domino pecuniam debet.
The freedman owes money to the master.
ancilla dominae cenam parabat.
The slave-girl was preparing dinner for the mistress.

Translating the dative

  • Find the verb and ask: is something being given, brought or owed? Then look for the dative.
  • Put "to" or "for" before it, or use the English order "gives the slave food". Both are right.
  • Check the other cases: -ae and -o each have more than one job.

Watch out

Common mistakes
  • In puellae cibum dant, there is no other subject, so puellae is "the girls". In femina puellae cibum dat, it is "to the girl".
  • The dative is never the subject: servo dat is "gives to the slave".
  • The direct object is the thing given; the indirect object is the person who gets it.
  • "Gives food to the slave" and "gives the slave food" are both right.
