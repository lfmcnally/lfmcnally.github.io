Purpose clauses

Saying why something is done: ut or ne, a verb in the subjunctive, and a clause that answers the question "what for?"
Grammar gym →
Lesson 3.1Subordinate clauses

What a purpose clause does

A purpose clause tells you why someone does something. In English we usually say "to" or "in order to": the soldiers hurried to the city to save the citizens. Latin does it with a small clause of its own, introduced by ut and with its verb in the subjunctive.

milites ad urbem festinaverunt ut cives servarent.
The soldiers hurried to the city to save the citizens.
ut opens the clause, servarent (imperfect subjunctive) closes it. Everything between them belongs to the purpose.

The clause answers "what for?". It is never a fact: the soldiers may or may not save anyone. That is why Latin uses the subjunctive, the mood for things wished, intended or imagined rather than things that happened.

  • ut introduces a positive purpose: "to", "so that".
  • ne introduces a negative purpose: "so that ... not", "in order not to". Latin never uses ut non for a purpose.
  • The verb of the clause is in the subjunctive and usually comes at the end of the clause.
  • se or suus inside the clause refers back to the subject of the main verb: servus tacuit ne dominus se puniret, "the slave kept quiet so that his master would not punish him" (the slave).

Which subjunctive: the sequence of tenses

The tense of the subjunctive follows the tense of the main verb. There are only two choices at GCSE.

Main verbSequenceSubjunctive in the clauseExample
present, future, perfect with "have"primarypresent subjunctivemurum aedificant ut hostes arceant

Most GCSE passages tell a story in the past, so most purpose clauses you meet have the imperfect subjunctive. It is easy to form: take the present infinitive and add the personal endings.

The present subjunctive changes its vowel by conjugation: portet, moneat, regat, audiat, and sit from sum. A good check: if the vowel before the ending looks "wrong" for the verb (portet instead of portat), you are looking at a subjunctive.

Translating it

English has several ways to say the same thing. Any of these earns the mark, as long as the clause reads as a purpose.

  • to: ut cives servarent, "to save the citizens". The shortest, and usually the best.
  • in order to, so as to: the same, a little more formal.
  • so that ... might / could / would: use this when the subject of the clause is different from the subject of the main verb: nuntium misi ut tibi haec diceret, "I sent a messenger so that he might tell you this".
  • Negative, with ne: "so that ... would not", "in order not to", "to avoid ...ing". "Lest" is correct but old-fashioned.
servus tacuit ne dominus se puniret.
The slave kept quiet so that his master would not punish him.
Different subjects, so "so that ... would not" reads better than "in order not to". se is the slave.
Romani murum aedificant ut hostes arceant.
The Romans are building a wall to keep off the enemy.
Present main verb, so the present subjunctive arceant. "To keep off" is enough.
rex nuntios misit qui pacem peterent.
The king sent messengers to ask for peace.
A relative purpose clause: qui with the subjunctive does the job of ut. You will meet this now and then; translate it just like ut.

Not to be confused with

ut has three other common jobs. The subjunctive and the signposts in the main clause tell them apart.

Looks like
How to tell
Translate
Result clause
tam fortis erat ut hostes fugerent
A signpost in the main clause: tam, tantus, tot, adeo, ita, sic. The negative is ut non, never ne.
"so ... that": he was so brave that the enemy fled.
Indirect command
dominus servo imperavit ut laboraret
A verb of ordering, asking or persuading before it: impero, rogo, persuadeo, moneo, oro.
"to": the master ordered the slave to work. The same English as a purpose, so either way of reading it is safe.
ut with the indicative
ut dixi, servus fugit
No subjunctive. The verb is an ordinary indicative.
"as" or "when": as I said, the slave ran away.
Purpose clause
servus fugit ut liber esset
No signpost, no verb of ordering, subjunctive verb. It answers "what for?"
"to", "in order to", "so that ... might".

In the exam

Exam focus
  • Find ut or ne, then find the subjunctive verb that closes the clause. Translate the main clause first, then add the purpose.
  • "To" is a complete and correct translation of ut + subjunctive. Do not write "that" for a purpose clause: "the soldiers hurried that they save the citizens" loses the mark.
  • ne is not non. "ne ... puniret" is "so that he would not punish", and the "not" belongs to the clause, not to the main verb.
  • Check who se is. In a purpose clause it points back to the subject of the main verb.
  • In a comprehension question, "why did the soldiers hurry?" is almost always answered by the purpose clause. Quote the Latin from ut to the verb.

Try it

Two short exercises. Nothing is saved; they are here so you can check you have it before the grammar gym tracks you.

Which translation?

0 of 5

Pick the translation that reads the ut or ne clause correctly.

1puella in horto manebat ut flores legeret.
Subjunctive legeret, no signpost, answers "what for?": a purpose. "When" would need the indicative; "so that she picked" turns it into a result.
2cives portas clauserunt ne hostes urbem intrarent.
ne makes the purpose negative, and the "not" belongs to intrarent. The citizens did close the gates.
3tam celeriter cucurrit ut nemo eum caperet.
tam in the main clause is the signpost of a result clause: "so quickly that". A purpose with a negative would use ne, not nemo with ut.
4pater filium ad Graeciam misit ut litteras disceret.
ut + imperfect subjunctive after a past main verb: a purpose. The son is sent in order to learn.
5ut dixi, servi iam fugerant.
dixi is indicative, so this ut is not a purpose at all. With the indicative, ut means "as" or "when".

Which subjunctive?

0 of 5

Choose the form of the verb that fits the sequence.

1milites castra muniunt ut hostes ___.
muniunt is present, so the sequence is primary and the clause takes the present subjunctive, arceant. arcent is indicative and cannot stand in a purpose clause.
2rex nuntium misit ut pacem ___.
misit is perfect, a past tense, so the sequence is historic: imperfect subjunctive, peteret (infinitive petere + t).
3servi tacebant ne dominus iratus ___.
tacebant is imperfect, so historic sequence: esset, the imperfect subjunctive of sum.
4ad forum festino ut amicum ___.
festino is present: primary sequence, present subjunctive videam. Notice the vowel: video becomes videam.
5puer ad villam cucurrit ut matrem ___.
cucurrit is perfect: historic sequence, so moneret. monebat is the imperfect indicative, which looks similar but has no place in a purpose clause.
