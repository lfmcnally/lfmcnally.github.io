Chapter 25 (V.25)

Psyche watches Cupid fly away · Cupid & Psyche · GCSE Latin · OCR J282/03

1Psyche vero, humi prostrata et, quantum visu poterat, volatus mariti prospiciens, extremis affligebat lamentationibus animum.
1Psyche meanwhile, flung down on the ground and, watching the flight of her husband as far as she could with her eyes, tormented her heart with the most intense laments.
2sed ubi remigio plumae raptum maritum proceritas spatii fecerat alienum, per proximi fluminis marginem praecipitem sese dedit.
2But when the great distance had taken from her the husband carried off by the rowing of his wings, she threw herself headlong from the bank of the nearest river.
3sed mitis fluvius, in honorem dei scilicet, qui consuevit urere et ipsas aquas, metuens sibi, confestim eam innoxio volumine super ripam florentem herbis exposuit.
3But the gentle stream, no doubt in honour of the god who is accustomed to set even the waters themselves on fire, was afraid for itself, and at once laid her on its bank, rich with flowers and grass, with a harmless swirl of its current.

Style notes this section · 14Repetition and structure · 3Sound and metre · 1Word choice · 4Imagery and tone · 5Grammar · 1
humi prostrata … volatus maritiContrast
flung on the ground … her husband's flight
Psyche lies flat on the earth while Cupid soars away, so the distance between them is shown as a picture.
quantum visu poterat … prospiciensVivid detail
watching as far as she could see
The reader shares her straining gaze as she follows him until he is out of sight.
extremis affligebat lamentationibus animumPathos
she tormented her heart with the most intense laments
Psyche’s grief is heaped up: the superlative extremis lamentationibus ("with the bitterest of laments") frames the verb affligebat ("battered, tormented"). She does not merely weep — she beats her own heart with sorrow.
affligebatImperfect tense
she kept tormenting
The imperfect shows her grief going on and on, not a single cry.
remigio plumae raptumEpic Metaphor
carried off by the oarage of his wings
The phrase remigium plumae ("the oarage of his wing") is a grand, almost epic metaphor — Cupid "rows" through the air on his feathers, as a ship is rowed over the sea. The lofty image suits the god’s soaring departure.
fecerat alienumWord choice
had made him another's
alienus means "belonging to someone else": distance has taken her husband from her. The word stresses that he is no longer hers.
per proximi fluminis marginem praecipitemAlliteration
over the edge of the nearby river, headlong
The repeated p sounds give the clause a hurried, falling movement as she throws herself in.
praecipitem sese deditSuicidal Despair
she flung herself headlong
Bereft, Psyche tries to end it all: praecipitem sese dedit — "she threw herself headlong" into the river. The violent reflexive recalls her earlier suicide attempt with the blade; grief drives her again to self-destruction.
praecipitem sese deditEcho
she threw herself headlong
The same phrase describes the sisters' fatal leaps in sections 11 and 12. Psyche is saved and they are not, which shows whom the gods favour.
mitis fluvius ... metuens sibi ... exposuitPersonification
the gentle stream ... fearing for itself ... set her down
The river is fully personified: mitis ("gentle"), it is metuens sibi ("afraid for itself") of Cupid’s wrath, and so carefully sets Psyche down unharmed on a flowery bank. Even nature conspires to keep her alive for the trials ahead.
in honorem dei scilicetIrony
in honour of the god, of course
The narrator's "of course" is tongue in cheek: the river is less moved by pity than by fear of Cupid.
qui et ipsas aquas urere consuevitWitty Paradox
who is accustomed to set even the waters themselves on fire
A clever conceit explains why the river spares her: it honours Cupid, the god whose fire of love can even set water itself ablaze. The paradox of fire burning water captures the irresistible, boundary-breaking power of love.
innoxio volumineWord choice
with a harmless eddy
The rescue is gentle: the river that could have drowned her sets her down without harm.
ripam florentem herbisVivid detail
a bank rich with grass
The soft, flowery bank is a peaceful landing place, in contrast with her despair a moment before.
Notes on the text 1
qui consuevit urere et ipsas aquasRivers and Cupid's fire
who is used to setting even the waters on fire
Rivers were thought of as gods. Love was pictured as a fire, and Cupid as carrying a torch as well as a bow; the joke is that even a river god has felt his flames and fears them.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. How does Apuleius make the reader feel Psyche's despair in this section?
  2. The river saves Psyche "in honour of the god". Is this passage meant to be sad, funny, or both?
  3. Compare Psyche's leap with her sisters' leaps in sections 11 and 12. Why does she survive when they do not?
Pan and Echo background

The set text leaves out the end of chapter 25. On the river bank Psyche meets Pan, the rustic god of shepherds, who is sitting with the nymph Echo and teaching her to sing. He guesses that she is lovesick, tells her not to try to kill herself again, and advises her to win Cupid back with prayers. Section 9 begins just as he finishes speaking.
