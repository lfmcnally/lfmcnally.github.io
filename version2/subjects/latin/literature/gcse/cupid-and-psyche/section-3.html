1iam -que lassa, salute defecta, dum saepius divini vultus intuetur pulchritudinem, recreatur animi.
1And now, weary and robbed of her well-being, while she gazes again and again at the beauty of the divine face, she is revived in spirit.
2videt capitis aurei genialem caesariem ambrosia temulentam, cervices lacteas genas -que purpureas pererrantes crinium globos decoriter impeditos, alios antependulos, alios retropendulos, quorum splendore nimio fulgurante iam et ipsum lumen lucernae vacillabat.
2She sees the glorious hair of his golden head, drenched in ambrosia, and the curls gracefully tangled as they stray over his milk-white neck and rosy cheeks, some hanging in front, others behind; and at their dazzling, flashing brilliance even the light of the lamp began to flicker.
3per umeros volatilis dei pinnae roscidae micanti flore candicant et quamvis alis quiescentibus extimae plumulae tenellae ac delicatae resultantes tremule lasciviunt inquieta.
3On the shoulders of the winged god, the dewy wings gleam white with a glistening bloom, and although his wings are at rest, the soft and delicate little feathers at the edges, bouncing tremulously, frolic restlessly.
4ceterum corpus glabellum atque luculentum et quale peperisse Venerem non paeniteret.
4The rest of his body was smooth and shining, the kind of body that Venus would not regret having given birth to.
5ante lectuli pedes iacebat arcus et pharetra et sagittae, magni dei propitia tela.
5At the foot of the little bed lay the bow, the quiver and the arrows, the kindly weapons of the great god.

Style notes this section · 16Repetition and structure · 4Word choice · 5Imagery and tone · 7
lassa, salute defecta … recreaturContrast
weary, all hope gone … she is revived
The sentence moves from exhaustion to new life, which shows the power of Cupid's beauty over her.
saepius … intueturEmphasis
she gazes again and again
saepius shows her looking repeatedly, as if she cannot believe what she sees, and it is the looking itself that revives her.
capitis aurei genialem caesariem ambrosia temulentamSensuous Imagery
the delightful hair of his golden head, dripping with ambrosia
The blazon of Cupid opens with his hair: aureum caput ("golden head"), the genialis caesaries ("delightful locks") and ambrosia temulenta — "drunk/soaked with ambrosia". The divine perfume and golden gleam mark him at once as a god.
aurei … lacteas … purpureasColour imagery
golden … milk-white … rosy
Three colours paint Cupid like a picture: gold hair, a white neck and red cheeks, the ideal of ancient beauty.
pererrantesWord choice
wandering
The curls are said to wander over his cheeks, which makes even his hair seem alive and playful.
decoriterUnusual word
gracefully
A rare adverb of the kind Apuleius enjoys. The unusual word suits a description of something extraordinary.
alios antependulos, alios retropendulosCoinage & Antithesis
some hanging in front, others hanging behind
Apuleius coins a matched pair of compound adjectives — ante-pendulos and retro-pendulos ("fore-hanging" / "back-hanging") — set in neat antithesis (alios ... alios). The invented words are part of his showy, novel style.
splendore nimio fulguranteHyperbole
flashing with excessive splendour
His hair shines so brightly that it outdoes the lamp, an exaggeration which shows that he is a god.
et ipsum lumen lucernae vacillabatPersonification
even the very light of the lamp flickered
Cupid’s radiance is so great (splendore nimio fulgurante) that the lamp’s own flame vacillabat — wavers, as if dazzled. The light that exposed him is itself outshone.
pinnae roscidae micanti flore candicantImagery
the dewy feathers gleam white with a glistening bloom
The wings are painted in light and moisture: roscidae ("dewy"), candicant ("gleam white"), micanti flore ("with a sparkling bloom"). The downy feathers are made to shimmer like petals wet with dew.
alis quiescentibus … inquietaContrast
though his wings are still … restless
The wings are at rest but the tiny feathers at their tips keep moving, so even in sleep the god seems full of life.
plumulae tenellae ac delicatae ... inquieta lasciviuntDiminutives
the delicate, dainty little feathers ... frisk restlessly
A cluster of soft diminutives and playful verbs — plumulae ("little feathers"), tenellae ("ever so delicate"), lasciviunt ("frisk, play"), resultantes tremule ("quivering tremulously") — gives the feathers a restless, almost mischievous life of their own.
glabellum … lectuliDiminutives
smooth little … little bed
More diminutive forms keep the description soft and delicate, as if everything about Cupid is precious.
quale peperisse Venerem non paeniteretPraise by Litotes
such as Venus would not regret having borne
His body is so perfect that not even his mother Venus, goddess of beauty, would regret having given birth to him. The understatement (litotes — "would not regret") is the highest possible compliment.
arcus et pharetra et sagittaePolysyndeton
bow and quiver and arrows
The repeated et lists the weapons slowly, one by one, and draws the eye to the arrows that will matter in the next section.
arcus et pharetra et sagittae, magni dei propitia telaOminous Detail
the bow, quiver and arrows, the gracious weapons of the mighty god
The blazon ends on the weapons lying at the bed’s foot. propitia tela ("gracious/kindly weapons") is gently ironic: these are the arrows that wound gods and mortals with love — and Psyche is about to handle one.

A description like a painting background

This section is an ecphrasis: a detailed description of a work of art, or of something seen as if it were one. Apuleius describes Cupid as a painter would show him, from his golden hair down to his body and the weapons at the foot of the bed. Readers would have known images of Eros as a beautiful winged youth from statues, wall paintings and mosaics, and Apuleius invites them to see him through Psyche's eyes.
