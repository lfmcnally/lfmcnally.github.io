Chapter 22 (V.22)

At the sight of Cupid, Psyche abandons her plan and tries to commit suicide · Cupid & Psyche · GCSE Latin · OCR J282/03

English
1tunc Psyche, et corporis et animi alioquin infirma fati tamen saevitia subministrante viribus roboratur, et prolata lucerna et adrepta novacula sexum audacia mutavit.
1Then Psyche, although otherwise weak in both body and mind, is nevertheless strengthened, as the cruelty of Fate supplies her with strength; she brings out the lamp, seizes the razor, and in her boldness changes her sex.
2sed cum primum luminis oblatione tori secreta claruerunt, videt omnium ferarum mitissimam dulcissimam -que bestiam, ipsum illum Cupidinem formonsum deum formonse cubantem
2But as soon as the secrets of the bed were revealed by the bringing of the light, she sees the gentlest and sweetest beast of all wild creatures, Cupid himself, the handsome god, lying there handsomely.
3cuius aspectu lucernae quoque lumen hilaratum increbruit et acuminis sacrilegi novaculam paenitebat.
3At the sight of him even the light of the lamp grew brighter, as if in joy, and the razor repented of its sacrilegious edge.
4at vero Psyche tanto aspectu deterrita et impos animi, marcido pallore defecta tremens -que desedit in imos poplites et quaerit abscondere, ferrum sed in suo pectore.
4But Psyche, terrified by so great a sight and no longer in control of her mind, faint with a deathly paleness and trembling, sank down onto her knees and tries to hide the blade, but in her own breast.
5quod profecto fecisset, nisi ferrum timore tanti flagitii manibus temerariis delapsum evolasset.
5And she would surely have done it, had the blade not slipped from her rash hands in horror at so great a crime and flown away.

Style notes this section · 16Word order · 2Repetition and structure · 4Word choice · 2Imagery and tone · 7Grammar · 1
et corporis et animiPairing
both in body and in mind
et … et stresses that she is weak in every way, which makes her sudden boldness all the more surprising.
fati … saevitia subministrantePersonification
with the cruelty of Fate supplying
Fate is cruel and active, like an accomplice handing her strength, which suggests that she is being driven towards disaster.
viribus roboraturEmphasis
she is strengthened with strength
Both words mean strength, so the doubling stresses how unnatural her new courage is.
prolata lucerna et adrepta novaculaParallelism
bringing out the lamp and seizing the razor
Two matching ablative absolutes give the two tools of the plan equal weight: a light to see by and a blade to kill with.
sexum audacia mutavitBold Diction
she changed her sex by her daring
A startling phrase: Psyche, helped by Fate, "changes her sex" — she finds a man’s courage. audacia ("daring") is the means; the verb mutavit makes the transformation sound almost physical, fitting a book of Metamorphoses.
luminis oblatione tori secreta clarueruntSymbolism
as the light was offered, the secrets of the bed became clear
The secret marriage is revealed by light. The lamp breaks the one rule her husband gave her, which was never to see him.
omnium ferarum mitissimam dulcissimamque bestiamIrony
the gentlest and sweetest beast of all wild animals
The "monster" her sisters described is redefined with two superlatives, mitissimam and dulcissimam. To call a god the gentlest and sweetest bestia of all wild animals is pointed irony — the dreaded beast is Love himself.
ipsum illum CupidinemDelay
Cupid himself
The name is held back until after the description of the "beast", so the reader shares Psyche's shock at the revelation.
formonsum deum formonse cubantemPolyptoton
a handsome god reclining handsomely
The adjective formonsum and the adverb formonse frame deum in a play on the same root (polyptoton). The repeated sound lingers admiringly on Cupid’s beauty — handsome, and handsomely posed.
lucernae quoque lumen hilaratum increbruitPersonification
even the light of the lamp grew brighter, cheered
The lamp’s flame is personified: it is hilaratum ("gladdened") and flares up at the sight of the god. The inanimate light shares in the wonder of the scene.
hilaratum … deterritaContrast
cheered … terrified
The lamp brightens with joy while Psyche is terrified, an ironic contrast between the object and the woman holding it.
acuminis sacrilegi novaculam paenitebatPersonification
the razor regretted its profane sharpness
The razor too is personified — it "regrets" (paenitebat) its sacrilegum acumen, its profane sharpness, as if ashamed to be raised against a god. Both lamp and blade recoil from the deed.
marcido pallore defectaVivid detail
fainting with a sickly pallor
The picture of her pale, failing body shows the physical shock of what she has seen.
sed in suo pectoreSurprise ending
but in her own breast
"She tries to hide the blade" seems to mean concealing it, until the last words reveal she means plunging it into her own heart.
fecisset, nisi … evolassetConditional
she would have done it, if it had not flown away
The past unreal condition shows how close she came to death, and how only the blade's "fear" saved her.
ferrum ... manibus temerariis delapsum evolassetPersonification
the blade slipped from her rash hands and flew away
In the unreal condition the blade saves the day: timore ... delapsum, it slips from her manus temerariae in fear and "flies away" (evolasset). The weapon acts with a will of its own.
Notes on the text 2
lucerna … novaculaThe lamp and the razor
lamp … razor
The two tools the sisters told her to hide: the lamp to see the "monster" and the razor to cut off its head.
CupidinemCupid
Cupid
Cupid (Greek Eros) is the son of Venus and the god of love, usually shown with wings, a bow and arrows. Here he is not a child but a handsome young god.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. How does Apuleius make Psyche's sudden courage seem strange and unnatural?
  2. Why do you think the name Cupidinem is held back until after the description of the "beast"?
  3. The lamp and the razor both seem to have feelings in this passage. What does this add to the scene?
