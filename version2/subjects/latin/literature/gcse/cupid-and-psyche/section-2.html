Chapter 22 (VI.22)

At the sight of Cupid, Psyche abandons her plan and tries to commit suicide · Cupid & Psyche · GCSE Latin · OCR J282/03

1tunc Psyche, et corporis et animi alioquin infirma fati tamen saevitia subministrante viribus roboratur, et prolata lucerna et adrepta novacula sexum audacia mutavit.
1Then Psyche, (though) otherwise weak in both body and mind, nevertheless, by the savagery of Fate lending aid, she is strengthened with strength, and, having brought out a lamp and seized a razor, she changed her sex by her daring.
2sed cum primum luminis oblatione tori secreta claruerunt, videt omnium ferarum mitissimam dulcissimam -que bestiam, ipsum illum Cupidinem formonsum deum formonse cubantem
2But as soon as the secrets of the couch became clear, with the offering of light, she sees the gentlest and sweetest beast, of all wild beasts, that famous Cupid himself, a handsome god, reclining handsomely.
3cuius aspectu lucernae quoque lumen hilaratum increbruit et acuminis sacrilegi novaculam paenitebat.
3At the sight of him even cheerful the light of the lamp grew stronger and the razor regretted profane of its sharpness
4at vero Psyche tanto aspectu deterrita et impos animi, marcido pallore defecta tremens -que desedit in imos poplites et quaerit abscondere, ferrum sed in suo pectore.
4But Psyche, however, terrified by such a great sight and powerless over her mind, drained, with languid paleness and trembling, sank down onto the bottom of her knees and she seeks to hide the blade — but in her own breast.
5quod profecto fecisset, nisi ferrum timore tanti flagitii manibus temerariis delapsum evolasset.
5She would surely have done which if ... had not the blade, in fear of such a great disgrace, having slipped reckless from her rash had not flown away.

sexum audacia mutavitBold Diction
she changed her sex by her daring
A startling phrase: Psyche, helped by Fate, "changes her sex" — she finds a man’s courage. audacia ("daring") is the means; the verb mutavit makes the transformation sound almost physical, fitting a book of Metamorphoses.
omnium ferarum mitissimam dulcissimamque bestiamIrony
the gentlest and sweetest beast of all wild animals
The "monster" her sisters described is redefined with two superlatives, mitissimam and dulcissimam. To call a god the gentlest and sweetest bestia of all wild animals is pointed irony — the dreaded beast is Love himself.
formonsum deum formonse cubantemPolyptoton
a handsome god reclining handsomely
The adjective formonsum and the adverb formonse frame deum in a play on the same root (polyptoton). The repeated sound lingers admiringly on Cupid’s beauty — handsome, and handsomely posed.
lucernae quoque lumen hilaratum increbruitPersonification
even the light of the lamp grew brighter, cheered
The lamp’s flame is personified: it is hilaratum ("gladdened") and flares up at the sight of the god. The inanimate light shares in the wonder of the scene.
acuminis sacrilegi novaculam paenitebatPersonification
the razor regretted its profane sharpness
The razor too is personified — it "regrets" (paenitebat) its sacrilegum acumen, its profane sharpness, as if ashamed to be raised against a god. Both lamp and blade recoil from the deed.
ferrum ... manibus temerariis delapsum evolassetPersonification
the blade slipped from her rash hands and flew away
In the unreal condition the blade saves the day: timore ... delapsum, it slips from her manus temerariae in fear and "flies away" (evolasset). The weapon acts with a will of its own.
