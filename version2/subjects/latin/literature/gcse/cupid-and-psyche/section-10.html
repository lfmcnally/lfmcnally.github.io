1sed cum primum, ut aeque placuerat, conscio lumine vultus eius aspexi, video mirum divinum -que prorsus spectaculum, ipsum illum deae Veneris filium, ipsum inquam Cupidinem, leni quiete sopitum.
1But as soon as, as we had all agreed, I looked at his face with the light as my accomplice, I see a wonderful and utterly divine sight: the very son of the goddess Venus, Cupid himself, I tell you, lulled in gentle sleep.
2ac dum tanti boni spectaculo percita et nimia voluptatis copia turbata fruendi laborarem inopia, casu scilicet pessimo lucerna fervens oleum rebullivit in eius umerum.
2And while, excited by the sight of so great a blessing and confused by too great an abundance of pleasure, I was struggling because I could not enjoy it, by the worst luck, of course, the boiling lamp bubbled hot oil out onto his shoulder.
3quo dolore statim somno recussus, ubi me ferro et igni conspexit armatam, tu quidem inquit ob istud tam dirum facinus confestim toro meo divorte, tibi -que res tuas habeto.
3Jolted at once from sleep by this pain, when he saw me armed with blade and fire, he said, 'As for you, because of this terrible crime, leave my bed immediately and take your belongings with you.'
4ego vero sororem tuam et nomen quo tu censeris aiebat iam mihi confarreatis nuptiis coniugabo.
4'But I shall now take your sister as my wife in a solemn marriage,' he kept saying, and he spoke the name by which you are known.
5et statim Zephyro praecipit ultra terminos me domus eius efflaret.
5And at once he orders the West Wind to blow me beyond the bounds of his house.

conscio luminePersonification
with the lamp my accomplice
Psyche calls the lamp her conscius — "accomplice, fellow-conspirator". The light that helped her break the taboo is cast as a partner in crime, a wry, knowing touch in her retelling (and an echo of the lamp that betrayed her).
videoHistoric present
I see
Psyche switches to the present tense as she describes the moment of revelation, as if she were seeing it again.
mirum divinum -que prorsus spectaculumPairing
a wonderful and utterly divine sight
Two adjectives build up the wonder of the sight before she names who it is.
ipsum illum ... filium, ipsum inquam CupidinemEmphatic Repetition
that very son ... Cupid himself, I mean
The doubled ipsum ... ipsum, with the breathless aside inquam ("I mean"), conveys Psyche’s astonishment as she relives the revelation: not a monster, but the son of Venus — Cupid himself.
leni quiete sopitumSoft sounds
lulled in gentle sleep
The soft l, q and s sounds suit the picture of the god peacefully asleep.
tanti boni spectaculoRepetition
the sight of so great a blessing
spectaculo picks up spectaculum from the previous sentence, keeping the reader's attention on what Psyche saw.
fruendi laborarem inopiaParadox
I was struggling with the inability to enjoy it
A neat paradox of desire: overwhelmed by a copia ("abundance") of longing, Psyche suffers an inopia ("lack/helplessness") — she cannot enjoy what she has. The antithesis of plenty and want captures the agony of love.
casu scilicet pessimoIrony
by the worst of luck, of course
Psyche's "of course" makes the accident sound unlucky but innocent. She tells the story so that her sister will not suspect a trap.
fervens oleum rebullivitOnomatopoeia
the boiling oil bubbled over
rebullivit imitates the bubbling of the hot oil, so the reader can almost hear it spill.
ferro et igni … armatamVivid detail
armed with sword and fire
Cupid wakes to see his wife armed like an attacker, which explains his anger.
divorte … habetoImperatives
depart … keep
Blunt commands make Cupid's sentence of divorce sound final.
tibi -que res tuas habetoLegal language
and keep your things for yourself
This was the formula a Roman husband used to divorce his wife, so Cupid's words sound like a real Roman divorce.
sororem tuam ... mihi confarreatis nuptiis coniugaboDramatic Irony
I will join your sister to me in official marriage
Here is Psyche’s revenge in motion: she pretends Cupid means to take the sister as his solemn wife (confarreatae nuptiae = the most binding Roman marriage). The lie, named to flatter, will lure the jealous sister to her death — a trap baited with her own greed.
Zephyro praecipit ... me ... efflaretClosure
he ordered the West Wind to blow me away
Psyche’s tale ends as her happiness began — with the Zephyrus. Once the West Wind carried her gently to Cupid’s palace; now the same wind is commanded to efflare ("blow") her out of it. The symmetry seals her expulsion.
Notes on the text 2
deae Veneris filiumVenus' son
the son of the goddess Venus
Cupid is the son of Venus, goddess of love, who is jealous of Psyche's beauty and had ordered him to make her fall in love with a worthless man.
confarreatis nuptiisConfarreatio
by a solemn marriage
Confarreatio was the oldest and most solemn form of Roman marriage, with a sacred cake of spelt. Promising it to the sister makes the bait irresistible.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Compare Psyche's account of the lamp scene with what really happened in sections 2 to 5. What has she changed, and why?
  2. How does Psyche make her story especially tempting to her sister?
  3. What is the effect of putting Cupid's words into direct speech?
Truth and lies background

Much of Psyche's story is true: she did see Cupid by the lamp, the oil did burn him, and he did fly away. But Cupid never said he would marry her sister. Psyche adds that detail, and the West Wind, on purpose: she knows that her sister's jealousy will send her straight to the rock from which Zephyrus once carried Psyche down to Cupid's palace.
