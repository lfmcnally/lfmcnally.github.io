Chapter 24 (V.24)

1at Psyche, statim resurgentis eius crure dextero manibus ambabus adrepto, sublimis evectionis adpendix miseranda et per nubilas plagas penduli comitatus extrema consequia, tandem fessa delabitur solo.
1But Psyche, having seized his right leg with both hands as he immediately rose up, a pitiful appendage to his lofty flight and the last dangling companion trailing behind him through the cloudy regions, at last, worn out, slips down to the ground.
2nec deus amator humi iacentem deserens, involavit proximam cupressum, deque eius alto cacumine sic eam graviter commotus adfatur:
2But the god, her lover, did not leave her lying on the ground: he flew into the nearest cypress tree and, from its high top, deeply troubled, spoke to her like this:
3ego quidem, simplicissima Psyche, parentis meae Veneris praeceptorum immemor, quae te miseri extremi -que hominis devinctam cupidine infimo matrimonio addici iusserat,
3I, indeed, most innocent Psyche, forgetting the instructions of my mother Venus, who had ordered that you, bound by desire for the most wretched and lowest of men, should be handed over to the lowest marriage,
4ipse potius amator advolavi tibi.
4myself flew to you instead, as your lover.

resurgentis eius crure dextero manibus ambabus adreptoAblative absolute
having seized his right leg with both hands as he rose
The long ablative absolute packs her desperate grab into one quick movement.
manibus ambabusVivid detail
with both hands
The small detail shows how desperately she clings to him.
sublimis evectionis adpendix miseranda ... penduli comitatus extrema consequiaImagery
a pitiable appendage of the lofty flight ... the furthest retinue of the hanging escort
Psyche, clinging to Cupid’s leg as he soars, is reduced to two startling metaphors: a miseranda adpendix ("a pitiable appendage") and the extrema consequia ("the very tail of the retinue") of his flight. She is no longer a person but a dangling afterthought of the god.
sublimis … soloContrast
lofty … to the ground
The sentence begins high in the air and ends with her slipping to the ground, so it traces her fall.
per nubilas plagasVivid detail
through the cloudy regions
The phrase lifts the scene into the sky, so the reader pictures her dangling high above the earth.
nec deus amator … deserensCharacterisation
the god, her lover, not deserting her
Cupid is still called her lover, and he does not abandon her completely, which shows that he still loves her.
cupressumSymbolism
a cypress
The cypress was linked with death and mourning, a gloomy place from which to speak words of parting.
ego quidemDirect speech
I indeed
Cupid speaks to Psyche for the first time in the passage, and ego, placed first, stresses his own part in what happened.
ego … advolaviDelay
I … flew to you
ego is separated from its verb by a long clause about Venus' orders, so the reader waits to hear what Cupid himself did.
simplicissima PsycheSuperlative
most simple Psyche
The superlative is both fond and reproachful: she was far too trusting of her sisters.
parentis meae Veneris praeceptorum immemor ... iusseratDramatic Revelation
heedless of my mother Venus’s commands ... she had ordered
Cupid reveals the back-story: Venus had ordered (iusserat) him to make Psyche fall for a worthless man, and he disobeyed (immemor) to take her himself. The long relative clause unpacks the plot the reader has been missing.
miseri extremique hominis ... infimo matrimonioContemptuous Diction
a wretched and lowest-of-the-low man ... a most lowly marriage
Cupid piles up Venus’s scorn for the husband she intended: miser ("wretched"), extremus ("lowest of the low"), infimum matrimonium ("the most degrading marriage"). The heaped superlatives capture the goddess’s contempt — and the depth of Cupid’s defiance.
devinctam cupidineWordplay
bound by desire
Venus wanted Psyche bound by cupido, desire, for a wretched man. Instead Cupido himself has fallen for her.
ipse potius amator advolavi tibiEmphatic Climax
I, your lover, have flown to you myself instead
The speech lands on a proud, wounded declaration: ipse ... amator ... advolavi tibi — "I myself, your lover, flew to you instead". The emphatic ipse and first-person verb set Cupid’s devotion against his mother’s command.

Questions to think about · 3
  1. How does Apuleius make Psyche's attempt to hold on to Cupid both pitiful and slightly comic?
  2. What do we learn about Cupid from the way he speaks to Psyche?
  3. Why might the cypress tree be a significant place for Cupid to perch?
Venus and Psyche background

Cupid reminds Psyche of something she did not know: his mother Venus had ordered him to make her fall in love with the most wretched of men, as punishment for drawing worshippers away from the goddess. Instead he disobeyed his mother and became her husband himself. Venus' anger, and the trials she will set Psyche, drive the rest of the story.
