Apuleius and the Golden Ass

Apuleius was born around AD 125 in Madauros, a Roman town in North Africa (now in Algeria). He studied at Carthage and Athens, travelled widely, and became famous as a speaker and philosopher. He was once put on trial for using magic to win a rich widow as his wife, and his speech in his own defence survives.

His novel, the Metamorphoses (Transformations), is better known as The Golden Ass. Its narrator, a young man called Lucius, dabbles in magic and is turned into a donkey by mistake. In his adventures as an ass he hears many stories, and the longest and most famous is the tale of Cupid and Psyche, told by an old woman to a kidnapped bride to cheer her up. It fills parts of Books 4, 5 and 6 of the novel.

Cupid and Psyche: the story so far

Psyche is a princess so beautiful that people worship her instead of Venus. The jealous goddess sends her son Cupid to make Psyche fall in love with the lowest of men; instead Cupid falls in love with her himself. An oracle orders her family to leave her on a mountain top as the bride of a monster, but the West Wind carries her to a magnificent palace, where invisible servants wait on her.

Each night an unseen husband comes to her, and he forbids her ever to try to see his face. Psyche’s two jealous sisters visit, see her riches and convince her that her husband is really a huge serpent who will devour her and her unborn child. They persuade her to hide a lamp and a razor, wait until he is asleep, and kill him. The set text begins as night falls and Psyche, alone with her plan, is torn between fear and love.

Reading Apuleius’ style

Apuleius’ Latin is rich, playful and showy, and quite unlike the plain prose of Caesar or Nepos. Things to look out for:

  • Sound: alliteration, assonance and rhyming endings that make phrases ring.
  • Pairs and lists: short words in rapid pairs or threes, often without connectives (asyndeton).
  • Word choice: rare, poetic or old-fashioned words, and diminutives for tenderness.
  • Description: lingering, almost painterly descriptions, above all of Cupid himself.
  • Paradox and contrast: monster and husband, hatred and love, darkness and light.
Questions on the set text
  1. How does Apuleius make Psyche a sympathetic character, even when she sets out to kill her husband?
  2. How important is the lamp in the story? Think about what it shows and what it does.
  3. Is Cupid fair to Psyche when he leaves her?
  4. How does Apuleius mix the serious and the playful in these chapters?
  5. Choose the description you find most vivid and explain how Apuleius achieves the effect.
