Chapter 23 continued (V.23)

The lamp spills a drop of oil over Cupid · Cupid & Psyche · GCSE Latin · OCR J282/03

1sed dum bono tanto percita saucia mente fluctuat, lucerna illa sive perfidia pessima sive invidia noxia
1But while, stirred by so great a blessing, she wavers in her wounded mind, that lamp, whether through the worst treachery or harmful envy,
2sive quod tale corpus contingere et quasi basiare et ipsa gestiebat, evomuit de summa luminis sui stillam ferventis olei super umerum dei dexterum.
2or because it too was eager to touch such a body and, as it were, to kiss it, spat out from the tip of its flame a drop of burning oil onto the god's right shoulder.
3hem audax et temeraria lucerna et amoris vile ministerium, ipsum ignis totius deum aduris,
3Ah, bold and reckless lamp, worthless servant of love, you are burning the very god of all fire,
4cum te scilicet amator aliquis, ut diutius cupitis etiam nocte potiretur, primus invenerit.
4when surely it was some lover who first invented you, so that he could enjoy what he desired for longer, even at night.
5sic inustus exsiluit deus visa -que detectae fidei colluvie prorsus ex osculis et manibus infelicissimae coniugis tacitus avolavit.
5Burnt like this, the god leapt up and, having seen the foul betrayal of his trust, at once flew away in silence from the kisses and hands of his most unhappy wife.

bono tanto percita saucia menteContrast
thrilled by such a blessing, wounded in mind
Joy and injury sit side by side, which shows love as both a delight and a wound.
fluctuatEcho
she wavers
The same verb was used of her wavering like the sea in section 1. Now she wavers again, but from love, not fear.
sive perfidia ... sive invidia ... sive quod ... gestiebatTricolon
whether through treachery ... or envy ... or because it longed ...
The narrator offers three guesses for why the lamp spilled its oil, in a rising tricolon of sive clauses: wicked treachery, harmful envy, or simple desire to touch the god. The mock-uncertainty teases the reader and personifies the lamp as a jealous accomplice.
perfidia pessimaAlliteration
most wicked treachery
The heavy p sounds, together with the superlative, make the lamp's possible treachery sound shocking.
ipsa gestiebatPersonification
it too was longing
The lamp is imagined as wanting to touch and kiss Cupid like a lover, which turns an accident into an act of jealousy or desire.
evomuitWord choice
spewed out
A coarse, violent verb for a single drop of oil, which makes the accident seem ugly and almost deliberate.
super umerum dei dexterumEmphatic position
onto the god's right shoulder
The exact spot ends the sentence, so the reader's attention lands on the moment of impact.
hem audax et temeraria lucernaApostrophe
Ah, bold and rash lamp!
The narrator breaks off to address the lamp directly (apostrophe), scolding it as audax and temeraria ("bold and rash"). The sudden second person and the exclamation hem bring mock-epic indignation to the little household object.
amoris vile ministeriumIrony
base servant of love
The lamp is named amoris vile ministerium — "love’s cheap/base servant". The object that should serve love has instead betrayed it; the contemptuous vile turns the helpful lamp into a treacherous menial.
ipsum ignis totius deum adurisParadox
you are scorching the very god of all fire
A neat paradox: a tiny lamp-flame aduris ("you scorch") the ignis totius deus, the god who rules all fire — Love, whose flames burn gods and men. The little flame burns the lord of all flame.
amator aliquis … primus inveneritIrony
some lover first invented you
The lamp is said to have been invented for lovers, yet here it destroys a love affair.
exsiluit … avolavitFast pace
he leapt up … he flew away
Two quick verbs frame the sentence, so Cupid is gone almost as soon as he wakes.
colluvieWord choice
filth
colluvies means filth or sewage, a startlingly strong word that shows how betrayed Cupid feels.
infelicissimae coniugis tacitus avolavitPathos
in silence he flew away from his most ill-fated wife
The chapter ends on quiet desolation: the god leaves tacitus ("in silence"), and Psyche is infelicissima coniunx ("his most ill-fated wife"). No reproach, no farewell — just silence, the superlative loading the loss with pathos.
Notes on the text 1
ignisThe god of fire
of fire
Love was often pictured as a fire, and Cupid is sometimes shown carrying a torch, so the god of love can be called the god of fire.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Apuleius gives three possible reasons for the lamp's "behaviour". What does this add to the story?
  2. Why do you think the narrator breaks in to speak to the lamp directly?
  3. How does Apuleius make Cupid's departure dramatic, even though he says nothing?
The narrator's voice background

The story is told by an old woman, and at moments like this she cannot help breaking in. Speaking directly to the lamp (an apostrophe) lets her show her own feelings and invites the reader to share them. Apuleius likes this kind of playful, theatrical narration, which keeps reminding us that we are listening to a story being told.
