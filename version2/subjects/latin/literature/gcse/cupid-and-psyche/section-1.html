1at Psyche relicta sola nisi quod infestis Furiis agitata sola non est aestu pelagi simile maerendo fluctuat,
1But Psyche, left alone (except that, harassed by the hostile Furies, she is not alone), tosses in her grief like the surging sea,
2et quamvis statuto consilio et obstinato animo iam tamen facinori suas manus admovens adhuc incerta consilii titubat multis -que calamitatis suae distrahitur affectibus.
2and although her plan was settled and her mind determined, yet now, as she sets her hands to the deed, she is still uncertain in her resolve and falters, and is torn apart by the many emotions of her misfortune.
3festinat differt, audet trepidat, diffidit irascitur; et quod est ultimum, in eodem corpore odit bestiam, diligit maritum.
3She hurries, then delays; she dares, then panics; she despairs, then rages; and, strangest of all, in the same body she hates the beast but loves the husband.
4vespera tamen iam noctem trahente, praecipiti festinatione nefarii sceleris instruit apparatum.
4However, as evening was now drawing on the night, in headlong haste she prepares the equipment for her wicked crime.
5nox aderat et maritus advenerat primus -que Veneris proeliis velitatus in altum soporem descenderat.
5Night had come, and her husband had arrived and, after first skirmishing in the battles of Venus, had sunk into a deep sleep.

at PsycheEmphatic position
but Psyche
The narrative cuts straight back to Psyche, named immediately after at. With her sisters gone, the reader's attention is fixed on her alone.
relictaWord choice
left behind
relicta suggests being abandoned, not just being alone: her sisters have stirred her up and then left her to face the deed by herself.
relicta sola ... sola non estParadox
left alone ... is not alone
Apuleius plants a paradox: Psyche is relicta sola ("left alone") and yet, hounded by the Furiae, sola non est ("is not alone"). Her guilt peoples the empty room.
infestis Furiis … fluctuatAlliteration
hostile Furies … she wavers
The repeated f sounds link the Furies that torment her with her wavering, so her fear and her indecision sound like one and the same.
aestu pelagi simile ... fluctuatSimile
wavers like the swell of the sea
Her wavering is compared to the aestus pelagi, the swell of the open sea, and the verb fluctuat ("surges, billows") keeps the water-image alive. Her emotions rise and fall like waves.
fluctuat … titubat … distrahiturHistoric present
she wavers … she falters … she is torn apart
The present tenses put us inside the moment, so we feel each surge of emotion as it takes hold of her.
statuto consilio … incerta consiliiRepetition
her plan settled … uncertain in her plan
consilium appears twice in one sentence, first fixed, then uncertain, which shows how quickly her resolve slips away.
obstinato animo … titubatContrast
with a resolute mind … she falters
Firm words in the first half of the sentence are undone by unsteady ones in the second, so the sentence itself wavers as she does.
distrahitur affectibusMetaphor
she is torn apart by her feelings
distrahitur means pulled in different directions, which turns her emotions into physical forces tearing at her.
festinat differt, audet trepidat, diffidit irasciturAsyndeton
she hurries, delays, dares, panics, despairs, rages
Six present-tense verbs in three clipped, conjunction-less pairs (asyndeton), each pair an opposite. The breathless rhythm enacts her flickering, contradictory feelings — we feel her mind racing.
in eodem corpore odit bestiam, diligit maritumAntithesis
in the same body she hates the beast, loves the husband
The climax of her division: in eodem corpore ("in one and the same person") she both odit and diligit. Bestiam (the monster her sisters described) is balanced against maritum (the husband she loves).
bestiamDramatic irony
the beast
Psyche has been told her husband is a monstrous serpent, but the reader may already suspect that the "beast" is something very different.
vespera ... noctem trahentePersonification
evening drawing on the night
In the ablative absolute the evening itself "drags on" the night (noctem trahente). Time is personified and made to move slowly, drawing out the suspense before the deed.
praecipiti festinationeWord choice
in headlong haste
praeceps describes falling headfirst, so her rush to get ready hints at the disaster she is rushing into.
nefarii scelerisLoaded Diction
of the wicked crime
The narrator’s own judgement breaks in: the plan is a nefarium scelus, an "unspeakable crime". The heavy moral vocabulary darkens the scene just as Psyche prepares the lamp and blade.
nox aderat … advenerat … descenderatTricolon
night was at hand … he had arrived … he had sunk
Three short clauses set the scene in quick succession, so everything is in place for the deed almost before the reader is ready.
Veneris proeliis velitatusMilitary Metaphor
having skirmished in the battles of love
Love-making is cast as warfare: Veneris proelia are the "battles of Venus", and velitatus is a technical term for the light skirmishing that opens a fight. Spent from his "campaign", Cupid sinks into deep sleep.
in altum soporemEmphasis
into a deep sleep
The depth of his sleep is exactly what the plan needs, which makes the sleeping god seem helpless and the moment tense.
Notes on the text 3
PsychePsyche
Psyche
Her name is the Greek word for "soul" (it can also mean "butterfly"). Many readers have taken the story as an allegory of the soul's search for love.
FuriisThe Furies
the Furies
Goddesses of vengeance who hounded wrongdoers. Here they stand for Psyche's guilt and fear before she has even acted.
VenerisVenus
of Venus
Venus is the goddess of love, so Veneris proeliis means love-making. She is also Cupid's mother and Psyche's bitter enemy.
Questions to think about · 2
  1. Find three phrases in which Apuleius shows that Psyche is pulled in two directions. Which do you think is most effective, and why?
  2. The passage moves quickly from evening to night to Cupid's deep sleep. Why might Apuleius speed up at this point?
The story so far background

The tale of Cupid and Psyche is told in Apuleius' novel the Metamorphoses (often called The Golden Ass) by an old woman, to cheer up a girl held captive by bandits. Psyche is the youngest of three princesses, so beautiful that people neglect the worship of Venus to admire her. In a jealous rage Venus sends her son Cupid to make Psyche fall in love with the lowest of men, but Cupid falls in love with her himself.

Psyche is carried by the west wind, Zephyrus, to a magical palace, where an unseen husband visits her only in the dark. He warns her that she must never try to see his face. Her jealous sisters persuade her that he is a monstrous serpent who will devour her and her unborn child, and tell her to hide a lamp and a sharp razor, wait until he is asleep, and cut off his head. This passage begins as they leave her alone with the plan.
