1sed hoc feci leviter, scio, et praeclarus ille sagittarius ipse me telo meo percussi te -que coniugem meam feci,
1But I did this rashly, I know, and I, that famous archer, struck myself with my own weapon and made you my wife,
2ut bestia scilicet tibi viderer et ferro caput excideres meum, quod istos amatores tuos oculos gerit.
2so that, of course, I might seem a beast to you and you might cut off with a blade my head, which carries those eyes that love you.
3haec tibi identidem semper cavenda censebam, haec benivole remonebam.
3Again and again I advised that you should always beware of these things; I kindly kept warning you about these things.
4sed illae quidem consiliatrices egregiae tuae tam perniciosi magisterii dabunt actutum mihi poenas;
4But those excellent advisers of yours will immediately pay me the penalty for such ruinous teaching;
5te vero tantum fuga mea punivero.
5But you I shall punish only by my departure.
6et cum termino sermonis pinnis in altum se proripuit.
6And with the end of his speech, he hurled himself into the heights on his wings.

praeclarus ille sagittarius ipseCharacterisation
that famous archer himself
Cupid describes himself in grand, almost boastful terms. This makes his confession that he wounded himself all the more striking.
me telo meo percussiIrony
I struck myself with my own weapon
The god of love confesses he is a victim of love: me telo meo percussi — "I struck myself with my own dart". The famous archer (praeclarus sagittarius) has, ironically, shot himself; the emphatic me ... meo drives the point home.
percussi te -que coniugem meam feciPairing
I struck myself and made you my wife
Two verbs are joined by -que: the wound leads straight to the marriage. Love, for Cupid as for anyone, was something that happened to him.
bestia ... viderer et ferro caput excideres meumTragic Irony
I might seem a beast, and you might cut off my head with a knife
Cupid spells out what the sisters’ plot would have meant: he would "seem a bestia" and Psyche would cut off the very head whose eyes she loves. The reader recalls Chapter 22, where she really did raise the blade — disaster only just averted.
scilicetSarcasm
doubtless
"Doubtless" is bitterly sarcastic: Cupid suggests that the reward for his love was to be thought a beast and to have his head cut off.
caput excideres meumHyperbaton
you might cut off my head
caput and meum are separated, so "my" comes last. The reader is left with the shock that the head in question is the god's own.
istos amatores tuos oculosParadox
those eyes, your lovers
Cupid calls his eyes Psyche's lovers. It is a tender compliment and a reproach at once: she wanted to destroy the very eyes that adored her.
haec ... cavenda censebam, haec ... remonebamAnaphora
I urged you to guard against these things ... I warned you of these things
The repeated, fronted haec ("these very things") in two balanced clauses hammers home how often Cupid had warned her. The anaphora makes his exasperation audible — he told her, again and again.
cavendaGerundive
things to be guarded against
The gerundive expresses obligation: these were dangers Psyche had to avoid. It shows that the rules were clear and she broke them.
benivoleWord choice
kindly
Cupid insists that his warnings were meant kindly, which makes Psyche's disobedience seem worse and his hurt clearer.
illae … te veroContrast
they … but you
The sisters and Psyche are set against each other: they will be punished harshly, she only by his absence.
illae ... consiliatrices egregiae tuaeSarcasm
those marvellous counsellors of yours
egregiae consiliatrices ("marvellous advisers") is heavy sarcasm for the wicked sisters, whose perniciosum magisterium ("ruinous instruction") destroyed Psyche’s happiness. The mock-praise drips with contempt.
dabunt actutum mihi poenasForeshadowing
will soon pay me the penalty
The threat against the sisters is fulfilled in sections 11 and 12. The old-fashioned word actutum ("at once") gives it a solemn, final ring.
te vero tantum fuga mea puniveroPathos
you, however, I shall punish only with my flight
Cupid’s punishment of Psyche is gentle to the point of heartbreak: tantum fuga mea — "only with my flight". The harshest thing the god of love can do to her is to leave; the loss itself is the penalty.
puniveroFuture perfect
I shall have punished
The future perfect makes the punishment sound complete and final: once he has flown away, that is all the punishment she needs.
pinnis in altum se proripuitVivid Action
on his wings he hurled himself into the heights
The chapter ends on a single violent movement: se proripuit ("he tore himself away/upward") on his wings into the altum. The forceful verb leaves Psyche — and the reader — abruptly abandoned, gazing after him.
Notes on the text 1
telo meoCupid's arrows
with my own weapon
Cupid's arrows make whoever they strike fall in love. In chapter 23 Psyche, handling his arrows, pricks herself and falls in love with him; here Cupid admits the same happened to him.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Cupid says he "made you my wife" after wounding himself with his own arrow. How does this change the way we see their marriage?
  2. Cupid punishes Psyche only by leaving her. Is this a kind punishment or a cruel one? Explain your view.
  3. Find two places where Cupid is sarcastic. Whom is his anger really aimed at?
Cupid's warning background

From the start of the marriage Cupid had visited Psyche only at night and had warned her, more than once, never to try to see his face, or she would lose him. Her jealous sisters persuaded her that her unseen husband was a monstrous serpent who would devour her, and talked her into hiding a lamp and a razor to kill him in his sleep. When the lamp revealed Cupid himself, a drop of hot oil woke him. In this speech, as he flies off, he reminds her of those warnings and promises that the sisters will be punished.
