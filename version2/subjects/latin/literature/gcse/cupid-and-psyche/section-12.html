Chapter 27 continued (V.27)

The second sister meets a similar fate · Cupid & Psyche · GCSE Latin · OCR J282/03

1nam per saxa cautium membris iactatis atque dissipatis et proinde ut merebatur laceratis visceribus suis, alitibus bestiis -que obvium ferens pabulum interiit.
1For as her limbs were flung and scattered over the rocks of the crags, and her innards, just as she deserved, were torn apart, she perished, providing ready food for birds and beasts.
2nec vindictae sequentis poena tardavit.
2Nor was the punishment of the next act of revenge slow in coming.
3nam Psyche rursus errabundo gradu pervenit ad civitatem aliam, in qua pari modo soror morabatur alia.
3For Psyche again with wandering step comes to another town, in which the other sister was living in a similar way.
4nec setius et ipsa fallacia germanitatis inducta et in sororis sceleratas nuptias aemula, festinavit ad scopulum in -que simile mortis exitium cecidit.
4In just the same way, she too, taken in by her sister's deceit and eager to rival her sister in a wicked marriage, hurried to the crag and fell to a similar deadly end.

membris iactatis atque dissipatis ... laceratis visceribusGory Imagery
her limbs tossed about and scattered ... her innards torn apart
The sister’s death is told in unsparing, butcherly detail: membra iactata and dissipata ("limbs flung and scattered"), viscera lacerata ("innards torn"). The piled-up participles of mangling deliver the chapter title’s promised "sticky end".
iactatis atque dissipatis … laceratisTricolon
flung, scattered … torn apart
Three participles build up the violence of her death step by step.
proinde ut merebaturPoetic Justice
accordingly, as she deserved
The narrator’s aside ut merebatur ("as she deserved") makes the gore moral. This is not random cruelty but earned punishment — the wicked sister gets exactly what her treachery has earned, and the reader is told so plainly.
alitibus bestiisque obvium ferens pabulumDegrading Image
providing ready food for birds and beasts
Her body’s final use is as carrion: pabulum ("fodder") obvium ("ready to hand") for the alites and bestiae. The woman who schemed for a god’s bed ends as a meal for scavengers — a bathetic, contemptuous close.
pabulumWord choice
fodder
The word normally used of animal feed reduces the proud sister to food for birds and beasts.
nec vindictae sequentis poena tardavitSwift Retribution
nor was the punishment of the revenge that followed slow
A crisp transitional sentence promises the same again: the poena of the second sister’s vindicta "was not slow". The litotes (nec ... tardavit) hurries us straight on to her identical doom.
errabundo graduVivid detail
with wandering step
Psyche is still wandering without a plan, as in section 9, so the second revenge seems guided by fate.
civitatem aliam, in qua pari modo soror morabatur aliaFraming
another city, in which the other sister was living in the same way
aliam and alia frame the clause, and pari modo ("in the same way") tells the reader that the pattern is about to repeat.
inque simile mortis exitium ceciditRing Composition
and she fell to a like destruction of death
The tale of the sisters closes by exact repetition: lured by the same lie, vying for the same marriage, the second sister falls to a simile mortis exitium — "a death just like" her sister’s. The mirrored ending delivers symmetrical, satisfying justice.
fallacie germanitatisIrony
by the deceit of sisterhood
Sisterhood should mean trust, but here it is the source of the trick: the sisters are caught by the same weapon they used on Psyche.
sceleratas nuptiasLoaded word
a wicked marriage
She hurries towards a marriage that would rest on crime and betrayal, so her ruin seems deserved.
aemulaWord choice
rivalling
The second sister wants to outdo the first, which shows that envy, not love, drives both of them.
festinavit ad scopulumFast pace
she hurried to the rock
A single verb of haste sends her to her death, as quickly as her sister.

What happens next background

Psyche's troubles are far from over. She wanders in search of Cupid, while Venus, furious that her son has married a mortal she hates, hunts her down. Venus sets her a series of impossible tasks: sorting a vast heap of mixed grain in one night (the ants help her), fetching golden wool from savage sheep, bringing water from the source of the river Styx, and fetching a box of beauty from Proserpina in the underworld. On the way back Psyche opens the box out of curiosity and falls into a deathly sleep. Cupid, now healed, rescues her, and Jupiter makes her immortal so that the two can marry. Their daughter is called Voluptas, Pleasure.
