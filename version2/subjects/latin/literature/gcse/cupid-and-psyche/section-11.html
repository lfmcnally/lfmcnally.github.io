1necdum sermonem Psyche finierat,
1Psyche had not yet finished her speech,
2et illa vesanae libidinis et invidiae noxiae stimulis agitata, e re concinnato mendacio fallens maritum, quasi de morte parentum aliquid comperisset,
2and her sister, driven on by the goads of mad lust and harmful envy, deceiving her husband with a lie made up to suit the situation, as if she had learned something about the death of her parents,
3statim navem ascendit et ad illum scopulum protinus pergit.
3at once boards a ship and makes straight to that rock.
4et quamvis alio flante vento, caeca spe tamen inhians, dicens
4And although a different wind was blowing, nevertheless longing with blind hope, and saying:
5accipe me, Cupido, dignam te coniugem, et tu, Zephyre, suscipe dominam,
5'Take me, Cupid, a wife worthy of you; and you, Zephyr, carry your mistress up!'
6saltu se maximo praecipitem dedit.
6With a mighty leap she flung herself headlong.
7nec tamen ad illum locum vel saltem mortua pervenire potuit.
7However, she could not reach that place, not even in death.

necdum sermonem Psyche finieratFast pace
Psyche had not yet finished speaking
The sister does not even wait for the end of the story, which shows how eager and greedy she is.
vesanae libidinis et invidiae noxiae stimulis agitataMoral Diction
driven by the goads of mad lust and harmful jealousy
The sister is condemned in her every epithet: vesana libido ("frenzied lust") and invidia noxia ("ruinous envy") are the stimuli ("goads") that drive her. The animal image of being goaded marks her as a creature of base passions.
stimulis agitataMetaphor
driven by goads
A goad was a pointed stick used to drive cattle. Lust and envy drive the sister on like an animal, with no thought at all.
concinnato mendacio fallens maritumCharacterisation
deceiving her husband with a lie cooked up
She lies to her own husband without hesitation, which shows that deceit comes naturally to her.
quasi de morte parentumIrony
as if about the death of her parents
She uses her parents' death as an excuse, which makes her seem heartless as well as dishonest.
statim navem ascendit et ad illum scopulum protinus pergitFast pace
at once she boards a ship and goes straight to that rock
statim and protinus, with two verbs in the present tense, rush her towards her death.
alio flante ventoForeshadowing
though another wind was blowing
The wrong wind is blowing, so the reader knows Zephyrus will not carry her down as he carried Psyche.
caeca spe tamen inhiansIrony
gaping, nevertheless, in blind hope
Caeca spes ("blind hope") is the perfect phrase for her delusion: she gazes hungrily (inhians) for a Zephyr that will not come. The adverse wind blows and yet she leaps — hope has blinded her to the obvious.
accipe me, Cupido, dignam te coniugem ... suscipe dominamHubris
Take me, Cupid, a wife worthy of you ... receive your mistress
Her last words reek of arrogance: she names herself a coniunx digna ("a worthy wife") and a domina ("mistress"). The pompous command to Cupid and Zephyr — neither of whom is listening — makes her self-importance the immediate cause of her ruin.
accipe me, Cupido … et tu, ZephyreApostrophe
take me, Cupid … and you, Zephyrus
She addresses the gods directly with commands, as if they must obey her, which shows her arrogance.
saltu se maximo praecipitem deditClimax
with a mighty leap she flung herself headlong
The fatal moment is told in one swift clause: saltu maximo ("with a huge leap") she gives herself praecipitem ("headlong") to the air. The phrase deliberately echoes Psyche’s own river-plunge — but for the sister there is no gentle rescue.
praecipitem deditEcho
she threw herself headlong
The same words described Psyche's leap into the river in section 8. Psyche was saved; the sister is not.
nec tamen ad illum locum vel saltem mortua pervenire potuitGrim Wit
yet she could not reach that place — not even dead
The chapter’s last line is darkly funny: dashed on the rocks below, she could not even when dead (vel saltem mortua) reach the spot she aimed for. The grim understatement is the "sticky end" of the chapter title — her body never even arrives.
Notes on the text 1
ZephyreZephyrus
Zephyrus
The West Wind, a gentle spring wind, who served Cupid by carrying Psyche, and later her sisters on their visits, between the rock and the palace.

The rock background

When Psyche was first given to her mysterious husband, an oracle of Apollo ordered her parents to leave her on a high rock, dressed as for a funeral, to be married to a monster. From there the gentle West Wind, Zephyrus, carried her down to Cupid's palace. The sister expects the same journey; but she goes without Cupid's protection, and the wrong wind is blowing.
