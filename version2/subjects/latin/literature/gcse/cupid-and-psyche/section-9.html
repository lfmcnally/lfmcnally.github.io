Chapter 26 (V.26)

Psyche is reunited with one of her sisters · Cupid & Psyche · GCSE Latin · OCR J282/03

1sic locuto deo pastore nullo -que sermone reddito sed adorato tantum numine salutari, Psyche pergit ire.
1When the shepherd god had spoken like this, Psyche, giving no reply but only worshipping the saving god, goes on her way.
2sed cum aliquam multum viae laboranti vestigio pererrasset, inscia quodam tramite die iam labente
2But when she had wandered a good distance with weary steps, without realising it, along a certain path, as the day was now fading,
3accedit quandam civitatem, in qua regnum maritus unius sororis eius optinebat.
3she comes to a certain town in which the husband of one of her sisters ruled as king.
4qua re cognita Psyche nuntiari praesentiam suam sorori desiderat.
4When she learned this, Psyche wants her arrival to be announced to her sister.
5mox inducta, mutuis amplexibus alternae salutationis expletis, percontanti causas adventus sui sic incipit:
5Soon she was led in and, when they had finished their embraces and exchange of greetings, as her sister asks the reasons for her arrival, she begins like this:
6meministi consilium vestrum scilicet quo mihi suasistis ut bestiam, quae mariti mentito nomine mecum quiescebat, priusquam ingluvie voraci me misellam hauriret, ancipiti novacula peremerem.
6You remember, of course, your advice, by which you both persuaded me to kill with a double-edged razor the beast which used to sleep with me under the false name of husband, before it could swallow poor little me with its greedy gullet.

sic locuto deo pastore nullo -que sermone reddito sed adorato tantum numine salutariFast pace
when the shepherd god had spoken thus, with no reply given, but only reverence paid to the saving god
Three ablative absolutes cover the end of the meeting with Pan in a single breath, so the story moves quickly on.
numine salutariWord choice
the saving god
Psyche treats Pan with reverence as a god who brings safety, a hint that the gods are still watching over her.
laboranti vestigioTransferred epithet
with weary steps
It is Psyche who is tired, but the word is given to her footsteps, which makes the long, painful journey easy to picture.
inscia quodam tramiteEmphasis
unknowing, by some path
She does not know where she is going, so her arrival at her sister's city seems the work of fate rather than a plan.
iam die labenteAtmosphere
as the day was now fading
A quiet ablative absolute sets the scene: iam die labente, "with the day now slipping away". The fading light mirrors Psyche’s exhaustion and the gathering gloom of her fortunes as she stumbles, lost, towards the sisters’ city.
die iam labenteAtmosphere
as the day was now slipping away
Evening falls as she arrives, a quiet but ominous moment before the trap is set.
acceditHistoric present
she comes to
The switch to the present tense makes her arrival vivid and immediate.
mutuis amplexibus alternae salutationisIrony
with mutual embraces of shared greeting
The warm hugs and greetings are full of irony: each sister is hiding her real feelings from the other.
scilicetWord choice
of course
Psyche's "of course" sounds innocent but is pointed: she reminds her sister, with mock simplicity, of the advice that ruined her.
mariti mentito nomineLoaded Diction
under the false name of husband
Psyche repeats the sisters’ lie back to them: the "beast" had slept with her mariti mentito nomine — "under the lying name of husband". The phrase drips with the deception she has swallowed, and dramatic irony: the "beast" was her true, divine husband.
ingluvie voraci me misellam hauriretMonstrous Imagery
(before) he could gulp down poor little me with his ravenous throat
The sisters’ invented monster looms here: a vorax ingluvies ("ravenous gullet") about to haurire ("gulp down") Psyche whole. The cannibal imagery makes vivid the lie she believed — and the horror she was driven to.
me misellamPathetic Diminutive
poor little me
The diminutive misellam ("poor little [me]") is a touch of self-pity that also flatters the listening sister. Apuleius loves such affectionate diminutives; here it underscores Psyche’s helplessness — and, to the reader, her dangerous naivety.
ancipiti novaculaVivid detail
with a two-edged razor
The weapon is named precisely, reminding the reader of the near-murder in the lamp scene.
Notes on the text 2
deo pastorePan
the shepherd god
Pan, the half-goat god of shepherds and wild places, who comforted Psyche on the river bank in the part of chapter 25 left out of the set text.
maritus unius sororisThe sisters
the husband of one of her sisters
Psyche's two elder sisters were married to foreign kings and lived as queens, but they envied her marriage to a god.
Questions to think about · 2
  1. How has Psyche changed between section 8 and this section?
  2. How does Apuleius make Psyche's speech to her sister sound innocent while it is really a trap?
Psyche's revenge background

Psyche's sisters were married to kings in other cities, and their jealousy of her rich and mysterious marriage drove them to persuade her that her husband was a monster. Now Psyche turns their own weapon, deceit, against them. The story she tells in this section and the next is partly invented, and designed to lure each sister to her death.
