Chapter 23 (V.23)

Psyche falls in love with Cupid · Cupid & Psyche · GCSE Latin · OCR J282/03

1quae dum insatiabili animo Psyche, satis et curiosa, rimatur atque pertrectat et mariti sui miratur arma,
1While Psyche, with an insatiable spirit and more than a little curious, examines and handles her husband's weapons and marvels at them,
2depromit unam de pharetra sagittam et punctu pollicis extremam aciem periclitabunda trementis etiam nunc articuli nisu fortiore pupugit altius,
2she draws out one arrow from the quiver, and, testing the very tip, with a prick of her thumb, because the effort of her hand (which was) trembling even now was too strong, she pricked (herself) too deeply,
3ut per summam cutem roraverint parvulae sanguinis rosei guttae.
3so that tiny drops of rosy blood trickled over the surface of her skin.
4sic ignara Psyche sponte in Amoris incidit amorem.
4Thus Psyche, unknowing of her own accord, fell into love of Love.
5tunc magis magis -que cupidine flagrans Cupidinis, prona in eum efflictim inhians, patulis ac petulantibus saviis festinanter ingestis, de somni mensura metuebat.
5Then, more and more with desire burning for Cupid, leaning forward, desperately gazing over him, hurriedly showered (on him), open-mouthed and wanton kisses she grew afraid about the measure of his sleep.

insatiabili animo … satis et curiosaWordplay
with an insatiable mind … more than curious enough
satis echoes insatiabili, and the jingle points to her curiosity, which is the flaw that will bring her trouble.
rimatur atque pertrectat et … miraturTricolon
she explores and handles and marvels at
Three verbs show her curiosity growing from looking, to touching, to wonder.
mariti sui … armaCharacterisation
her husband's weapons
She sees the bow and arrows simply as her husband's belongings, unaware of how dangerous they are.
punctu pollicis … pupugitAlliteration
with a prick of her thumb … she pricked
The repeated p sounds seem to mimic the little pricks of the point.
periclitabundaUnusual word
testing
A long, rare adjective of a kind Apuleius loves, which slows the moment down as she tries the point.
pupugit altiusDramatic Irony
she pricked (herself) too deeply
Handling the god’s own weapons, Psyche pricks herself on one of his arrows — and pupugit altius ("pricked too deep"). The arrows of Love do their work: she is wounded into loving the very god lying before her.
parvulae sanguinis rosei guttaeVivid detail
tiny drops of rosy blood
Rosy blood and the diminutive parvulae make the wound small and almost pretty, a wound of love, not of violence.
ignara ... sponteParadox
unknowing ... of her own accord
Two ideas in tension: Psyche acts sponte ("of her own volition") yet is ignara ("unaware") of what she does. She chooses her love freely and blindly at once — the human condition of falling in love.
in Amoris incidit amoremWordplay
she fell into the love of Love
A neat figura etymologica: Amoris (Love the god, Cupid) beside amorem (the emotion). She falls into love (amorem) with Love (Amor) himself — the same word naming both the feeling and its god.
magis magis -queRepetition
more and more
The doubled word builds the intensity of her desire step by step.
cupidine flagrans CupidinisWordplay
burning with desire for Cupid
Another etymological play: cupido means "desire", and Cupido is the god’s very name. She burns with cupido for Cupido — desire for Desire personified.
flagransFire metaphor
burning
Love is a fire that blazes up in her, an image that becomes literal when the lamp's oil burns Cupid in the next section.
patulis ac petulantibus saviisAlliteration
with open-mouthed and wanton kisses
The hissing, plosive alliteration of patulis ... petulantibus ... saviis matches the breathless, greedy kisses Psyche showers on the sleeping god — sensuous sound for a sensuous moment.
de somni mensura metuebatForeshadowing
she was afraid about how long his sleep would last
She fears that he will wake, and the reader senses that he is about to.
Notes on the text 1
curiosaCuriosity
curious
Curiosity is the key flaw in both Psyche and the novel's hero Lucius, whose curiosity about magic turns him into a donkey.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. How does Apuleius show that Psyche's curiosity gets the better of her?
  2. Explain the wordplay in Amoris incidit amorem. Why is it such a neat way to describe what happens?
  3. How does Apuleius make the prick of the arrow seem small, but its effect huge?
Love's arrows background

In Greek and Roman myth Cupid's arrows made whoever they struck fall helplessly in love: Apollo's love for Daphne, for example, began with one of them. When Psyche pricks her own thumb on one of her husband's arrows, she falls in love in the most literal way possible, with Love himself.

Curiosity (curiositas) is one of the great themes of the whole novel. Its hero, Lucius, is turned into a donkey because he is too curious about magic, and Psyche's curiosity is about to cost her dearly too.
