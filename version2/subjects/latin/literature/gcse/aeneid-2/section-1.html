1They all fell silent, and fixed their eyes attentively on him.
2īndĕ tŏrō pătĕr Aēnēās sīc ōrsŭs ăb āltō:
3īnfāndūm, rĕgĭna͜, iūbēs rĕnŏvārĕ dŏlōrĕm,
4Troīānās ŭt ŏpēs ēt lāmēntābĭlĕ rēgnŭm
5ēruērīnt Dānaī; quaēque͜ īpsĕ mĭsērrĭmă vīdĭ,
6ēt quōrūm pārs māgnă fuĭ. quīs tăliă fāndō
7Mȳrmĭdŏnūm Dŏlŏpūmve͜ aūt dūrī mīlĕs Ŭlīxĭ
8tēmpĕrĕt ā lācrīmĭs? ĕt iām nōx ūmĭdă caēlō
9praēcĭpĭtāt, suādēntquĕ cădēntĭă sīdĕră sōmnōs.
2Then father Aeneas, having risen from his tall couch [said]
3O queen, you order [me] to repeat unspeakable pain,
4how the Danaans destroyed the Trojan wealth and lamentable kingdom;
5and those most wretched things I have seen myself,
6and of which I have been a large part. Who, of the Myrmidons
7or of the Dolopes, or even a soldier of harsh Ulysses speaking such things
8could abstain from tears? and already the damp night is falling
9from the sky and the setting stars are urging us to sleep.
10sēd sī tāntŭs ămōr cāsūs cōgnōscĕrĕ nōstrōs
11ēt brĕvĭtēr Troīaē sūprēmum͜ aūdīrĕ lăbōrĕm,
12quāmquam͜ ănĭmūs mĕmĭnīsse͜ hōrrēt, lūctūquĕ rĕfūgĭt,
13īncĭpĭām. frāctī bēllō fātīsquĕ rĕpūlsī
10But if your desire is so great to learn of our downfall
11and to hear briefly the last agony of Troy,
12Although my mind shudders to have remembered and fled with grief,
13I shall begin. Broken by war and rejected by fate,

1
conticuereEmphatic position
they fell silent
conticuere is the first word of Book 2, so the silence of Dido's whole court is the first thing we hear. The reader feels the hush as everyone waits for Aeneas to speak.
1
omnesEmphatic position
everyone
omnes comes straight after the verb and ends its phrase, stressing that every single person is spellbound. It shows how eager the whole hall is to hear the story.
2
toro altoHyperbaton
from his high couch
toro and alto are split, with Aeneas' name and the start of his speech in between. Ending the line on "high" gives him a dignified place as he begins.
2
paterEpithet
father
pater presents Aeneas as a father to his people, which shows responsibility as well as respect. He speaks as the leader who must look after the Trojans.
3
infandum doloremHyperbaton
unspeakable grief
infandum opens the line and dolorem closes it, so the grief stretches across the whole line. The separation makes the pain feel vast and heavy.
5
eruerint DanaiEnjambment
the Greeks destroyed
The verb and its subject spill over into line 5, so the destruction of Troy lands with force at the start of the line. The reader feels the shock of the city's fall.
5
miserrimaSuperlative
most pitiful
miserrima stresses how wretched the things Aeneas saw were. It makes the reader expect a deeply tragic story.
6
quaeque, quorumPolyptoton
which … of which
The relative pronoun appears twice in different cases (quae, quorum). Aeneas was both a witness and a sufferer, which makes his account personal.
6
fuiPerfect tense
I was
fui is short and comes at the end of its sentence. It reminds the reader that Aeneas played a large part in these events, and that Troy now belongs to the past.
7
Myrmidonum, Dolopum, UlixiTricolon
Myrmidons, Dolopes, Ulysses
Three sets of enemies are listed, building up to Ulysses, the most hated. The list stresses that even Troy's fiercest enemies would weep.
7
ve, autVariatio
or … or
Virgil varies his words for "or", using -ve and then aut, which keeps the list of enemies flowing. It makes the list sound natural rather than mechanical.
7
duriEpithet
harsh
durus describes Ulysses as hard and pitiless. Even the cruellest of the Greeks, Aeneas suggests, would weep at this story.
8
a lacrimisDelay
from tears
a lacrimis is held back to the start of line 8, after a long question. The delay makes "tears" land with emotional weight.
9
praecipitatEnjambment
is rushing down
The verb spills over into line 9, so night seems to fall suddenly. It reminds the reader that time is passing and that the story is a long one.
9
suadentque cadentiaAssonance
and the setting (stars) urge
The repeated -ent- sounds in suadentque cadentia create a soft, lulling rhythm. It suits the idea of the stars persuading people to sleep.
9
sidera somnosAlliteration
stars … sleep
The repeated s sounds in sidera somnos are soft and drowsy. The sound helps the reader feel the lateness of the hour.
10
casus nostrosHyperbaton
our misfortunes
casus and nostros are separated by cognoscere, so "our" closes the line. It stresses that these disasters happened to Aeneas' own people.
11
supremum laboremHyperbaton
the last agony
supremum and laborem are separated by audire, so "Troy's final suffering" frames the act of hearing. It stresses that this is the end of Troy.
12
quamquam animus meminisseAlliteration
although my mind (shudders) to remember
The repeated m sounds slow the line down. They suggest Aeneas' reluctance and pain as he forces himself to remember.
13
incipiamEmphatic position
I shall begin
incipiam is held over to the start of a new line and followed by a full stop. The short, decisive word marks the moment Aeneas makes himself start.
13
fracti, bello, fatis, repulsiChiasmus
broken by war and thwarted by the fates
The words form an ABBA pattern: participle, noun, noun, participle. The balanced phrase sums up how the Greeks were held back by both war and fate.

Questions to think about · 3
  1. Why do you think Book 2 begins with everyone falling silent?
  2. Aeneas calls his story infandum ("unspeakable") and says his mind shudders to remember it. What does this make the reader expect?
  3. How does Aeneas suggest that even Troy's enemies would be moved by his story?
Where this comes in the Aeneid background

At the end of Book 1, Aeneas and his Trojans, shipwrecked on the coast of Africa, have been welcomed by Dido, queen of the new city of Carthage. At a banquet in his honour she asks him to tell the story of the fall of Troy and of his wanderings since. Books 2 and 3 are that story, told by Aeneas himself.

Book 2 is the night Troy fell: the wooden horse, the warning of Laocoön, the lies of Sinon, and the destruction of the city. Because Aeneas is the narrator, we see everything through the eyes of a Trojan who lived through it, and his grief colours every line.
