234We break through the walls and lay open the defences of our city.
235āccīngūnt ōmnēs ŏpĕrī, pĕdĭbūsquĕ rŏtārum
236sūbĭcĭūnt lāpsūs, ēt stūppĕă vīncŭlă cōllo
237īntēndūnt: scāndīt fātālīs māchĭnă mūrōs,
238fēta͜ ārmīs. pŭĕrī cīrcum͜ īnnūptaēquĕ pŭēllaē
239sācră cănūnt, fūnēmquĕ mănū cōntīngĕrĕ gaūdēnt.
235All set themselves to the work; under its feet they put
236wheels to roll on, and round its neck they stretch ropes of flax.
237The engine of fate mounts our walls
238teeming with armed men. Boys and unmarried girls sing their hymns
239around it, and they rejoice to touch the rope with their hands.
240īllă sŭbīt, mĕdĭaēquĕ mĭnāns īnlābĭtŭr ūrbi.
240Up it comes, and glides menacingly into the heart of the city.
241ō pătrĭa,͜ ō dīvōm dŏmŭs Īlĭum,͜ ĕt īnclŭtă bēllo
242moēnĭă Dārdănĭdūm, quătĕr īpso͜ īn līmĭnĕ pōrtaē
243sūbstĭtĭt, ātque͜ ŭtĕrō sŏnĭtūm quătĕr ārmă dĕdēre:
244īnstāmūs tămĕn īnmĕmŏrēs caēcīquĕ fŭrōre,
245ēt mōnstrum͜ īnfēlīx sācrātā sīstĭmŭs ārce.
241O my homeland, O Ilium, home of the gods, and O walls of the Dardanians,
242famous in war! Four times, on the very threshold of the gate,
243it stopped, and four times the weapons in its belly gave out a sound;
244Yet we press on, heedless and blind with frenzy,
245and we stand the accursed monster on our consecrated citadel.

Notes on the text 5
234
dividimusNote
The horse was too large to pass through the gates of Troy
239
sacra canuntHymns
they sing hymns
Choirs of boys and unmarried girls sang hymns at Roman religious festivals. The Trojans treat the arrival of the horse as a holy procession.
241
IliumIlium
Ilium
Another name for Troy, from Ilus, one of its early kings. Aeneas calls it the home of the gods because gods were said to have helped build its walls: Apollo and Neptune built them for King Laomedon.
242
DardanidumThe Trojans
of the Trojans
The Dardanidae are the descendants of Dardanus, son of Jupiter and founder of the Trojan royal family, from whom Aeneas himself was descended.
243
In limine portae substititNote
To stumble on the threshold was always held as a bad omen
