magna and caterva are split across the end of the line, so the words spread out like the crowd itself, which shows how many people follow Laocoön.

blazing
ardens, "blazing", shows Laocoön burning with anger and urgency, so the reader sees how desperate he is to stop the Trojans.

miseri civesHyperbaton

The line hurries through dona carere dolis to Danaum, hardly pausing at the caesura, which suits Laocoön's angry, rapid speech.

The repeated d sound in dona dolis Danaum links gifts, tricks and Greeks together, so the sound itself makes Laocoön's point that Greek gifts are tricks.

inclusi and Achivi are split by the rest of the line, and the Greeks themselves come last, hidden at the end of the line just as they are hidden inside the horse.

47
inspectura, venturaFuture participles of purpose
to look into ... to come down
inspectura and ventura are future participles expressing purpose, a Greek-style construction, which makes the threat sound as if it is already planned.
47
venturaEmphatic position
and to come down
ventura follows in the same way as inspectura, which builds up Laocoön's list of the dangers inside the horse.
48
errorEmphatic position
trick
error ends its clause just before the strong pause, so the vague idea of "some trick" is left hanging ominously.
48
ne crediteImperative
do not trust
Laocoön gives a direct order, ne credite, which shows how urgent and certain he is.
48
ne creditePoetic construction
do not trust

41
LaocoonLaocoön
A Trojan priest of Neptune (line 201). Ancient writers disagree about his family; in one version he is the brother of Anchises, Aeneas' father.
41
arceThe citadel
the citadel
The arx was the fortified high point of Troy, where Priam's palace and the temples stood. Laocoön runs all the way down from it to the horse on the plain below.
42

The epic equivalent of insanis in Comedy. Virgil has dressed up a phrase that any ordinary Roman might use at seeing something preposterous.

Virgil uses several names for the Greeks: Danai (after Danaus, an early king of Argos), Achivi (Achaeans) and Argivi. The Trojans are often Teucri, after Teucer, an early king of Troy.

One of the most quoted lines in Latin, still used today as a warning to be wary of generosity from an enemy.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Why do you think Virgil makes Laocoön run down from the citadel, with a crowd behind him?
  2. Laocoön offers several explanations for the horse. Which is closest to the truth, and how does his language make him persuasive?
  3. Why do you think the Trojans ignore his warning?
Laocoön background

Laocoön was a Trojan priest, usually said to be a priest of Apollo, though in this story he is serving Neptune. In Virgil's account he is the only Trojan who sees the danger clearly. Ancient writers told his story in different ways: in some he is punished for an earlier offence against Apollo, but Virgil makes his death look like a punishment for attacking the horse, and that is what persuades the Trojans to take it in.

A famous marble statue of Laocoön and his sons in the coils of the serpents, found in Rome in 1506, is now in the Vatican Museums.
