108
Often the Greeks had wanted to flee Troy, having left behind
109
what they had endeavoured to do, leaving behind the long and tiring war;
110
Oh if only they had done so! Often the winter closed them in there
111
with rough seas or South-wind frightened them as they were sailing
112
Now, especially, when this horse of entwined maple timbers
113
stood here, the clouds thundered around the whole sky.
114
Having been kept in suspense, we sent Eurypylus to inquire after the Phoebon
115
oracle, and these are the sad words he brought back from there:
116
‘With blood and a sacrificial virgin you assuaged the winds,
117
when you first came to the shores of Ilium, Greeks;
118
with blood you must seek return and with a Greek spirit you must
119
make a sacrifice’. When his voice came to the ears of the people,
120
their hearts froze and an icy tremble ran through the depths of their
121
bones, for whom should they prepare the prophecy, whom might Apollo demand.
122
Here with great uproar the Ithacan dragged the prophet
123
Calchas into the centre of the crowd; he demanded to show towards whom the
124
divine nod was directed. And many already predicted the cruel wickedness
125
of the schemer towards me, they saw what was coming and were silent.
126
For ten days he remained silent, and covered, he refused
127
to reveal his utterance that would fix death for someone.
128
At last with much ado, driven by the great clamour of the Ithacan,
129
forced out his voice as planned and fixed me to the altar.
130
they were all aware of it, and since every man was fearing for himself
131
with wretched destruction fixed on a single man, they could bear it.
132
That abominable day was here already; the sacred rites were prepared for me
133
and the salted fruits and the fillets around my brow
134
I snatched myself away, I confess, from death and burst my chains,
135
I lay hidden in the reeds of a muddy lake through the night
136
while they set sail, if perhaps they had gone.
137
Now I have no hope of ever seeing my previous homeland
138
nor my sweet children and my father I longed for,
139
Perhaps they will demand punishment against then for my
140
escape, and they will appease with the wretched deaths for my fault.
141
Wherefore, through the gods and the powers that know the truth,
142
and through pledge if there be such as still remains anywhere among men
143
inviolable, I beg, have pity on such great labours.
