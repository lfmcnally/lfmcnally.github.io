50Having spoken thus, he hurled a huge spear with his powerful strength
51into the side and into the belly of the beast curved with timbers:
52it stood there quivering, and as the belly was struck and shaken,
53the hollow caverns echoed and gave out a groan.
54ēt, sĭ fătā dēūm, sī mēns nōn laēvă fŭīssĕt,
55īmpŭlĕrāt fērro͜ Ārgŏlĭcās foēdārĕ lătēbrās,
56Troīăquĕ, nūnc stārēt, Prĭămīque͜ ārx āltă, mănērēs.
54And if the fates of the gods, if our minds had not been against us,
55he would have driven us to tear apart the Greek hiding places with steel,
56and Troy would now be standing, and you, lofty citadel of Priam, would still remain.

PriamiquePriam

Priam was the old king of Troy. Aeneas, who is telling the story, belonged to a junior branch of the royal family.
Questions to think about · 2
  1. What does the sound of the spear striking the horse suggest to the reader, and why do the Trojans not notice?
  2. In lines 54–56 Aeneas imagines what might have been. What does this tell us about his feelings as he tells the story?
What if they had listened? background

Lines 54 to 56 are a moment of "if only". Aeneas is telling the story years later at Dido's court, and he knows that Troy fell. He blames two things: the fates set by the gods, and the Trojans' own folly. This mix of divine will and human blindness runs through the whole of Book 2: the gods want Troy to fall, but the Trojans also make their own fatal choices.
