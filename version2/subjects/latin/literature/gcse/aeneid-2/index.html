Aeneid II

Virgil, Aeneid II.1–804 — the whole book, divided into twenty readable sections. Aeneas tells Dido how Troy fell: the wooden horse, Sinon’s lie, Laocoön and the serpents, the death of Priam, and the night’s escape.

The prescribed lines for Verse Literature B (cycle 5, 2027 & 2028) are 2.1–56 and 2.195–253, marked below. The rest of the book is here as wider reading — the context the set lines sit in.

  • Lesson
    Section 1 — Lines 1–39 — Aeneas begins; the Greeks feign departure Set text
    Silence falls over Dido’s hall and Aeneas begins his story: the Greeks, worn out by war, build the horse, hide their best men inside it and sail for Tenedos. The Trojans pour out onto the empty shore, and the crowd is split over what to do with the offering.
  • Lesson
    Section 2 — Lines 40–76 — Laocoön’s warning; Sinon captured Set text: 40–56
    Laocoön runs down from the citadel with the famous warning against Greeks bearing gifts and hurls his spear into the horse’s flank — until Trojan shepherds drag in a captive, and Sinon begins his performance.
  • Lesson
    Section 3 — Lines 77–107 — Sinon: Palamedes and the hatred of Ulysses
    Sinon spins his first web: kinsman of the ruined Palamedes, he claims he spoke out against the man’s judicial murder and so earned the lasting enmity of Ulysses.
  • Lesson
    Section 4 — Lines 108–144 — Sinon: the false oracle and the escape
    The Greeks, he says, wanted to sail home but were held by the weather; Calchas named Sinon himself as the sacrifice owed for their return. He broke his bonds and hid in a marsh — and the Trojans, moved to pity, spare him.
  • Lesson
    Section 5 — Lines 145–194 — Sinon on the Palladium and the horse
    Freed and questioned, Sinon explains the theft of the Palladium, the anger of Minerva, and why the horse was built so vast: to keep it out of the walls, or to make its entry the guarantee of Troy’s survival.
  • Lesson
    Section 6 — Lines 195–233 — Laocoön and the serpents Set text
    The lie is believed. Two great sea-snakes come from Tenedos, take Laocoön’s sons and then the priest himself, and glide up to the citadel of the goddess — a portent read exactly as Sinon would have wished.
  • Lesson
    Section 7 — Lines 234–267 — The horse brought in; the Greeks return Set text: 234–253
    The walls are breached to bring the horse inside; Cassandra is not believed. Night falls, the fleet returns from Tenedos under a silent moon, Sinon unlocks the horse, and the Greeks slip out into a sleeping city.
  • Lesson
    Section 8 — Lines 268–297 — The ghost of Hector
    In the first sleep Hector appears to Aeneas as he was after the dragging — mangled, filthy, weeping — and tells him to abandon the city and carry away Troy’s gods to a new home across the sea.
  • Lesson
    Section 9 — Lines 298–337 — Aeneas wakes; Panthus brings the news
    The noise of the sack breaks in. Aeneas arms without thinking; Panthus, priest of Apollo, arrives with the sacred things and the plain truth: the last day has come, and the Greeks are masters in the burning city.
  • Lesson
    Section 10 — Lines 338–369 — A band gathered; the sack rages
    Aeneas collects a handful of companions — Rhipeus, Epytus, Coroebus mad for Cassandra — and drives them on with a speech that offers only death. Grief, terror and the image of death are everywhere.
  • Lesson
    Section 11 — Lines 370–401 — Androgeos and the change of armour
    Androgeos blunders into them, mistaking them for allies, and is cut down. Coroebus urges the band to take Greek shields and devices — a ruse that works for a while.
  • Lesson
    Section 12 — Lines 402–437 — Cassandra dragged away; the ruse fails
    Cassandra is hauled from Minerva’s temple; Coroebus charges to save her, and the disguise destroys them — killed by Trojans from the roofs and Greeks who see through it. Rhipeus, most just of the Trojans, falls.
  • Lesson
    Section 13 — Lines 438–475 — The assault on Priam’s palace
    The fighting concentrates on the palace: ladders at the walls, the roof-tiles and gilded beams torn down and hurled on the attackers, and Pyrrhus glittering at the threshold like a snake come out into the light.
  • Lesson
    Section 14 — Lines 476–505 — Pyrrhus breaks in
    The doors give way, the guards are killed, and the great house is flooded with Greeks. Aeneas, watching from the roof, sees Hecuba and her hundred daughters and Priam at the altars.
  • Lesson
    Section 15 — Lines 506–558 — The death of Priam
    Priam straps on armour he can no longer carry, is rebuked by Hecuba, and watches Polites killed in front of him. His feeble spear is mocked; Pyrrhus drags him through his son’s blood and kills him at the altar.
  • Lesson
    Section 16 — Lines 559–620 — Helen and the vision of Venus
    Aeneas thinks of his own father and finds Helen hiding. Venus stops his hand, and strips the mist from his eyes: it is Neptune, Juno, Minerva and Jupiter himself who are pulling Troy down.
  • Lesson
    Section 17 — Lines 621–678 — Anchises refuses to leave
    Aeneas reaches home, but Anchises will not go: he would rather die in the ruins. Aeneas arms again to look for death, and Creusa clings to him in the doorway with Iulus in her arms.
  • Lesson
    Section 18 — Lines 679–729 — The flame on Iulus; the flight begins
    A tongue of harmless fire plays about the boy’s head, thunder sounds on the left and a star runs across the sky. Anchises yields at last; Aeneas takes him on his shoulders, Iulus by the hand, and sets out.
  • Lesson
    Section 19 — Lines 730–770 — Creusa lost
    A noise of feet, a moment of panic, and Creusa is gone. Aeneas hides the others in a valley, arms again, and goes back through the whole city calling her name.
  • Lesson
    Section 20 — Lines 771–804 — The ghost of Creusa; the exiles gather
    Creusa’s shade appears, larger than life, forbids his grief and foretells the long exile and the kingdom by the Tiber. Three times he tries to hold her. At dawn he finds a great crowd of exiles waiting, and takes to the hills.
