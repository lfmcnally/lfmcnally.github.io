220While he strains with his hands to tear apart the knots,
221his sacred ribbons drenched with gore and black venom,
222at the same time, he raises horrendous shouts to the stars:
223like the bellowing of a wounded bull when it has fled the altar
224and shaken the badly aimed axe from its neck.

Notes on the text 3
223–224
saucius aram taurusThe sacrificial bull
a wounded bull ... the altar
Laocoön had been sacrificing a bull to Neptune when the snakes came (line 202). Now he bellows like a sacrificial bull that has escaped the axe; but unlike the bull he cannot escape. The priest has become the victim.
226
TritonidisMinerva
the Tritonian goddess
Minerva (Pallas Athena), called Tritonis after Lake Triton in Libya, near which she was said to have been born. She supported the Greeks, her temple stood on the citadel of Troy, and the horse had been built as an offering to her.
227
clipeique ... orbeThe goddess's shield
and the round of her shield
Statues of Minerva showed her armed, with a round shield, and snakes were one of her symbols. By curling up at her feet, the snakes show that they belong to her.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. How does the bull simile in lines 223–224 increase our sympathy for Laocoön?
  2. Why do the snakes go to Minerva's shrine, and what do the Trojans conclude from this?
  3. How does Virgil show that the crowd has completely misunderstood what has happened?
