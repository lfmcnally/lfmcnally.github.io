esse… tuērīParallel Infinitives
to be… to guard
Two infinitive phrases in parallel: “to be priests” and “to guard your shrine.” The couple’s prayer is beautifully balanced — they ask for a role (priests) and a responsibility (guardians). Nothing greedy, nothing excessive: classic Baucis and Philemon modesty.
poscimusDelayed Verb
we ask
The main verb “we ask” does not appear until line 65, even though it is the first word you translate. Ovid makes us wait through everything they are asking for before revealing the asking itself — like saying “to be happy, to be healthy, to be loved — that is what I want.”
auferat hōra duōs eademEmphatic Word Order
may the same hour take away the two of us
The word eadem (“the same”) is the key — they do not just want to die, they want to die together, at the exact same moment. Its emphatic placement, framing the line around duōs (“the two”), echoes the unity theme we have seen throughout.
busta meaeEuphemism
the grave of my (wife)
Busta literally means “tomb” or “funeral pyre” — a delicate way of referring to death without saying “dead body” or “corpse.” Philemon cannot bear to speak directly about his wife dying. The euphemism shows his tenderness and the depth of his feeling.
neu sim tumulandus ab illāGerundive of Obligation
nor may I have to be buried by her
The gerundive tumulandus (“having to be buried”) expresses obligation or necessity. Philemon dreads the idea that Baucis would have the awful duty of burying him. It is not just about dying — it is about sparing each other the grief of being the survivor.
frondēre… frondēreHistoric Infinitive
to grow leaves… to grow leaves
The historic infinitive uses an infinitive instead of a finite verb for vivid, rapid narration. Frondēre (“to grow leaves”) appears twice — unusual grammar that creates urgency. The transformation is happening so fast that normal verb tenses cannot keep up.
Philēmona Baucis / Baucida… PhilēmōnParallelism
Baucis (saw) Philemon / (saw) Baucis… Philemon
Parallelism is when the same structure repeats. Here we get object–subject, then object–subject again: Philemon (obj) – Baucis (subj) / Baucis (obj) – Philemon (subj). The mirrored structure shows them doing the exact same thing at the exact same time — each watching the other transform.
dum licuitPathos
while they could
“While they could” — just two words, but devastating. They are exchanging words while they still can, because in moments they will not be able to speak at all. Ovid slips this in almost casually, but it is heartbreaking: their human voices are about to disappear forever.
valē… ō coniūnxDirect Speech
farewell… o spouse
Ovid gives us their actual last words in direct speech: “Farewell, o husband/wife.” After all the third-person narration, we suddenly hear their voices directly. It is intimate and immediate — and notice that they both say the same thing to each other. Even their final words are shared.
dīxēre simul, simul… tēxitRepetition
they said at the same time, at the same time (it) covered
The word simul (“at the same time”) appears twice in quick succession. This is no accident — Ovid hammers home that their final words and their transformation happen at exactly the same moment. They say “farewell” and the bark covers them simultaneously. Even in metamorphosis, they are perfectly synchronised.
vōta fidēs sequiturPersonification
fulfilment follows the prayers
“Fulfilment followed the prayers” — fidēs (faith, trust, fulfilment) is personified as something that actively follows. It is as if the gods’ reliability is a living thing that responds to worthy prayers. The gods keep their word.
vōta fidēs sequiturBrevity
fulfilment follows the prayers
After Philemon’s long, emotional prayer (lines 64–67), Ovid gives us just three words: “Fulfilment followed the prayers.” That is it. Done. The brevity is striking — no drama, no elaboration. The gods simply grant the wish, and the contrast with the lengthy prayer makes it even more powerful.
ō coniūnxAmbiguity
o husband/wife
The word coniūnx can mean either “husband” or “wife” — it is gender-neutral. Ovid uses this to brilliant effect: we are told they both say “o coniūnx” at the same time, and it works perfectly for both of them. He does not need two separate speeches; one word captures both farewells.
