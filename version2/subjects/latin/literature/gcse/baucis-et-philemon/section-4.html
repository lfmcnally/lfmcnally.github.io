sponte suā… per sēPleonasm
of its own accord… by itself
Ovid hammers home the miraculous nature here. Sponte suā means “of its own accord” and per sē means “by itself” — essentially the same thing said twice. This pleonasm (repetition for emphasis) stresses that nobody is topping up this wine. It is refilling itself.
videntHistoric Present
they see
Ovid switches to the present tense vident (“they see”) even though he is narrating past events. This historic present makes the action feel immediate and vivid — one moment the bowl is empty, the next it is full. The present tense captures that “wait, what?!” moment.
sponte suā… sē… succrēscereSibilance
of its own accord… by itself… to grow up
Listen to all those “s” sounds: sponte suā… sē… succrēscere. This sibilance creates a hissing, whispering effect — almost like an incantation murmured under the breath. It is sound mimicking meaning: the wine is magically rising, and the language itself sounds magical.
attonitīEmotive Diction
thunderstruck
Attonitī literally means “thunderstruck” — struck by tonitrus (thunder). It is a strong word for shock, far beyond “surprised”. And here is the clever bit: they are being thunderstruck in the presence of Jupiter, the god of thunder. The couple have no idea why they feel this divine awe, but we do.
manibus supīnīsAblative Phrase
with upturned hands
Manibus supīnīs literally means “with hands turned upward” — the standard Roman prayer posture, palms facing the sky to receive blessings from the gods above. Ovid uses this technical religious detail to show that Baucis and Philemon instinctively know how to behave: proper, pious, doing everything right.
Baucis… PhilēmōnPolysyndeton
Baucis… Philemon
The couple are named together with the repeated -que (“both… and”), which is called polysyndeton. It emphasises their unity — they are a pair, a team, inseparable. The adjective timidus (“timid”) is sandwiched between them, and while it agrees grammatically with Philemon, it characterises them both.
ūnicus ānserPathos
a single goose
This single goose (ūnicus) is all they have. It is not just a pet — it is described as the cūstōdia (“guardian”) of their tiny home (minimae vīllae). The pathos is thick: they are willing to sacrifice literally their only possession to honour their guests. That is next-level hospitality.
dīs hospitibusDramatic Irony
for the gods, their guests
Dīs hospitibus means “for the gods, their guests” — but the couple do not know yet that their guests are gods. That is dramatic irony: we (the readers) know the truth, but they do not. They mean to sacrifice their goose “to honour the gods” by feeding the actual gods. It is both touching and darkly comic.
celer… tardōsAntithesis
swift… the slow ones
Picture the scene: the goose is celer (“swift”), zooming about on its wings (pennā), while the elderly couple are tardōs aetāte (“slow with age”), huffing and puffing to catch it. It is slapstick comedy — two old people chasing a goose round a tiny cottage — but with pathos too. The contrast of celer and tardōs is classic antithesis.
tandemClimax
finally
After all that chasing, we get tandem — “finally!” This little word marks the climax of the goose chase. And where does the goose flee? Ad ipsōs… deōs — “to the very gods themselves.” The emphatic ipsōs stresses that the goose fled to the actual gods, inadvertently blowing their cover.
dī sumusDivine Revelation
we are gods
After ten lines of build-up — the miracle, the shock, the goose chase — the gods reveal themselves with just two words: dī sumus (“we are gods”). No fanfare, no dramatic monologue. The brevity is the point. Gods do not need to explain themselves; they state facts. The contrast with the frantic human activity beforehand makes this hit harder.
vīcīnia… impiaEnjambment
the neighbourhood… irreligious
Look where impia (“irreligious”) lands — pushed onto the next line, separated from the noun it describes (vīcīnia, “neighbourhood”). This enjambment makes you wait for the damning adjective: you read “the neighbourhood will pay the penalty it deserves…” and then BAM — “irreligious.” The delay adds weight to the condemnation.
relinquite… comitāte… īteTricolon
leave… accompany… go
The gods issue three commands in quick succession: “leave your home… accompany our steps… go together.” This tricolon — a group of three parallel imperatives — gives the speech a rhythmic, authoritative quality. Gods do not waffle; they give clear, clipped orders. The final simul (“together”) is touching: even in command, the gods recognise the couple as a unit.
