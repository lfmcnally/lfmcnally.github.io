1Iuppiter hūc speciē mortālī cum -que parente
1Jupiter came here in human form and, with his father, (came)
2vēnit Atlantiadēs positīs caducifer ālīs.
2the wand-bearing descendant of Atlas, having set aside his wings.
3mīlle domōs adiēre locum requiem -que petentēs:
3They approached a thousand houses, seeking a place and rest:
4mīlle domōs clausēre serae. tamen ūna recēpit,
4However, one received (them), a small (one), it is true, covered with stalks — bars shut a thousand houses.
5parva quidem stīpulīs et cannā tēcta palūstrī,
5and marshy reeds.
6sed pia Baucis anus parīlī -que aetāte Philēmōn
6But the dutiful old woman Baucis, and Philemon, equal in age,
7illā sunt annīs iūnctī iuvenālibus, illā
7were united in their younger years in that cottage, (and)
8cōnsenūēre casā paupertātem -que fatendō
8grew old together in that cottage, and by acknowledging
9effēcēre levem nec inīquā mente ferendō.
9their poverty and made it light by bearing it not with an unfair mind.
10nec refert, dominōs illīc famulōs -ne requīrās:
10And it does not matter whether you look for masters there or slaves:
11tōta domus duo sunt, īdem pārent -que iubent -que.
11the two are the whole house, they both obey and command.

Style notes this section · 19Word order · 1Repetition and structure · 6Word choice · 4Imagery and tone · 1Grammar · 7
IuppiterInitial Position
Jupiter
The first word of the passage is the king of the gods. Ovid announces divine presence immediately — this is not a story about ordinary travellers. Placing Iuppiter emphatically at the start mirrors the fanfare of epic openings.
AtlantiadēsPatronymic
descendant of Atlas
Mercury is named not as Mercury but as "descendant of Atlas" — a Greek-style patronymic borrowed from Homer. Ovid uses it to elevate the register: this is proper epic diction, lending grandeur to a story set in a humble cottage.
positīs ālīsAblative Absolute
having set aside his wings
An ablative absolute compressing a whole subordinate clause into two words. Mercury has removed his winged sandals to pass as mortal. The gods are incognito — only the reader knows who is really standing at each door.
caduciferCompound Epithet
wand-bearing
A compound adjective that immediately identifies Mercury through his famous attribute, the caduceus. These compound epithets are a hallmark of epic style — one word doing double duty, naming and characterising simultaneously.
mīlle domōs… mīlle domōsAnaphora
a thousand houses… a thousand houses
The same phrase — mīlle domōs — recurs with opposite verbs: approached, then shut. The repetition makes the universal rejection feel crushing. Every single house said no. The parallelism of language mirrors the parallelism of the situation.
adiēreSyncopated Perfect
they approached
A shortened (syncopated) perfect — adiērunt compressed to adiēre for metrical elegance. These syncopated forms are a feature of Latin poetry. The same technique appears with clausēre and cōnsenūēre later.
clausēreSyncopated Perfect
shut
Another syncopated perfect, but note who performs the action: serae — the bars themselves. Not "people barred the doors" but "bars shut the houses." The buildings themselves seem actively hostile, making the rejection feel more absolute.
tamenPivotal Conjunction
however
A single word carries the entire narrative pivot. After a thousand rejections, tamen signals everything changes. One house was different. The brevity of the pivot — just one word — makes it more powerful than any elaboration could.
parva quidemConcessive
small, it is true
Ovid is upfront about the cottage's poverty. The concessive quidem ("admittedly") prepares for contrast: physical modesty will be offset by moral generosity. The honesty about the cottage's limitations makes the warmth inside it more striking.
sedAdversative
but
After describing the cottage's humble exterior, sed swings us to what really matters: the people inside. The physical description is done. Now comes the character introduction that makes this story special.
piaMoral Epithet
dutiful / pious
Pia is the highest Roman moral commendation — duty to the gods, to family, and to guests. Applying it to Baucis at her introduction tells the reader immediately: this woman is one of the good ones. Her pietas will earn divine favour.
Baucis… PhilēmōnNamed Subjects
Baucis… Philemon
After the anonymity of "a thousand houses," Ovid finally gives us names. These two individuals stand out precisely because everyone else has been faceless. Naming them personalises the story and signals their importance.
parīlī aetāteAblative of Description
equal in age
Two words conveying a lifetime of partnership. Matched in age, matched in everything. The ablative of description packs biographical detail into the briefest possible space — characteristic of Ovid's economy. The equality here anticipates the final line's antithesis.
illā… illāAnaphora
in that cottage… in that cottage
The same word repeated — that cottage, that cottage — drums home the couple's rootedness. They married there, grew old there; their whole life is in one small, unchanged place. The anaphora creates a rhythm of contentment.
cōnsenūēreCompound Verb
they grew old together
The con- prefix ("together, jointly") carries the emotional weight. They did not just age — they aged as one. A single syncopated perfect quietly contains their entire shared history.
effēcēre levemSyncopated Perfect
they made (it) light
A fourth syncopated perfect (effēcērunt → effēcēre), framed by two ablative gerunds — fatendō ("by acknowledging") and ferendō ("by bearing"). It is not wealth but attitude that makes their poverty levem ("light"): contentment is presented as a deliberate act, not an accident of circumstance.
tōta domus duo suntHyperbole
the two are the whole house
A proper Roman household would have dozens of people — master, slaves, freedmen, clients. Here? Just Baucis and Philemon. Calling them tōta domus is playfully grand, contrasting the language of a great household with the reality of two people in a cottage.
īdemEmphatic Pronoun
the same people
There is no distinction between who serves and who is served. In a normal Roman household, hierarchies were rigid. Here, Baucis and Philemon are simultaneously masters and servants. Īdem squashes all those distinctions into equality.
pārentque iubentqueAntithesis
they both obey and command
They obey and command — a paradox. In Rome these roles were rigidly separate. Baucis and Philemon have dissolved that hierarchy into a partnership of equals. The -que… -que polysyndeton links opposites into one seamless relationship — a witty, epigrammatic ending.
