amboEmphatic Position
both
Ovid opens this section with ambo (“both”) — and that is no accident. From the very start he stresses that these two act as a single unit. They obey, climb and respond together, and this word sets up the theme of unity for the whole passage.
nītunturHistoric Present
they strive
This is the historic present — Ovid switches from past tense to present to make the action vivid. Instead of “they struggled” he gives us “they strive”, putting us right beside the couple as they labour up the hill.
quantum semel īre sagitta missa potestEpic Simile
as far as an arrow, once shot, can go
Ovid borrows from the epic poets here. Measuring a distance by how far an arrow flies is very Homeric and Virgilian — grand imagery you would expect for heroes, not elderly peasants. That is the point: he gives this humble couple the epic treatment.
tantum sua tēcta manēreDivine Favour
only their own house remaining
Everyone else’s home is underwater, but their house is still standing. Ovid emphasises sua (“their own”) and tantum (“only”) to drive home how exceptional this is. The gods have singled these two out personally.
dum mīrantur… dum dēflentAnaphora
while they marvel… while they weep
Anaphora is the repetition of a word at the start of clauses — here dum (“while”). The effect is to show two emotions happening at once: they are amazed at being saved and weeping for their lost neighbours. The structure captures that mixed feeling.
illa vetus… casa parva… vertiturHyperbaton
that old, small house… is turned
Hyperbaton means placing words in an unusual order — here Ovid makes us wait for the main verb. We hear “that old house, small even for its two owners” and keep wondering “yes, and?” Then finally: “is turned into a temple.” The delay builds suspense and makes the transformation land harder.
vertitur… subiēre… flāvēscunt… videnturHistoric Present
is turned… replaced… become yellow… are seen
The historic present again — but now in a cluster. “Is turned… replace… turn golden… are seen.” Ovid fires off present-tense verbs one after another so we experience the metamorphosis in real time, happening before our eyes.
-que… -que… -quePolysyndeton
and… and… and
Polysyndeton means piling up conjunctions — “and… and… and…”. The repeated -que makes the list feel breathless and overwhelming. Golden roof and carved doors and marble floor — too much to take in, just as Baucis and Philemon must have felt.
placidō… ōreCharacterising Diction
with a calm expression
Jupiter has just wiped out an entire village. Now he speaks “with a calm expression” — not worried, not guilty, simply calm. That contrast is chilling, exposing the vast gap between gods and mortals: what devastates humans costs Jupiter nothing.
iūste senex… coniuge iūstōPolyptoton
honest old man… honest husband
Polyptoton is the repetition of the same word in different grammatical forms. Here Jupiter calls Philemon “honest” (iūste) and says he has an “honest” (iūstō) wife. The repetition hammers home the message: your justice is exactly why you have been saved.
iūdicium… commūneFraming
a shared decision
Remember ambo (“both”) at the start? Now we get commūne (“shared”) at the end. Ovid frames the passage with words about togetherness. Even when making the biggest decision of their lives, they make it jointly — the whole point of their story.
sagitta / missaEnjambment
an arrow / shot
Enjambment is when a phrase runs over a line break. Here sagitta (“arrow”) sits at the end of one line and missa (“shot”) opens the next, so the sense literally flies across the gap — just as the arrow does through the air.
