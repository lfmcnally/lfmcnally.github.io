cum in apparando acerrime esset occupatus, Carthaginienses bellum cum Romanis composueruntContrast
while he was busy preparing, the Carthaginians made peace with the Romans
Hannibal's preparations for war are set against his city making peace. This is the first sign of the split between Hannibal and his own people that leads to his exile.
acerrimeSuperlative
most energetically
The superlative adverb shows Hannibal throwing all his energy into preparing for more war, which makes the peace made behind his back all the more of a blow.
nihilo setiusEmphasis
none the less
The phrase shows Hannibal carrying on regardless, as if the peace did not apply to him.
itemque Mago frater eiusPairing
and likewise his brother Mago
Mago is linked to Hannibal, so the Barca brothers act as one. This prepares for the senate naming them both in its reply.
P. Sulpicium C. Aurelium consulesRoman point of view
the consuls Publius Sulpicius and Gaius Aurelius
Nepos dates events in Carthage by the Roman consuls, so the story is told from Rome's side even when the scene is Africa.
qui … gratias agerent … donarent … peterentTricolon
to thank … to present … to ask
The envoys come to thank, to give and only then to ask. Putting the thanks and the gift first shows Carthage humbly trying to win Rome's goodwill.
corona aureaVivid detail
with a golden crown
A gift of gold shows how eager the Carthaginians are to please Rome, which makes their general's continued command look like an embarrassment.
responsum est: munus eorum gratum acceptumque esseIndirect speech
the reply was that their gift was welcome and accepted
The senate's answer is reported in indirect statement, which gives it the cool, formal tone of an official decree.
gratum acceptumquePairing
welcome and accepted
Two near-synonyms make the reply sound gracious, which makes the refusal that follows all the sharper.
obsides … captivos non remissurosContrast
the hostages … they would not return the prisoners
The senate grants the first request and refuses the second. The balanced reply makes the "no" stand out.
Hannibalem, cuius opera susceptum bellum foretEmphatic position
Hannibal, through whose efforts the war had been begun
Hannibal is named and blamed for starting the war in the same breath. Rome holds him personally responsible.
inimicissimum nomini RomanoSuperlative
the bitterest enemy of the Roman name
The superlative and the phrase "the Roman name" make Hannibal the enemy of everything Rome stands for. This is the Roman view of him, put in the senate's own words.
etiamnumEmphasis
even now
Rome's complaint is that Hannibal is still in command. The word shows Roman suspicion that the war is not really over while he holds an army.
hoc responso Carthaginienses cognitoEnclosing word order
when the Carthaginians had learnt this reply
The Carthaginians are placed inside the ablative absolute, wrapped up in Rome's reply, which suits a city now ruled by Rome's wishes.
Hannibalem domum et Magonem revocaruntCharacterisation
they recalled Hannibal and Mago home
Carthage obeys at once and recalls its greatest general to please Rome, which foreshadows how readily it will abandon him later.
rexWord choice
king
Rex was a hated word at Rome, so calling Hannibal a "king" might startle Roman readers. Nepos explains at once that the office lasted only a year, like a consulship.
ut enim Romae consules, sic Carthagine … regesComparison
for as at Rome consuls, so at Carthage kings
Nepos explains a foreign office by comparing it with a Roman one. The balanced ut … sic helps his Roman readers and makes Carthage look like a mirror of Rome.
Notes on the text 5
Carthaginienses bellum cum Romanis composueruntThe peace of 201 BC
the Carthaginians made peace with Rome
After Zama (202 BC) Carthage gave up Spain and almost all its fleet, agreed to pay 10,000 talents over fifty years, and could not make war without Rome's permission.
MagoMago
Mago
Hannibal's younger brother, who fought with him in Italy and later commanded in Spain and northern Italy. Nepos's account of Mago here and in chapter 8 does not match Livy, who says Mago died in 203 BC of a wound on the voyage home from Italy.
FregellisFregellae
at Fregellae
A Latin colony on the river Liris, south-east of Rome. Carthaginian hostages could be kept there in some comfort, away from the city.
rex factus estSuffetes
he was made king
Carthage's two chief magistrates, elected every year, were called suffetes. Hannibal held the office in 196 BC. Nepos uses the Roman word rex for it.
praetorPraetor
general
Nepos uses a Roman title for Hannibal's command of the army in Spain, which he took over in 221 BC. Counted from then, Nepos's "twenty-second year" does not quite fit the date of his suffeteship, 196 BC.

Carthage after Zama background

After Scipio defeated Hannibal at Zama in 202 BC, Carthage accepted peace in 201 on hard terms. It gave up Spain and its other overseas possessions, surrendered all but ten of its warships and its war elephants, paid an indemnity of 10,000 talents spread over fifty years, and promised not to make war without Rome's permission.

Hannibal stayed in public life. In 196 BC he was elected one of the two suffetes, the annual chief magistrates of Carthage, and used the office to attack the corruption of the ruling families and to put the city's finances in order. His success made him powerful enemies at home, and they were quick to tell Rome that he was plotting a new war.
