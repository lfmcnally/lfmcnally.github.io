superabatur navium multitudineEmphatic position
he was outnumbered in ships
The verb comes first, so Hannibal's disadvantage is stated bluntly before anything else, and the reader wonders how he can possibly win.
multitudine … multitudinem … multitudinePolyptoton
the number … a great number … the sheer number
The same word returns in different cases: Hannibal's lack of numbers in ships is answered by a multitude of snakes.
doloEmphatic position
by trickery
dolo is placed first in its clause: since he cannot win by numbers, cunning becomes his weapon, which is the theme of the whole episode.
dolo erat pugnandumGerundive of obligation
he had to fight by trickery
The impersonal gerundive presents trickery as a necessity forced on him, not a choice, which lets Nepos excuse a method that Romans might think unworthy of a soldier.
dolo … armisContrast
by trickery … in arms
Trickery is set against armed strength, the two ways of winning a battle. Hannibal lacks the second, so he turns to the first.
quam plurimasSuperlative
as many as possible
quam with the superlative stresses the scale of the plan: Hannibal does nothing by halves.
venenatas serpentes vivasVivid detail
live poisonous snakes
Three words pile up the danger: they are snakes, they are venomous, and they are alive. The strange weapon would startle any reader.
in vasa fictiliaVivid detail
into earthenware pots
Cheap clay pots that would shatter on impact: the homely detail makes the secret weapon all the more surprising.
die ipsoEmphasis
on the very day
ipso stresses the timing: the plan is revealed to the men only on the day of the battle, which keeps it secret and raises the tension.
convocatHistoric present
he calls together
The switch to the present tense brings the reader into the moment of Hannibal's briefing, which makes the narrative vivid.
omnes ut in unamJuxtaposition
all … onto one
"All" is set right beside "one": every Bithynian ship is to converge on a single target.
omnes … in unam … a ceteris tantumContrast
all on one … only against the rest
The men are to throw everything at one ship and merely defend themselves against the others. The balance makes the plan easy to grasp.
unam Eumenis regis concurrant navemHyperbaton
onto the one ship of King Eumenes
unam is separated from navem, so the single target frames the king's name and the verb.
id illos facile serpentium multitudine consecuturosIndirect speech
that they would easily achieve this through the mass of snakes
Hannibal's orders are reported in indirect speech, so Nepos can summarise the briefing briskly without stopping the story.
facileCharacterisation
easily
Hannibal speaks with complete confidence: the snakes will "easily" do the rest. The reader sees his assurance as a commander.
quem si aut cepissent aut interfecissentConditional
if they captured or killed him
aut … aut makes either outcome worth the reward, which shows that Hannibal wants Eumenes out of the battle whatever it takes.
magno … praemioHyperbaton

magno is separated from praemio, so the promise of a great reward stretches across the clause and ends the chapter on the men's incentive.

Questions to think about · 3
  1. Nepos says that Hannibal "had to" fight by trickery. Does the chapter present his use of dolus as admirable, or as something that needs excusing to a Roman reader?
  2. How does Nepos make Hannibal's instructions to his men sound clear, confident and complete?
  3. Why do you think Nepos sets out the whole plan before he describes the battle? What does the reader gain from knowing it in advance?
The war between Bithynia and Pergamum background

After the defeat of Antiochus at Magnesia (190 BC), the peace terms Rome imposed required that Hannibal be handed over. He escaped, and after a stay on Crete he ended up at the court of Prusias I, king of Bithynia, in north-west Asia Minor. Prusias was at war with his neighbour Eumenes II of Pergamum, Rome's most loyal ally in the region, and he put Hannibal in charge of his forces.

The fighting lasted from about 186 to 183 BC, until Rome stepped in to end it. The story of the snakes thrown in pots onto the Pergamene ships is also told by Justin, in his summary of the lost history of Pompeius Trogus, so it was evidently a well-known episode. For Nepos it is the perfect example of Hannibal winning by intelligence when he could not win by numbers.
