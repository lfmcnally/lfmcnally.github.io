conflixerat … pepuleratPluperfect
he had fought … had driven back
The pluperfects reach back before chapter 3 ended, so the narrative picks up events that happened on the way to the Alps.
cum hoc eodem … tertio idem ScipioRepetition
with this same man … for the third time this same Scipio
Nepos keeps stressing that it is the same Roman commander, beaten again and again, which makes Hannibal's dominance seem relentless.
decernit … dimittitHistoric present
he fights it out … sends him away
The switch into the present makes this second battle vivid, as if happening before the reader's eyes.
sauciumque inde ac fugatumVivid detail
wounded and routed
Two participles show the consul both hurt and running, a humiliating picture of a Roman commander.
manum conseruit, utrosque profligavitAsyndeton
he engaged them, he crushed them both
Two short clauses with no connective: the battle is over as soon as it begins.
utrosqueEmphasis
both of them
Two consuls together still cannot stop Hannibal, which raises the scale of the victory.
adeo gravi morbo afficitur oculorum, ut postea numquam dextro aeque bene usus sitContrast
he was struck by so severe an eye disease that he never again used his right eye as well
Right in the middle of his victories, Hannibal is shown as a man suffering lasting physical harm, which makes him human.
lecticaque ferreturPathos
was being carried in a litter
The great general is too ill to walk or ride, which makes what follows all the more remarkable.
lecticaque ferretur, C. Flaminium consulem … occiditContrast
was carried in a litter … killed the consul Gaius Flaminius
A sick man in a litter destroys a consul and his army: the contrast shows that Hannibal's mind, not his body, wins battles.
insidiis circumventumCharacterisation
surrounded by an ambush
Hannibal wins by cunning, a theme that runs through the Life: Romans fall into his traps.
cum delecta manuEmphasis
with a picked force
Even Rome's best troops are beaten, which adds to the sense that no one can resist him.
obviam ei venerunt duo consulesCharacterisation
the two consuls came out to meet him
Rome sends both its consuls at once, a sign of how desperate the situation has become.
uno proelioEmphasis
in a single battle
Two consular armies routed in one battle: the phrase stresses the scale of Cannae without needing to name it.
Paulum consulem occidit et aliquot praeterea consulares, in his Cn. Servilium GeminumClimax
he killed the consul Paulus and several former consuls besides, among them Gnaeus Servilius Geminus
The list of dead rises from a consul to a group of ex-consuls, so the reader sees how much of Rome's leadership died at Cannae.
qui superiore anno fuerat consulPrecise detail
who had been consul the year before
Naming a man who was consul only a year earlier makes the losses concrete and personal for Roman readers.
Notes on the text 5
P. Cornelio Scipione consulePublius Cornelius Scipio
the consul Publius Cornelius Scipio
Consul in 218 BC and father of Scipio Africanus, who would beat Hannibal at Zama. At the Rhône there was only a clash of cavalry patrols: Scipio arrived after Hannibal had already crossed.
Clastidi apud PadumClastidium and the Po
at Clastidium on the Po
The battle usually called the Ticinus (218 BC), a cavalry battle near the Po, in which Scipio was badly wounded. Clastidium was a nearby town that Hannibal later took.
apud TrebiamThe Trebia
at the Trebia
A tributary of the Po. In December 218 BC Hannibal lured the Romans across the freezing river and ambushed them with troops hidden in a stream bed.
apud TrasumenumLake Trasimene
at Lake Trasimene
In June 217 BC Hannibal trapped Flaminius' army in mist between the hills and the lake shore. Flaminius and about 15,000 Romans were killed.
in ApuliamCannae
into Apulia
Cannae, in Apulia in south-east Italy, where in 216 BC Hannibal encircled and destroyed the army of Paullus and Varro (here C. Terentius).

Hannibal's victories in Italy background

In 218 BC Hannibal came down from the Alps into the Po valley. He beat Publius Cornelius Scipio in a cavalry battle at the Ticinus, near Clastidium, where Scipio was wounded, and then, in December, the combined armies of Scipio and Tiberius Sempronius Longus at the river Trebia. In spring 217 BC he crossed the Apennines and the flooded marshes of the Arno, where he lost the sight of one eye, and in June trapped the consul Gaius Flaminius at Lake Trasimene: Flaminius and most of his army were killed.

In 216 BC came Cannae in Apulia. The consuls Lucius Aemilius Paullus and Gaius Terentius Varro led the largest army Rome had ever put into the field, and Hannibal surrounded and destroyed it. Paullus died with tens of thousands of his men. It was the worst defeat in Rome's history.
