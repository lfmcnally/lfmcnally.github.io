tali cohortatione militum factaAblative absolute
with the men encouraged in this way
The ablative absolute wraps up the previous scene in a few words, so the story moves straight on to the battle.
deduciturHistoric present
is led out
The present tense makes the launching of the two fleets vivid and immediate.
priusquam signum pugnae dareturDelay
before the signal for battle was given
The temporal clause holds the fighting back: Hannibal's trick takes place in the tense moment before the battle begins.
HannibalEmphatic position
Hannibal
His name comes in the middle of the long sentence, after the scene is set, so attention moves from the two fleets to the one man in control.
ut palam faceret suis, quo loco Eumenes essetDramatic irony
to make clear to his men where Eumenes was
The purpose of the messenger is told to the reader before the enemy can guess it, so we watch the Pergamenes walk into the trap.
in scapha cum caduceoVivid detail
in a small boat with a herald's staff
The tiny boat and the herald's staff make the messenger look harmless and official, which is exactly why the trick works.
mittitHistoric present
he sends
The sentence ends on a crisp present-tense verb: Hannibal acts.
epistulamque ostendensVivid detail
holding up a letter
The letter held up for all to see is the bait: it gets the messenger taken to the king, and so reveals which ship is his.
statimEmphasis
at once
The messenger is taken to the king immediately, which shows how easily the trick succeeds.
quod nemo dubitabatIrony
since no one doubted
Everyone is sure the letter is about peace, but the reader knows the plan. The same words opened the Life (1.1), where they backed a certainty that was true; here the certainty is wrong.
ducis nave declarataAblative absolute
once the commander's ship had been pointed out
The real purpose of the mission is achieved in an ablative absolute, almost in passing, as if it were a minor detail.
eodem, unde erat egressusRing structure
back to where he had set out from
The messenger returns exactly where he started, his job done, which shows how smoothly the plan unfolds.
at EumenesContrast
but Eumenes
at marks a sharp turn from the messenger's success to the king's confusion.
nihil … nisi quae ad irridendum eum pertinerentEmphatic negative
nothing except what was meant to mock him
The letter holds only insults: Hannibal humiliates the king even before the battle, which shows his contempt for Rome's ally.
mirabatur neque reperiebatPairing
he wondered and could not find
Two verbs show Eumenes puzzled and baffled, so the reader enjoys knowing what he does not.
non dubitavitLitotes
he did not hesitate
The understatement makes Eumenes seem bold, but the reader knows he is hurrying into a trap.
Notes on the text 1
caduceoThe herald's staff
herald's staff
The caduceus (Greek kerykeion) was the staff of heralds and envoys and the emblem of Hermes. It marked its bearer as sacred and not to be harmed, so it guaranteed the messenger safe passage.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Is Hannibal's use of a herald with a herald's staff a fair stratagem or a breach of the rules of war? How does Nepos seem to want us to judge it?
  2. How does Nepos use the reader's knowledge of the plan to create tension and humour in this chapter?
  3. Why do you think the letter was written to mock Eumenes rather than simply left blank?
