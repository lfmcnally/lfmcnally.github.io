hac pugna pugnataFigura etymologica
this battle having been fought
The noun and the participle come from the same root, a heavy, emphatic way of marking the end of Cannae.
nullo resistenteAblative absolute
with no one resisting
Two words tell us that after Cannae there was nothing between Hannibal and Rome.
in propinquis urbi montibus moratus estVivid detail
he lingered in the hills near the city
Hannibal camping within sight of Rome is a frightening image for Roman readers, and moratus est hints at a missed chance.
Q. Fabius Maximus, dictator RomanusCharacterisation
Quintus Fabius Maximus, the Roman dictator
Fabius is given his full name and title, which builds him up as a worthy opponent before Hannibal outwits him.
hic clausus locorum angustiisParticiple
trapped here by the narrow terrain
The perfect participle shows Hannibal apparently caught, which makes his escape more striking.
sine ullo detrimento exercitusEmphatic negative
without any loss to his army
Ullo stresses that the escape was complete: not a single man was lost.
Fabioque, callidissimo imperatori, dedit verbaIrony
and he tricked Fabius, the most cunning of commanders
The superlative praises Fabius' cunning in the very clause where he is fooled, which makes Hannibal even cleverer. Dare verba is an idiom for deceiving someone.
obducta nocteAblative absolute
with night drawn over
The darkness is described as a covering, which suits a trick that depends on not being seen clearly.
sarmenta in cornibus iuvencorum deligata incenditVivid detail
he set fire to bundles of twigs tied to the horns of young cattle
The precise, strange detail of burning branches on the cattle's horns lets the reader picture the trick in the dark.
multitudinem magnam dispalatamEmphasis
a great number, scattered everywhere
Numbers and scattering make the lights seem like a whole army on the move.
repentino visuEmphasis
the sudden sight
Repentino stresses the shock of the moment, which explains the panic that follows.
tantum terrorem iniecit … ut egredi extra vallum nemo sit aususResult clause
he caused such terror that no one dared to go outside the rampart
The result clause shows the scale of the fear: a whole Roman army stays behind its wall.
dolo productum in proeliumCharacterisation
lured into battle by a trick
Dolo again: Hannibal wins by deception, the quality Romans both feared and condemned as Punic treachery.
Ti. Sempronium Gracchum, iterum consulem … M. Claudium Marcellum, quinquies consulemClimax
Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, consul for the second time … Marcus Claudius Marcellus, five times consul
The victims grow in seniority, ending with a man who had been consul five times, so the list builds to its most impressive name.
absensRepetition
while absent
The same word was used of Hannibal turning Philip against Rome (chapter 2). Hannibal can destroy his enemies even when he is not there.
longum est omnia enumerare proeliaPraeteritio
it would take too long to list all the battles
Nepos says he will not list every battle, which suggests there were far too many victories to count.
quantus ille fueritRhetorical emphasis
how great that man was
Ille gives Hannibal a grand, almost admiring tone, and the whole sentence is built to prove his greatness.
nemo ei in acie restitit, nemo adversus eumAnaphora
no one stood against him in battle, no one against him
Repeating nemo at the start of both clauses drives home the claim that no Roman could face him.

CapuamCapua
Capua
The richest city of Campania, which went over to Hannibal after Cannae and served as his base. Rome besieged and retook it in 211 BC.
in agro FalernoThe ager Falernus
in the Falernian territory
Rich farmland in northern Campania, famous for its wine. In 217 BC Fabius tried to trap Hannibal in the valley there.
M. Minucium RufumMinucius
Marcus Minucius Rufus
Fabius' Master of Horse in 217 BC. Frustrated with Fabius' caution, the people gave him equal command; he attacked Hannibal and had to be rescued by Fabius.
M. Claudium MarcellumGracchus and Marcellus
Marcus Claudius Marcellus
Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus was killed in an ambush in Lucania in 212 BC. Marcellus, the captor of Syracuse and called the Sword of Rome, died in an ambush near Venusia in 208 BC.

  2. How does the trick with the cattle show the qualities Nepos most admires in Hannibal?

Nepos and chronology background

Nepos is not interested in strict chronological order. Hannibal did not march on Rome straight after Cannae: he did that in 211 BC, five years later, in an attempt to draw the Romans away from the siege of Capua. The trick with the cattle against Fabius came in 217 BC, before Cannae, and the defeat of Minucius in the same year. Gracchus was killed in 212 BC and Marcellus in 208 BC.

Quintus Fabius Maximus was made dictator after Trasimene in 217 BC. His strategy was to shadow Hannibal and avoid pitched battle, wearing down the invaders while Rome recovered. It was unpopular at the time, and it earned him the nickname Cunctator, the Delayer, but after Cannae Rome came back to it.
