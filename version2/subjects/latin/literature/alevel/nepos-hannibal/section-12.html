sic conservatis suis rebus Poenus illusis Cretensibus omnibusBalance
his property thus saved, the Carthaginian, with all the Cretans fooled
Two ablative absolutes frame the subject, one for his money saved and one for the Cretans fooled, so the trick is summed up neatly before the story moves on.
PoenusWord choice
the Carthaginian
Nepos calls Hannibal "the Phoenician", the Roman word that brought to mind proverbial Punic cunning, just after he has tricked the Cretans.
omnibusEmphasis
all
Not some Cretans but all of them were fooled, which completes the joke of the last chapter.
eodem animo fuit erga ItaliamCharacterisation
he kept the same attitude towards Italy
His hatred of Rome is unchanged by exile, as Nepos promised in chapter 1.
neque aliud quicquam egit quamEmphatic negative
he did nothing else but
The negative shows Hannibal's single-minded focus: everything he does at Prusias' court is aimed at Rome.
armavit et exercuitPairing
he armed and trained
Two verbs show Hannibal turning a king into a weapon against Rome, as if Prusias were one of his soldiers.
domesticis opibus minus esse robustumWord choice
was not strong enough in his own resources
Minus, "less", gently plays down the king's weakness, but the point is clear: Prusias cannot fight Rome alone.
conciliabat ceteros reges, adiungebat bellicosas nationesAsyndeton
he won over other kings, he joined warlike nations to him
Two matching clauses without a connecting word, both in the imperfect, show Hannibal's tireless diplomacy.
Romanis amicissimusSuperlative
a very close friend to the Romans
Eumenes is defined by his friendship with Rome, the opposite of Hannibal, so his conflict with Prusias becomes Hannibal's war with Rome by proxy.
et mari et terraPairing
both by sea and by land
The war is fought on every front, which raises the stakes.
propter Romanorum societatemEmphatic position
because of his alliance with the Romans
The reason for Eumenes' advantage comes last: behind every enemy Hannibal faces, Rome is the real power.
opprimiWord choice
to be crushed
A violent verb shows how far Hannibal is prepared to go.
quem si removisset, faciliora sibi cetera fore arbitrabaturConditional
he thought that if he removed him, everything else would be easier for him
The condition shows Hannibal's clear strategic reasoning: remove the key ally and the rest will follow.
ad hunc interficiundumGerundive
for killing him
The blunt gerundive of purpose, with its old-fashioned -und- spelling, states Hannibal's aim without softening: murder.
talem iniit rationemForeshadowing
he adopted the following plan
The phrase echoes capit tale consilium in the Cretan story, so the reader expects another clever trick.
Notes on the text 2
PrusiamPrusias
Prusias
Prusias I, king of Bithynia (about 228 to 182 BC), in north-west Asia Minor. Nepos says Pontus, loosely: Bithynia lay on the coast of the Black Sea, the Pontus Euxinus.
EumenesEumenes
Eumenes
Eumenes II, king of Pergamum (197 to 159 BC), Rome's loyal ally in Asia Minor, who gained much of Antiochus' former territory at the peace of Apamea and fought Prusias in the 180s BC.

The kingdoms of Asia Minor background

After the peace of Apamea (188 BC) Rome took no land in Asia Minor for itself but rewarded its friends. Eumenes II of Pergamum and the island state of Rhodes received most of the territory Antiochus gave up, and Pergamum became Rome's most important ally in the region.

Prusias I of Bithynia, in the north-west of Asia Minor on the coast of the Black Sea, was a rival of Pergamum, and the two kings fought over border lands in the 180s BC. For Hannibal, Prusias offered a last chance to strike at Rome through one of its allies.
