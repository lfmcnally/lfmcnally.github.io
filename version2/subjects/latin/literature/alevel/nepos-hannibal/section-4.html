Grammarex his refers to the three armies just mentioned.
Tricolonunum ... alterum ... tertium three armies, three matching clauses, one after another with no et joining them (asyndeton). It's a neat, businesslike way of showing Hannibal dividing up his forces with total control.
ContextAncient writers give huge (and probably exaggerated) numbers here: nearly 14,000 men to Africa, 12,000 foot soldiers and 21 elephants left in Spain, and (according to Livy) up to 90,000 soldiers plus cavalry and elephants taken to Italy.

Litotesneminem nisi victum dimisit 'he let no one go except a conquered one': stacking up two negatives (neminem, nisi) to make a strong positive point is litotes, and it makes Hannibal's total dominance sound even more complete than if Nepos had just said 'he conquered everyone'.
Alliterationneminem nisi there's also a hint of alliteration on n running through this clause, emphasising Hannibal's ruthlessness.
StyleThis whole sentence is a good example of hyperbole (exaggeration) — Nepos wants to hammer home how unstoppable Hannibal's march was, even though in reality some tribes must have simply avoided him rather than fighting to the death.

ContextTo complete his tenth labour, Hercules had to fetch the cattle of the monster Geryon from the far west, where Europe supposedly met North Africa; on the way he built the two mountains known as the Pillars of Hercules, either side of what's now the Strait of Gibraltar.
StyleComparing Hannibal to Hercules even if the comparison isn't strictly accurate (see the note below on Graius) — flatters Hannibal by putting him almost on the level of a demigod.

Grammarmuniit a word used specifically for building a proper military road so supplies could get through easily. effecit ut introduces a result clause.
Vocabularyornatus describes the elephant kitted out with a rider, luggage and a seat (which could carry two people), usually under a sun canopy.
Asyndetonconcidit, loca patefecit, itinera muniit, effecit four verbs fired off one after another with no et linking them. This asyndeton makes Hannibal's (or really his troops') hard work sound relentless, non-stop, almost machine-like.
Antithesiselephantus ornatus ... unus homo inermis vix a fully laden elephant set against one unarmed man barely managing to crawl: the contrast (with vix, 'scarcely', tipping it over the edge) exists purely to underline just how impressive this achievement was.

Grammarhac sums up all the efforts just described, used adverbially ('by this route/means').
ContextHannibal set out from New Carthage (modern Cartagena) and crossed roughly 900 miles on foot. Of his 38,000 troops, only around 20,000 made it to Italy, and he lost almost all his elephants; the cavalry suffered the fewest losses, only 2,000 of 8,000. Rome now had to divert its own troops to deal with him.
1
After Hamilcar died and Hasdrubal was appointed general in his place, Hannibal was put in charge of all the cavalry.
1–3
When Hasdrubal was also killed, the army conferred on him the highest office of power and when this news was brought back to Carthage, it was approved publicly.
3–5
So it was that when he was less than twenty-five years old, Hannibal was made commander; and within the next three years he subdued all the tribes of Spain through war,
5–6
took Saguntum by force, a town allied with Rome, and obtained three massive armies.
7–8
Of these armies he sent one to Africa, left the second with his brother Hasdrubal in Spain, and led the third with himself into Italy.
8–9
He crossed the pass over the Pyrenees; wherever he made his journey he fought with all the local people: he left nobody behind unless conquered.
10–11
After he came to the Alps which separate Italy from Gaul, which no one before him had ever crossed with an army except the Greek Hercules,

12–13
he opened up places, built roads, and made it possible for an elephant loaded with equipment to go to such places through which, before, a single unarmed man could barely crawl.
13–14
In this way, he led his forces across and came into Italy.
