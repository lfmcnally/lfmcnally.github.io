cuius post obitum, Hasdrubale imperatore suffecto … hoc quoque interfectoFast pace
after his death, with Hasdrubal appointed commander … when he too was killed
Two ablative absolutes dispose of two commanders in a few words, hurrying the story on to the moment Hannibal takes charge.
equitatui omni praefuitCharacterisation
he commanded all the cavalry
Even before he is in overall command, Hannibal is given a major responsibility, which marks him out early.
detulit … delatumPolyptoton
conferred … reported
The same verb is used for the army giving him command and the news being brought to Carthage, linking the soldiers' choice to the city's approval.
publice comprobatum estEmphasis
was officially approved
Nepos stresses that Hannibal's command was legitimate, approved by the state, not seized by force.
minor V et XX annis natusEmphasis
under twenty-five years old
His youth is spelt out precisely, so the reader is struck by how young he was to command a whole army.
omnes gentes HispaniaeHyperbole
every people of Spain
Nepos claims Hannibal conquered all of Spain in three years, an exaggeration that makes his speed and success seem astonishing.
omnes gentes Hispaniae bello subegit; Saguntum … vi expugnavit, tres exercitus maximos comparavitAsyndeton
he subdued every people of Spain; took Saguntum by storm; raised three huge armies
Three achievements follow one another without connecting words, which gives an impression of rapid, relentless activity.
Saguntum, foederatam civitatemContrast
Saguntum, a city in alliance with Rome
Foederatam reminds the reader that Saguntum was Rome's ally, so the attack is an act of war on Rome itself, told in a single matter-of-fact phrase.
unum in Africam misit, alterum cum Hasdrubale fratre in Hispania reliquit, tertium in Italiam secum duxitTricolon
one he sent to Africa, the second he left in Spain with his brother Hasdrubal, the third he led into Italy
The three armies are dealt out in three balanced clauses, and the climax is the one Hannibal leads himself.
neminem nisi victum dimisitEmphatic negative
he let no one go unless defeated
A strong negative with nisi leaves no exceptions: every people on the march was beaten.
quae Italiam ab Gallia seiunguntRelative clause
which separate Italy from Gaul
Nepos explains the Alps as a natural barrier, which builds up the scale of what Hannibal is about to do.
nemo umquam cum exercitu ante eum praeter Herculem GraiumComparison
no one ever with an army before him except Greek Hercules
Hannibal's only predecessor is a legendary hero, so his crossing is placed on the level of myth.
concidit; loca patefecit, itinera muniit, effecitAsyndeton
he cut them down; opened up the ground, built roads, made it possible
A string of short verbs with no connectives conveys the speed and energy of the work.
elephantus ornatus … unus homo inermisContrast
a fully equipped elephant … a single unarmed man
The huge, laden elephant is set against a lone man without even a weapon, which shows how completely Hannibal transformed the route.
vix poterat repereVivid detail
could barely crawl
Repere, "to crawl", paints the mountain path as almost impassable before Hannibal's engineers arrived.
hac copias traduxit in Italiamque pervenitFast pace
by this route he led his forces across and reached Italy
After the long description, the arrival is told in a few quick words, so the chapter ends on Hannibal in Italy.
Notes on the text 4
HasdrubaleHasdrubal
Hasdrubal
Hamilcar's son-in-law, who commanded in Spain from 229/8 BC until he was murdered in 221 BC. Not to be confused with Hannibal's brother Hasdrubal (unit 7).
SaguntumSaguntum
Saguntum
A city on the east coast of Spain, south of the Ebro, on friendly terms with Rome. Hannibal's siege of it in 219 BC led directly to the Second Punic War.
cum Hasdrubale fratreHasdrubal, Hannibal's brother
with his brother Hasdrubal
He held Spain for Carthage until 208 BC, then led an army into Italy to reinforce Hannibal, but was defeated and killed at the Metaurus in 207 BC.
Herculem GraiumHercules and the Alps
Greek Hercules
Legend said that Hercules had crossed the Alps on his way back from Spain with the cattle of Geryon. The name of the Graian Alps (the area of the Little St Bernard Pass) was linked with him, though it more likely comes from a local people.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Nepos covers nearly twenty years (from Hamilcar's death to the arrival in Italy) in a single chapter. What does he choose to dwell on, and why?
  2. How does Nepos make the crossing of the Alps seem a superhuman achievement? Pick out three techniques.
  3. Saguntum, the event that started the war, gets only three words. What does that tell you about Nepos' priorities as a biographer?
From Spain to Italy background

Hamilcar died in Spain in 229 or 228 BC, and command passed to his son-in-law Hasdrubal, who extended Carthaginian power and founded New Carthage (Cartagena). When Hasdrubal was murdered in 221 BC, the army chose Hannibal, and Carthage confirmed the choice.

In 219 BC Hannibal besieged Saguntum, a city on the east coast of Spain that had made friends with Rome. It fell after eight months, and Rome declared war. In 218 BC Hannibal marched out of Spain, over the Pyrenees, across southern Gaul and the Rhône, and through the Alps in late autumn, arriving in northern Italy with perhaps half the army he had started with and only a few of his elephants. Which pass he used is still argued about.
