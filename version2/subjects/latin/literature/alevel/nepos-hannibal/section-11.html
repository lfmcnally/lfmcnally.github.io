Grammarverens ne dederetur a verb of fearing (verens) followed by ne means you're afraid something will happen ('in case'); dederetur is imperfect subjunctive.
Grammaraccidisset pluperfect subjunctive, part of an 'unfulfilled' past condition: it didn't actually happen, but would have.
GrammarCretam ad Gortynios the Latin word order (Crete, then 'to the Gortynians') mirrors the fact that Hannibal reached Crete first, then made his way to the town of Gortyn on the south of the island.
ContextAntiochus had been defeated by the Romans at Magnesia in 190 BC, commanded by Lucius Scipio, brother of the famous Scipio Africanus (Lucius earned the nickname Scipio Asiaticus for it). The defeat cost Antiochus his kingdom, his army and his money — and part of the peace deal was exactly what Hannibal feared: handing Hannibal over.

Grammarinscientibus iis refers to the Gortynians; tolleret refers to Hannibal taking the jars; ne ... tolleret ... duceret is a negative purpose clause ('so that ... not').
StyleThis last line delivers the punchline of the whole story: the greedy Gortynians end up guarding empty lead jars as though they were treasure, while the real fortune sits out in the open, disguised as ordinary statues. It's one of the clearest examples in the whole Life of Nepos showing off Hannibal's cleverness rather than his generalship.
1–2
After Antiochus had been put to flight, Hannibal, fearing in case he would be handed over — which without doubt would have happened if he had let himself be caught — came to the Gortynians in Crete, to work out there where he should go.
3–4
But the shrewdest of all men saw that he would be in great danger, unless he made some plan in advance, on account of the greed of the Cretans.
4–5
For he was carrying a large sum of money with him, and he knew that news of this had got out.
6
And so he came up with the following plan.
6–7
He filled several jars with lead, and covered the tops with gold and silver.
7–8
These, with the leading citizens present, he deposited in the temple of Diana, pretending that he was entrusting his fortunes to their care.
8–9
After he had led them into this misbelief, he filled some bronze statues which he was carrying with him with all his money, and left them lying around in the courtyard of his house.
10–11
The Gortynians guarded the temple with great care, not so much against others as against Hannibal, so that he would not take the jars without their knowledge and carry them off with him.
