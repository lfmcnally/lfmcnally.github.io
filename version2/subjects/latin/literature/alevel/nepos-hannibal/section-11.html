Antiocho fugatoAblative absolute
Antiochus having been routed
The defeat of a great king is dismissed in two words, because Nepos's interest is Hannibal, not Antiochus.
verens ne dedereturCharacterisation
fearing that he would be handed over
Hannibal's fear of surrender, already seen in chapter 7, drives the rest of his life.
quod sine dubio accidissetAuthorial comment
which without doubt would have happened
Nepos steps in to confirm that the danger was real, so Hannibal's flight looks prudent rather than cowardly.
vir omnium callidissimusSuperlative
the cleverest man of all
The superlative introduces a trickster story. Callidus means shrewd but also cunning, a word that fits Roman ideas about "Punic" cleverness.
nisi quid providissetConditional
unless he took some precaution
The condition shows Hannibal thinking ahead, the quality that saves him in the trick that follows.
propter avaritiam CretensiumCharacterisation
because of the greed of the Cretans
The Cretans are defined by greed, so the reader enjoys seeing the greedy outwitted.
capit … complet … operitHistoric present
he takes … fills … covers
The switch to the present tense makes the trick vivid, as if we are watching Hannibal at work.
complet plumbo, summas operit auro et argentoAntithesis
fills them with lead, covers the tops with gold and silver
Worthless lead inside, precious metal on top: the whole trick is caught in one contrast.
praesentibus principibusAlliteration
in the presence of the leading men
The repeated p sound draws attention to the witnesses, who are the very people being fooled.
simulans se suas fortunas illorum fidei credereIrony
pretending to entrust his fortune to their good faith
Hannibal pretends to rely on fides, the honesty the Cretans lack, so the trick turns their own vice against them.
his in errorem inductisAblative absolute
with them led into error
The deception is finished in four words, and the story moves straight on to the real hiding place.
in propatulo domi abicitVivid detail
he throws them down in the open courtyard of his house
Abicit, "tosses aside", shows the real treasure left carelessly in full view, which is the cleverest part of the plan.
non tam a ceteris quam ab HannibaleAntithesis
not so much from others as from Hannibal
The temple is guarded against its depositor, a comic reversal that shows how completely the Cretans have been fooled.
ne ille inscientibus iis tolleret suaIrony
in case he took away his own property without their knowledge
The Cretans guard jars of lead, while Hannibal's real wealth lies in his courtyard. Sua, "his own things", adds to the joke.
tolleret sua secumque duceretPairing
should take away his own property and carry it off with him
Two verbs for removing the treasure show how anxiously the Cretans imagine Hannibal's escape.
Notes on the text 3
GortyniosGortyn
the people of Gortyn
Gortyn was one of the leading cities of central Crete, famous for its inscribed law code.
CretensiumCretans
of the Cretans
The Cretans had a proverbial reputation for greed and dishonesty: the line "Cretans are always liars" is attributed to the Cretan poet Epimenides.
templo DianaeDiana
the temple of Diana
The temple of Artemis (Diana to the Romans). Temples were often used as safe places to deposit money and valuables.

Why Hannibal had to run background

After Antiochus was defeated at Magnesia (190 or 189 BC), the peace terms dictated by Rome included the surrender of Hannibal. He escaped before he could be handed over. Crete, a collection of independent cities with a reputation for piracy and mercenary service, offered a temporary refuge outside the reach of both Rome and the Seleucid king.
