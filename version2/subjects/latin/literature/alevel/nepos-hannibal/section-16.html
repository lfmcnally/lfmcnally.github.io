The scene switches from Hannibal's victories in Asia to Rome, where his fate is about to be decided.

Nepos stresses chance: Hannibal's downfall begins with a careless remark at a dinner, not with a defeat in battle.

The scene is seen through the eyes of a slave boy at the door, which makes Hannibal's last moments small and personal.

Hannibal's calm, practical question shows a commander still assessing his position at the very end.
non fortuito factum, sed se petiAntithesis
it had not happened by chance, but he was the target
The story began with chance (casu) and ends with a deliberate hunt for Hannibal. He sees the truth at once.
neque sibi diutius vitam esse retinendamGerundive
that he could no longer keep his life
The gerundive presents his death as a conclusion he reaches calmly, not a moment of panic.
ne alieno arbitrio dimitteretCharacterisation

Hannibal chooses the manner of his own death, so even in defeat he keeps control.
memor pristinarum virtutumCharacterisation
mindful of his former courage
Nepos presents the suicide as an act worthy of his past, in keeping with the Roman ideal of a noble death.

The poison comes early in its clause and sumpsit is held to the very end, so the narrative of the Life closes on his final act.

The death of Hannibal background

Hannibal died at Libyssa in Bithynia, on the coast of the Sea of Marmara, in about 183 BC. The Roman embassy that came for him was led by Titus Quinctius Flamininus, the victor of Cynoscephalae, and Prusias, who could not afford to defy Rome, let it surround Hannibal's house.

The story was told by many ancient writers, and they differ in the details. Livy (39.51) gives Hannibal a scornful last speech about relieving the Romans of their long anxiety; Juvenal (Satire 10) says that the poison was kept in a ring. Plutarch (Flamininus 21) reports that many Romans thought Flamininus had acted cruelly in hounding an old and powerless exile to his death. Nepos keeps his version short and lets Hannibal die with dignity, by his own choice.
