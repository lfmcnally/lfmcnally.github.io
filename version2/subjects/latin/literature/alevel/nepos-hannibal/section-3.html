Grammarfeceris future perfect of facio; celaris is a syncopated future perfect for celaveris (celo takes a double accusative: the person kept in the dark and the thing hidden).
Grammarme ... principem principem is in apposition, 'me as leader'.
Litotesnon imprudenter literally 'not unwisely', a negative used to make a positive point more forcefully. This is litotes.
Balancesi...cogitabis...feceris, si...celaris; cum...bellum parabis, te...frustraberis, si non...posueris the sentence is carefully balanced between two options for Antiochus: peace (amice) or war (bellum), a wise choice (non imprudenter) or a bad one (frustraberis). It's Hannibal laying out an ultimatum with real rhetorical polish, even though he's supposedly speaking off the cuff.

ContextThe historian Livy actually didn't believe this whole oath story — he says fama est, 'there is a story', which is Latin for 'take this with a pinch of salt'.
1
'While this religious ceremony was being performed, he asked me whether I would like to set out with him for the camp.
1–3
When I had eagerly accepted this and had begun to beg him not to hesitate in taking me, he then said: 'I will do it, if you will give me the pledge that I ask.'
3–5
With that he led me to the altar on which he had begun to make the sacrifice, and after he had dismissed all the others, he ordered me, touching it, to swear that I would never be in friendship with the Romans.
6–7
I, up to my present time of life, have kept this oath given to my father so faithfully, that there should be no doubt for anyone that in the time remaining I shall be of the same mind.
8–9
Therefore, if you shall have friendly thoughts about the Romans, you will not be acting unwisely if you hide them from me; indeed, when you prepare for war, you will go against your own interests if you do not make me its general.'
9–10
Therefore at this age, which I have named, with his father, he set off into Spain.
