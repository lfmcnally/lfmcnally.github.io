Virgil and the Aeneid

Publius Vergilius Maro (70 to 19 BC) was born near Mantua in northern Italy. After the Eclogues and the Georgics he spent the last ten years of his life on the Aeneid, an epic in twelve books on the Trojan prince Aeneas, who escapes the fall of Troy and, guided by fate and the gods, sails west to found the people from whom Rome will come. He died before he had finished revising it, and Augustus had it published against his wishes.

The poem looks back to Homer (Books 1 to 6 are an Odyssey of wandering, Books 7 to 12 an Iliad of war) and forward to Augustus, who claimed descent from Aeneas’ son Iulus. Again and again it asks what Rome’s greatness costs, and Book 4, the story of Dido, is where that cost is felt most sharply.

Dido and Aeneas

Dido was a princess of Tyre in Phoenicia. When her brother Pygmalion murdered her husband Sychaeus for his wealth, she fled with a band of followers to Africa and founded Carthage. She vowed to stay faithful to Sychaeus’ memory and refused all suitors, among them the African king Iarbas.

In Book 1 a storm sent by Juno, who loves Carthage and hates the Trojans, drives Aeneas’ fleet onto the African coast. Venus, his mother, makes sure that Dido welcomes him, and has Cupid kindle love for him in her heart. At a banquet Aeneas tells the story of Troy’s fall and his wanderings (Books 2 and 3).

The first passage (lines 1 to 128) shows Dido’s love taking hold and the goddesses’ plan to unite the pair. Between the passages, they come together in a cave during a storm; Rumour spreads the news; Iarbas complains to Jupiter, who sends Mercury to order Aeneas to leave; Aeneas prepares in secret, and Dido confronts him. The second passage (lines 529 to 629) covers the last night before he sails, his departure and Dido’s curse. Book 4 ends with her death on the pyre.

Reading Virgil’s verse

The Aeneid is written in dactylic hexameter, six feet to a line, each a dactyl (long, short, short) or a spondee (long, long), with the fifth foot almost always a dactyl and the sixth two syllables. Virgil uses the rhythm to shape meaning: heavy spondees for weight, grief or effort, quick dactyls for speed or excitement.

  • Word order: words separated for effect (hyperbaton), framing, emphatic first and last positions in the line.
  • Enjambment: a sentence running over into the next line, with weight falling on the first word there.
  • Sound: alliteration, assonance, elision.
  • Imagery: above all the wound and the fire of Dido’s love, which run through the whole book.
  • Speech: rhetorical questions, apostrophe, exclamation.
Questions on the set text
  1. How far is Dido responsible for her own tragedy, and how far is she the victim of the gods?
  2. How does Virgil keep the reader’s sympathy with Dido, even when she is at her most furious?
  3. Trace the images of fire and wounding through the two passages. How do they change?
  4. What does the set text suggest about the cost of Aeneas’ mission to found Rome?
  5. How would an Augustan Roman reader have heard Dido’s curse at the end of the second passage?
