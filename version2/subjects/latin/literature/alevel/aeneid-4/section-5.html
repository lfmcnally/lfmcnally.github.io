First, they go from shrine to shrine, seeking peace at every altar; they sacrifice, as custom demands, chosen sheep to Ceres the lawgiver, to Phoebus, to father Bacchus, and above all to Juno, who watches over the bonds of marriage.

62aut ante ora deum pingues spatiatur ad aras,

Dido herself, loveliest of all, holds the offering-dish in her right hand and pours it between the horns of a gleaming white cow; or walks before the faces of the gods to the altars heaped with offerings, and begins the day afresh with gifts; and, gazing into the opened breasts of the victims, searches their still-breathing entrails for a sign.

66quid delubra iuvant? est molles flamma medullas
67interea et tacitum vivit sub pectore vulnus.
Ah, how little the minds of seers know! What good are prayers, what good are shrines, to a woman already mad with love? All the while the flame is eating at her soft marrow, and the silent wound lives on beneath her heart.

Notes on the text 1
58
legiferae Cereri Phoeboque patrique LyaeoCeres, Phoebus and Lyaeus
to law-giving Ceres, to Phoebus and to father Lyaeus
Ceres was linked with law and settled civic life; Phoebus is Apollo; Lyaeus, "the releaser", is a title of Bacchus. The gods of a city's foundation and of marriage.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. What is the purpose of the sacrifices in lines 56–64? What do they tell us about Dido's state of mind?
  2. Virgil comments directly in line 65: "heu, vatum ignarae mentes!". What does he mean, and what is the effect of the narrator's voice breaking in?
  3. How do lines 66–67 bring together the fire and wound imagery from the start of the book?
Sacrifice and reading the signs background

Romans and Greeks asked the gods' favour before any important step, and marriage was one. Animals were sacrificed, and their entrails, especially the liver, were inspected by a haruspex for signs of the gods' will. A healthy, unblemished liver was a good omen; a deformed one meant the gods were against the plan.

Dido offers to Ceres, who was linked with laws and with marriage, to Apollo, to Bacchus and above all to Juno, goddess of marriage. She is doing everything correctly, which makes the narrator's comment all the more chilling: no ritual can help a woman whose mind is already lost.
