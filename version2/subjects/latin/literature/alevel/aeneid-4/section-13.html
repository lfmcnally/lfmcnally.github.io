Notes on the text 1
585
TithoniTithonus
of Tithonus
A Trojan prince loved by Aurora, the Dawn. She won him immortality but forgot to ask for eternal youth, so he grew ever older in her bed. His being Trojan is a quiet irony here.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Lines 579–583 describe the Trojans' departure in a rush of verbs. How does Virgil's language make the scene feel hurried, and what does that suggest about Aeneas' state of mind?
  2. The dawn description (lines 584–585) is calm and beautiful. Why place it immediately before Dido's discovery?
  3. Dido's first words on seeing the empty harbour mix grief, outrage and orders. Which do you think dominates, and how can you tell from the Latin?
Dawn over Carthage background

Virgil sets the departure and its discovery at first light. The same formula of Dawn rising from Tithonus' bed is used elsewhere in epic, so the moment has a grand, almost ceremonial quality, which contrasts with the panic on the shore and the fury on the tower.

Dido watches from her palace, as she has watched before: earlier in the book she looked out on the Trojans preparing their ships (lines 408–411). Now the harbour is empty. Her call for weapons and fire is the last moment at which she considers action against the Trojans; from line 595 she turns back on herself.
