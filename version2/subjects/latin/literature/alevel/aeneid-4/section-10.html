533sic adeo insistit secumque ita corde volutat:
But not so the Phoenician queen, unhappy at heart: she is never released into sleep, or lets the night into her eyes or her heart. Her anxieties redouble, and her love, rising up again, rages and surges on a great swell of anger. So she persists, and thus turns things over within herself in her heart:
534en, quid ago? rursusne procos inrisa priores
535experiar, Nomadumque petam conubia supplex,
536quos ego sim totiens iam dedignata maritos?
537Iliacas igitur classes atque ultima Teucrum
538iussa sequar? quiane auxilio iuvat ante levatos
539et bene apud memores veteris stat gratia facti?
'Well then, what am I to do? Am I, a laughing-stock, to try my old suitors again, and beg as a suppliant for marriage with the Numidians, men I have already refused so many times as husbands? Or should I follow the Trojan fleet instead, and obey the Trojans' every last order? Is it because they are glad to have been helped by my aid before, and gratitude for that old kindness stands firm among men who remember it so well?
540quis me autem, fac velle, sinet ratibusve superbis
541invisam accipiet? nescis heu, perdita, necdum
542Laomedonteae sentis periuria gentis?
543quid tum? sola fuga nautas comitabor ovantes?
But, even if I wanted to, who would let me? Who would take a woman they despise aboard their proud ships? Don't you know, alas, lost woman? Don't you see yet the broken oaths of Laomedon's race? What then? Should I run off alone, and join their crews as they sail away rejoicing?
544an Tyriis omnique manu stipata meorum
545inferar et, quos Sidonia vix urbe revelli,
546rursus agam pelago et ventis dare vela iubebo?'
Or should I go after them, surrounded by the Tyrians and the whole band of my people, and drive out to sea again those I could hardly tear away from the Sidonian city, and order them to spread their sails to the winds?'

but not the unhappy Phoenician queen
The opening at non sets Dido apart from the sleeping world described just before: everyone else rests, but she does not.
529
infelixEpithet
unhappy
infelix returns from line 68: the doomed queen of the first half of the book is now near her end.
530–531
solvitur in somnos oculisve aut pectore noctem accipitTricolon
is released into sleep, or takes the night into her eyes or heart
Sleep is denied three ways, in her body, her eyes and her heart, so the sleeplessness seems total.
531
ingeminant curaeEmphasis
her cares redouble
ingeminant, "they double", shows her anxieties multiplying rather than calming in the night.
531–532
resurgens saevit amorContrast
love rises again and rages
Love itself "rages" (saevit), a verb used of wild animals and storms, so love and anger have become one.
532
magnoque irarum fluctuat aestuSea imagery
she surges on a great tide of anger
Dido's emotions are a stormy sea, an image that suits a story of ships and sailing. Her love has turned into rage.
534
en, quid ago?Deliberative question
well, what am I to do?
The present tense ago, where a subjunctive might be expected, makes the question sudden and urgent, as if she is speaking her thoughts aloud.
534–535
rursusne procos inrisa priores experiarPathos
am I to try my former suitors again, a laughing stock?
inrisa, "laughed at", shows how much Dido fears humiliation. She once rejected these men, and now pictures them mocking her.
535
supplexCharacterisation
as a suppliant
The proud queen imagines herself begging: supplex shows how far she has fallen.
538
quiane auxilio iuvat ante levatosSarcasm
because, of course, they are glad to have been helped before?
Dido's bitter irony shows her sense of betrayal: the Trojans she rescued are repaying her with desertion.
539
bene apud memores veteris stat gratia factiIrony
gratitude for an old kindness stands firm among those who remember it
The sarcasm continues: Dido means the opposite, that the Trojans have no gratitude at all.
540
quis me autem, fac velle, sinetRhetorical question
but who, suppose I wanted to, would allow me?
Even the option of following Aeneas is closed off, as Dido answers her own question.
541
nescis heu, perditaSelf-address
don't you know, alas, ruined woman?
Dido turns on herself as perdita, "ruined" or "lost". The self-address shows a mind divided against itself.
542
Laomedonteae sentis periuria gentisAllusion
the perjuries of Laomedon's race
Dido brands all Trojans as liars, recalling the king of Troy who cheated the gods. The heavy, five-syllable adjective gives the insult weight.
543
sola fuga nautas comitabor ovantesPathos
shall I go alone in flight with the rejoicing sailors?
sola is set against the crowd of cheering sailors, so the picture of Dido alone among the joyful Trojans is especially painful.
545
quos Sidonia vix urbe revelliEmphasis
whom I barely tore away from the city of Sidon
revelli, "tore away", recalls Dido's flight from Tyre after her brother killed her husband: she cannot ask her people to uproot themselves again.
546
pelago et ventisPairing
to the sea and the winds
Sea and wind recall the dangers the Tyrians already faced. The pairing makes the idea of another voyage feel exhausting and impossible.

535
NomadumqueThe Nomads

The Numidians, the native peoples of north Africa around Carthage, among them Iarbas, whose offers of marriage Dido had refused.

An early king of Troy who refused to pay Apollo and Neptune for building the city's walls, and later cheated Hercules. His broken promises became a byword for Trojan treachery.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Dido's speech is a series of questions (534–546). What options does she consider, and why does she reject each one?
  2. How does Virgil contrast the peace of the night (the lines just before this section) with Dido's state of mind?
  3. Dido calls herself "perdita" (541). How far has she come from the queen we saw building Carthage in Book 1?
Between line 128 and line 529 background

The set text jumps from the goddesses' plan to the end of Dido's story. In between, Dido and Aeneas meet in the cave and live openly as a couple, and Rumour spreads the news. The African king Iarbas, a rejected suitor, complains to Jupiter, who sends Mercury to remind Aeneas of his destiny in Italy. Aeneas prepares to leave secretly; Dido finds out and confronts him, and the two speeches that follow are among the most famous in Latin literature. He insists that he goes to Italy not of his own will. Dido, in despair, builds a pyre, pretending that she means to burn Aeneas' belongings in a magic rite.

Section 10 opens on the last night before the Trojans sail. Everything else in the world sleeps, but Dido cannot.
