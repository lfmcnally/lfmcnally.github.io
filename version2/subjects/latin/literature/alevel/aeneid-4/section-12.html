Then indeed Aeneas, terrified by the sudden vision, tore himself from sleep and drove his men to headlong haste: 'Wake up, men, and take your places on the rowing benches; quickly, loose the sails. A god sent down from high heaven, look, is spurring us on again to hasten our flight and cut the twisted ropes. 560
nate deaEpithet
son of a goddess
Mercury reminds Aeneas of his divine mother, Venus, and so of the destiny that goes with his birth. The address is respectful and pointed at once.
560
potes hoc sub casu ducere somnosRhetorical question
can you sleep at a time like this?
The opening question is a rebuke, not a request for information. It makes Aeneas' calm sleep look like dangerous carelessness.
560–562
potes … nec … cernis … nec … audisTricolon
can you … don't you see … don't you hear
Three questions in a row build pressure on Aeneas, each pointing to something he is failing to notice.
562
demensInsult
madman
Mercury calls the hero "out of his mind". The word usually describes Dido's frenzy, so its use for Aeneas is startling and urgent.
563
illa dolos dirumque nefas in pectore versatCharacterisation
she is turning over treachery and dreadful crime in her heart
Mercury presents Dido as a plotter. The pronoun illa keeps her at a distance, which makes her seem a threat rather than a person in pain.
564
variosque irarum concitat aestusMetaphor
and stirs up shifting tides of anger
Dido's anger is pictured as a stormy sea, which suits the voyage ahead and suggests something too powerful to control.
565
non fugis hinc praecepsRhetorical question
won't you flee from here headlong?
Another question as a command: fugis, flee, is a blunt word for the departure of a hero, which adds to the urgency.
565
praeceps, dum praecipitarePolyptoton
headlong … to rush headlong
The same root repeated in different forms, with the hard p and c sounds, drives home the need for speed.
566–567
iam mare turbari … iam fervere litoraAnaphora
soon the sea churned … soon the shore blazing
The repeated iam paints a sequence of horrors as if they are already happening, so the threat feels immediate.
566–567
saevasque videbis conlucere facesVivid detail
you will see fierce torches blazing
The image of fire looks forward both to Dido's pyre and, in the curse, to the wars of Carthage and Rome.
569
heia age, rumpe morasInterjection
come on, break off delay
Short, clipped words in the language of everyday urging. The rhythm itself hurries Aeneas on.
569–570
varium et mutabile semper feminaGeneralisation
a woman is always a fickle and changeable thing
The neuter adjectives make "woman" a thing rather than a person. Mercury dismisses Dido with a general sneer, and the line has drawn criticism from readers ever since.
569–570
semper feminaEnjambment

femina is held over to the start of line 570 and stands alone, so the scornful judgement lands with full weight.
570

The god vanishes into darkness, which leaves the scene ominous and gives Aeneas' fear a sudden cause.
572
corripit … fatigatHistoric present

Present tenses make Aeneas' reaction quick and vivid: he moves the moment he wakes.
573–574
vigilate … considite … solviteImperatives
wake up … take your seats … unfurl
A string of commands shows Aeneas taking control of his men, and the short phrases match the haste of the moment.
576–577

again … again
Aeneas stresses that this is the second divine command, which presents his departure as obedience to the gods, not desertion.
576–577

Aeneas prays to the god even without knowing exactly who he is. The prayer shows his pietas, his dutiful obedience to divine will.

Questions to think about · 3
  1. Mercury's speech is full of questions, commands and threats. How does Virgil make it feel urgent, and does it change the way we judge Aeneas' departure?
  2. "varium et mutabile semper femina." Is this Virgil's view, Mercury's, or a rhetorical tactic aimed at Aeneas? How should a modern reader respond to it?
  3. How does Aeneas' speech to his men (lines 573–579) show him as a leader and as a pious hero? Is anything missing from it?
The second warning background

Mercury's first visit (4.265–276) was a formal embassy from Jupiter: Aeneas was to remember Italy and his son's future. Aeneas obeyed, but tried to leave quietly, and Dido discovered his preparations. This second visit comes in a dream, and its message is practical as well as divine: Dido is dangerous, the wind is right, and every hour's delay risks an attack at dawn.

Virgil leaves open whether the threat is real. Dido does briefly imagine burning the Trojan ships (lines 592–594 and 604–605), but by then she has already resolved to die.
