15si mihi non animo fixum immotumque sederet
16ne cui me vinclo vellem sociare iugali,
17postquam primus amor deceptam morte fefellit;
18si non pertaesum thalami taedaeque fuisset,
19huic uni forsan potui succumbere culpae.
'If it were not fixed and immovable in my mind that I would not wish to join myself to anyone again in the bond of marriage, ever since my first love cheated and deceived me by his death; if I had not come to hate the marriage bed and the torch, perhaps I could have given way to this one fault.
20Anna, fatebor enim, miseri post fata Sychaei
21coniugis et sparsos fraterna caede penates
22solus hic inflexit sensus animumque labantem
23impulit. agnosco veteris vestigia flammae.
Anna — I will admit it — since the death of my poor husband Sychaeus, since our household gods were spattered with blood when my brother murdered him, this man alone has moved my feelings, and stirred my wavering heart. I know the signs of the old flame.
24sed mihi vel tellus optem prius ima dehiscat
25vel pater omnipotens adigat me fulmine ad umbras,
26pallentes umbras Erebi noctemque profundam,
27ante, pudor, quam te violo aut tua iura resolvo.
But I would sooner pray that the earth gape open to its depths for me, or that the almighty father drive me down with his thunderbolt to the shadows, the pale shadows of Erebus, and deep night, before I wrong you, Honour, or break your laws.
28ille meos, primus qui me sibi iunxit, amores
29abstulit; ille habeat secum servetque sepulcro.'
30sic effata sinum lacrimis implevit obortis.
That man, who first joined me to himself, has taken my love away; let him keep it with him and guard it in his grave.' So she spoke, and filled her lap with welling tears.

15–19
si … sederet … si non pertaesum … fuisset … potuiConditional
if it were not … if I had not … I could have
Two "if not" clauses hold back the main verb. Dido builds her defences first, then admits in a single word, potui, that she could have given in.
15
fixum immotumquePairing
fixed and unmoved
Two near-synonyms stress how firm her resolve is, or how firm she wants it to sound. The reader soon sees it shaken.
16
vinclo … iugaliWord choice
the bond of marriage
Marriage is described as a chain or yoke. Dido presents it as something binding, which is what she fears and desires at once.
17
primus amor deceptam morte fefellitPersonification
my first love cheated and deceived me by dying
Her first love is spoken of as a person who tricked her. deceptam … fefellit doubles the idea of betrayal, a theme that will return with Aeneas.
18
thalami taedaequeAlliteration
the marriage chamber and the torch
The repeated t sounds stress her weariness of everything to do with marriage; the torch is the wedding torch that led the bride to her new home.
19
huic uni … culpaeEuphemism
to this one fault
Dido calls her love a culpa, a fault or sin, before anything has happened. huic uni tries to make it small: just this one failing.
19
succumbereEmphasis
to give way
A verb of physical collapse, "to sink beneath". Love is pictured as a force that overpowers her.
20–21
miseri … Sychaei coniugisPathos
of my poor husband Sychaeus
miseri shows her continuing pity and love for Sychaeus, which makes her new feelings all the more painful to admit.
21
sparsos fraterna caede penatesVivid detail
the household gods spattered with a brother's bloodshed
The murder is pictured through the blood on the family gods: a crime against the home itself, committed by her own brother.
22
solus hicEmphasis
this man alone
solus stresses that only Aeneas has moved her, which makes her confession more complete and more dangerous.
22–23
animumque labantem impulitMetaphor
he has pushed my tottering heart
Her heart is pictured as a wall already slipping, and Aeneas has given it the push. The run-on to impulit enacts the push.
23
agnosco veteris vestigia flammaeFire imagery
I recognise the traces of the old flame
Her love for Sychaeus was a flame; now she feels its marks again. The famous line is a moment of painful self-knowledge.
24–25
vel tellus … dehiscat vel pater omnipotens adigatPairing
either may the earth gape … or the almighty father drive
Two terrible alternatives, one from below and one from above, show how strongly she means her oath.
25–26
ad umbras, pallentes umbrasRepetition
to the shades, the pale shades
umbras is picked up and expanded at once, so the reader lingers in the darkness of the underworld, where Dido will end the book.
27
ante, pudor, quam te violoTragic irony
before I violate you, Honour
Dido swears she would rather die than break her vow. She will break it, and she will die: the oath is a curse on herself.
27
pudorApostrophe
Honour
Dido turns and speaks directly to her own sense of honour as if it were a goddess. Breaking faith with it becomes a kind of sacrilege.
28–29
ille … illeRepetition
that man … that man
ille at the start of both clauses points emphatically to Sychaeus. She tries to hand her love back to him in his grave.
30
sinum lacrimis implevit obortisPathos
she filled her lap with welling tears
Her speech ends not in resolve but in tears, which shows how weak her defences really are.

Questions to think about · 3
  1. Dido gives two reasons for resisting her feelings: her vow and her pudor. How convincing does she find them herself?
  2. Compare the oath in lines 24–27 with what happens at the end of Book 4. What does Virgil gain by having Dido call down her own destruction here?
  3. "agnosco veteris vestigia flammae" (line 23). Why is this line so powerful, and what does it reveal about Dido?
The univira background

Romans especially admired the univira, a woman who had only ever had one husband and did not remarry after his death. It was an ideal more than a rule, and remarriage was common in practice, but loyalty to a dead husband was praised on tombstones and in literature.

Dido has vowed to stay faithful to Sychaeus. Her pudor is more than modesty: it is her sense of honour and of what she owes to him and to her own good name. Breaking that vow is the step that the whole tragedy turns on, and she knows it.
