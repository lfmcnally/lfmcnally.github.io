So much for the pledged hand and good faith of the man who, they say, carries his ancestral household gods with him, who carried his father, worn out with age, on his own shoulders!

Whom did I have to fear, since I was going to die? I would have carried torches into his camp, filled the decks with flame, wiped out son and father together with their whole line, and thrown myself on top of it all.

Sun, whose fires look down on every deed done on this earth; Juno, witness and go-between of these torments of mine; Hecate, howled to at midnight crossroads in every city; you avenging Furies, and gods of dying Elissa: hear this, turn on these wrongs the divine power they deserve, and listen to my prayers.'

what am I saying? where am I? what madness changes my mind?
Three short questions in a row show Dido coming to her senses with a jolt, aware that her orders are useless.
596
infelix DidoApostrophe

Dido speaks to herself in the third person, as if she were watching her own tragedy from outside. Infelix is the word most often used of her in the poem.
596
facta impiaWord choice
impious deeds
Impia is pointed: Aeneas is famous for pietas, and Dido now calls his conduct its opposite. (The phrase could also refer to her own deeds, the broken vow to Sychaeus, which makes it more painful still.)
597

then was the time, when you were handing over your sceptre
The past tenses look back to her mistake: she should have been wary when she shared her kingdom with him, not now.
597

so much for his right hand and his good faith
Sarcastic en: the right hand was the pledge of a promise, and Dido mocks the trust she placed in it.
598–599

The repeated quem lists the famous proofs of Aeneas' piety, carrying the household gods and his father, only to mock them.

One word turns praise into hearsay: Dido now doubts the stories she once found so moving.

The gerundive conjures the most horrifying revenge of myth, a son served to his father, which shows Dido's thoughts at their darkest.

The past unreal subjunctives describe what she could have done, and the fire imagery anticipates her own pyre.

Questions to think about · 3
  1. Dido moves from confusion (line 595) to fantasies of revenge (lines 600–606) to prayer (lines 607–612). How does Virgil mark each change of mood in the Latin?
  2. Dido mocks the reports of Aeneas' piety in lines 597–599. Is her scorn justified? How does this passage test the idea of Aeneas as pius?
  3. The revenge Dido imagines in lines 600–602 recalls monstrous myths such as Atreus and Thyestes. Why might Virgil give her such horrific thoughts at this point?
Dido's gods background

Dido ends this section with a formal prayer, calling on the powers who see and punish wrongdoing. Sol, the Sun, sees everything on earth; Juno, goddess of marriage, presided over her "wedding" in the cave and is Carthage's protector; Hecate, goddess of crossroads, magic and the underworld, is linked with the rites Dido has been preparing; the Dirae, or Furies, avenge wrongs, especially broken oaths and crimes against family.

The prayer is the first half of a curse. Its second half, in the next section, asks these gods for specific punishments on Aeneas and on his descendants.
