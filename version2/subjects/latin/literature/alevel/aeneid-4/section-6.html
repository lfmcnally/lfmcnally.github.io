68uritur infelix Dido totaque vagatur
69urbe furens, qualis coniecta cerva sagitta,
70quam procul incautam nemora inter Cresia fixit
71pastor agens telis liquitque volatile ferrum
72nescius: illa fuga silvas saltusque peragrat
73Dictaeos; haeret lateri letalis harundo.
Unhappy Dido burns, and wanders in her madness through the whole city — like a deer struck by an arrow, which some shepherd, hunting among the Cretan woods, has caught off guard and pierced from a distance, leaving the flying shaft in her without knowing it; she runs in flight through the forests and glades of Dicte, the deadly arrow still fixed in her side.
74nunc media Aenean secum per moenia ducit
75Sidoniasque ostentat opes urbemque paratam,
76incipit effari mediaque in voce resistit;
77nunc eadem labente die convivia quaerit,
78Iliacosque iterum demens audire labores
79exposcit pendetque iterum narrantis ab ore.
One moment Dido leads Aeneas through the heart of the city, showing him her Phoenician wealth and the city already built; she begins to speak, and stops halfway through the words. The next, as the day fades, she calls for the same feast all over again, and in her madness begs to hear once more the sufferings of Troy, and hangs once more on the speaker's every word.
80post ubi digressi, lumenque obscura vicissim
81luna premit suadentque cadentia sidera somnos,
82sola domo maeret vacua stratisque relictis
83incubat. illum absens absentem auditque videtque,
84aut gremio Ascanium genitoris imagine capta
85detinet, infandum si fallere possit amorem.
Later, once they have parted, and the fading moon in turn dims her light and the setting stars urge sleep, she lies grieving, alone in the empty house, stretched out on the couch he has left behind. Lost within herself, she hears him and sees him, absent though he is; or takes Ascanius onto her lap, caught by his likeness to his father, and keeps him there, hoping she might somehow deceive a love she cannot speak of.
