Unhappy Dido burns, and wanders in her madness through the whole city — like a deer struck by an arrow, which some shepherd, hunting among the Cretan woods, has caught off guard and pierced from a distance, leaving the flying shaft in her without knowing it; she runs in flight through the forests and glades of Dicte, the deadly arrow still fixed in her side.

One moment Dido leads Aeneas through the heart of the city, showing him her Phoenician wealth and the city already built; she begins to speak, and stops in mid-sentence. The next, as the day fades, she calls for the same feast all over again, and in her madness begs to hear once more the sufferings of Troy, and hangs once more on the speaker's every word.

Later, once they have parted, and the fading moon in turn dims her light and the setting stars urge sleep, she lies grieving, alone in the empty house, stretched out on the couch he has left behind. Apart from him, she hears him and sees him, though he is gone; or takes Ascanius onto her lap, caught by his likeness to his father, and keeps him there, hoping she might somehow deceive a love she cannot speak of.

Dido clings to the child because he looks like Aeneas. Readers of Book 1 know that Cupid took Ascanius' form there, so the scene is doubly charged.

Notes on the text 2
70
nemora inter CresiaCrete and Dicte
among the Cretan woods
Crete was famous for its archers and hunters. Mount Dicte, in the east of the island, was said to be where Zeus was hidden as a baby.
84
AscaniumAscanius
Ascanius
Aeneas' young son, also called Iulus, from whom the Julian family (and so Julius Caesar and Augustus) claimed descent. In Book 1 Venus had Cupid take his place to make Dido fall in love.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. The simile of the wounded deer (69–73) is full of details: the shepherd who does not know he has hit her, the arrow that stays in her side. How far does each detail match Dido's situation, and who is the "shepherd"?
  2. Lines 74–79 move between "nunc … nunc". What does this pattern suggest about Dido's state of mind, and about how she is spending her days?
  3. How does Virgil show that Dido's love is already damaging her role as queen, even before the next section tells us so directly?
The wounded deer background

Virgil sets the simile in Crete, famous for its archers and hunters, and in the woods of Mount Dicte. The details are chosen with care. The shepherd shoots from a distance and never learns that he has hit anything: Aeneas has done Dido harm without meaning to and without knowing it. The deer runs, but cannot escape the arrow fixed in her side, just as Dido's restless wandering through the city carries her wound with her.

The simile also looks back to Book 1, where Venus appeared to Aeneas dressed as a huntress, and forward to the hunt of lines 117–128, where Dido rides out to the hunt that will lead to the cave.
