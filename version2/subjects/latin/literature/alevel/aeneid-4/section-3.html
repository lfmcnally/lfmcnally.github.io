31Anna refert: 'o luce magis dilecta sorori,
32solane perpetua maerens carpere iuventa

34id cinerem aut manes credis curare sepultos?
Anna answered: 'O you who are dearer to your sister than the light itself, will you waste your whole youth alone, grieving, and never know sweet children, or the rewards of love? Do you really think the dust and ashes of the dead care about that?
35esto: aegram nulli quondam flexere mariti,
36non Libyae, non ante Tyro; despectus Iarbas
37ductoresque alii, quos Africa terra triumphis
38dives alit: placitone etiam pugnabis amori?
Very well — granted that, until now, no suitor has moved you, not in Libya, not before that in Tyre; that you turned down Iarbas, and the other chieftains that Africa, rich in triumphs, breeds. But will you fight against a love that pleases you too?
39nec venit in mentem quorum consederis arvis?
40hinc Gaetulae urbes, genus insuperabile bello,
41et Numidae infreni cingunt et inhospita Syrtis;
42hinc deserta siti regio lateque furentes
43Barcaei. quid bella Tyro surgentia dicam
44germanique minas?'
Have you forgotten whose lands you have settled in? On one side, the cities of the Gaetulians hem you in — a people unconquerable in war — with the unbridled Numidians, and the pitiless Syrtis; on the other, a land parched with drought, and the Barcaeans, raging far and wide. Why should I even mention the war brewing from Tyre, and the threats from your own brother?'

Anna brushes aside the dead Sychaeus as mere dust. The tone is blunt and practical, the opposite of Dido's devotion.

One word sums up Iarbas' humiliation. The rejected suitor will return later in the book as a dangerous enemy.
38

The key question: resisting unwanted suitors was easy, but this love is welcome. pugnabis makes it sound like a pointless war against herself.

Literally "without bridles": the Numidians famously rode without bridles, and the word also makes them sound wild and lawless.

Notes on the text 3
36
IarbasIarbas
Iarbas
A North African king, said to be a son of Jupiter Ammon, who sold Dido the land for Carthage and wanted to marry her. Her refusal makes him an enemy.
40–41
Gaetulae … NumidaeGaetulians and Numidians
Gaetulian … Numidians
Peoples of the North African interior. The Numidians were famous horsemen, later allies and then enemies of Rome.
41–43
Syrtis … BarcaeiSyrtis and the Barcaeans
the Syrtis … the Barcaeans
The Syrtes were shallow, shifting sandbanks off the Libyan coast, feared by sailors. Barce was a city in Cyrenaica, to the east.
Questions to think about · 3

  3. How does Virgil use the list of Carthage's enemies in lines 40–44 to make an argument for the marriage?
Anna as adviser background

Anna plays the part that a nurse or confidante plays in Greek tragedy: the loving helper whose advice, given in good faith, leads the heroine to ruin. Her speech is a skilful piece of persuasion, mixing emotional appeals (youth, children, love) with hard political ones (enemies on every side).

The peoples she names were real neighbours of Carthage in Virgil's day: the Gaetulians and Numidians of the interior, the dangerous sandbanks of the Syrtes off the coast, and the people of Barce in Cyrenaica. Rome had fought long wars in North Africa, and Virgil's readers would know these names.
