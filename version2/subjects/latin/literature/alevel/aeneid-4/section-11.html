'No — die, as you deserve, and put an end to your grief with the sword. You, sister, gave way to my tears, and it was you who first loaded me down, mad as I already was, with all this suffering, and threw me to my enemy. I was not allowed to live out my life, innocent, untouched by marriage, the way a wild creature does, free of troubles like these. The vow I made to Sychaeus's ashes — I have not kept it.'

547
quin morere ut merita esImperative
no, die as you deserve
Dido gives herself an order, as if she were her own judge passing sentence. The abrupt imperative cuts off all the alternatives she has just considered.
547
ut merita esCharacterisation
as you deserve
Dido believes she has earned death. Her guilt over Sychaeus has turned into self-condemnation, so the reader sees how completely she now blames herself.
547
ferroque averte doloremEuphemism
and turn away your grief with the sword
Suicide is described as a cure for pain, which shows that Dido now sees death as a release rather than a punishment.
548
tu lacrimis evicta meis, tu primaAnaphora
you, overcome by my tears, you first
The repeated tu turns Dido's anger on Anna. The accusation is unfair, since Dido herself wanted Anna's advice, which shows a mind looking for someone to blame.
548
furentemEmphatic position
raging
The participle stands at the end of line 548, so Dido's madness is the last word before the burden of suffering is placed on her.
549
germanaApostrophe

The affectionate word sits in the middle of an accusation, which shows Dido torn between love for Anna and resentment.
549

Two verbs of violence present Anna as an attacker. Calling Aeneas hosti, an enemy, shows how far Dido's feelings have turned.
550–552

Two sentences open with non, setting out what Dido has lost: an innocent single life, and her loyalty to her dead husband.
551

in the manner of a wild creature
Dido envies the freedom of a wild animal, which lives without marriage or guilt. The image recalls the wounded deer of lines 68–73 and suggests a life outside human ties.
552
SychaeoEmphatic position

Dido's speech ends on her dead husband's name, so the reader is left with the vow she has broken.
553

Virgil stresses the size of her grief, and rumpebat, burst out, makes it sound violent, as if her heart could not hold it.
554–555
Aeneas celsa in puppi iam certus eundi carpebat somnosContrast
Aeneas, high on the stern, now set on leaving, was enjoying his sleep
The scene cuts straight from Dido's sleepless despair to Aeneas asleep and calm. The contrast makes his peace of mind seem cold beside her torment.
554–555
iam … iamRepetition

The repeated iam stresses that Aeneas is ready and his mind is made up, while Dido is still struggling.
555
rite paratisCharacterisation
duly prepared
Rite suggests that everything has been done properly, even religiously. Aeneas is shown as orderly and dutiful, which sharpens the difference between him and the disordered Dido.
558–559
vocemque coloremque et crines flavos et membra decoraPolysyndeton

The list joined by -que and et lingers on the god's appearance item by item, which gives the dream vision a vivid, solid presence.
558
vocemque coloremqueMetre
his voice and his colouring
The double -que makes line 558 hypermetric: its final syllable elides into the next line. The line seems to spill over, much as the vision floods into Aeneas' sleep.
Notes on the text 2
552
cineri promissa SychaeoSychaeus
promised to the ashes of Sychaeus
Dido's husband at Tyre, murdered for his wealth by her brother Pygmalion. After his death she vowed never to remarry (4.24–29), and her shame at breaking that vow runs through Book 4.
558
MercurioMercury
to Mercury
The messenger god, son of Jupiter. His first visit (4.265–276) ordered Aeneas to leave Carthage; this dream repeats the command with fresh urgency.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Dido blames herself, Anna and her broken vow to Sychaeus in quick succession. Whose fault does she finally think her suffering is, and how far does Virgil invite us to agree?
  2. Compare the sleeping Aeneas in lines 554–555 with the sleepless Dido of the lines before. What effect does Virgil achieve by setting the two scenes side by side?
  3. Why do you think Mercury appears to Aeneas a second time, and in a dream?
Dido's night background

This passage closes the long night that begins at line 522, where Virgil contrasts the calm of the whole sleeping world with Dido, who cannot rest. Her monologue (lines 534–552) moves through every option left to her: to go back to her old suitors, to follow the Trojans, to sail after them with her people. Each is rejected, and she ends where she began, with death.

Aeneas, by contrast, has already decided. He has been visited once by Mercury (lines 265–276), sent by Jupiter to remind him of Italy and of Ascanius' future. Now the god appears again, this time in a dream, to tell him that the moment to leave is now.
