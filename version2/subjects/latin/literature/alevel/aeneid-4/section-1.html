But the queen, long since wounded by a deep anxiety, feeds the wound in her veins, and is consumed by a hidden fire. Again and again his courage comes back to her mind, again and again the honour of his line; his face, his words, stay fixed in her heart, and her love allows her limbs no peaceful rest.

The next dawn was lighting the earth with Phoebus's lamp, and had cleared the dewy shadow from the sky, when, hardly herself, she spoke to her sister, who shared her every thought: 'Anna, my sister, what dreams terrify me and leave me so uneasy!
10quis novus hic nostris successit sedibus hospes,

12credo equidem, nec vana fides, genus esse deorum.
13degeneres animos timor arguit. heu, quibus ille

Who is this new guest who has come into our home? How noble his bearing, how brave his heart, how strong in arms! I really do believe, and it is no idle faith, that his family is divine. Fear exposes a low-born spirit. Ah, by what fates he has been tossed! What wars he told of, and endured to the end!'

1
at reginaContrast
but the queen
at marks a sharp turn from the end of Book 3, where Aeneas finally falls silent and rests. While he sleeps, the queen does not.
1
iamdudumEmphasis
for a long time now
Her suffering has already lasted all night, perhaps since the banquet in Book 1, so the book opens in the middle of her pain.
1–2
saucia … vulnusWound imagery
wounded … wound
Love is pictured as a wound, as if from Cupid's arrow. The image of a wound that will not heal runs through the book and ends in Dido's real wound from Aeneas' sword.
1–5
cura … curaRing structure
anxiety … anxiety
The first sentence opens and closes with cura, enclosing Dido in her love-sickness. At the end, cura denies her body any rest.
2
vulnus alit venisParadox
she feeds the wound in her veins
A wound should be tended so that it heals; Dido instead feeds it, which shows that she is keeping her love alive even as it harms her.
2
caeco carpitur igniFire imagery
she is consumed by a hidden fire
The hidden fire eats her away from inside. Fire is the book's main image for Dido's love, and it will end in the real flames of her pyre.
2
caeco carpiturAlliteration
consumed by a hidden
The hard repeated c sounds give a harsh, biting quality to the idea of being eaten away.
3
multa … multusqueRepetition
again and again … again and again
multus is repeated at the start of two clauses, so the reader feels Aeneas' qualities returning to Dido's mind over and over.
3
viri virtusAlliteration
the man's courage
The v sounds bind the man to his courage. Dido does not separate the two: she loves Aeneas for his virtus.
4–5
haerent infixi pectore vultus / verbaqueEnjambment
his face and words stay fixed in her heart
verbaque spills over into the next line, so even the structure shows his words clinging on. infixi, "driven in", recalls the arrow and the wound.
6
Phoebea lustrabat lampade terrasContrast
was lighting the earth with Phoebus' lamp
A calm, bright dawn is set against the dark night of Dido's mind. The world wakes refreshed; she has not slept at all.
8
unanimamCharacterisation
of one mind with her
Anna is a sister who shares Dido's every thought, which explains why her advice will carry so much weight.
8
male sanaUnderstatement
hardly sane
Literally "badly sane": the phrase warns the reader that Dido's judgement is already unsound before she speaks.
10
hospesIrony
guest, stranger
Aeneas is a guest, protected by the laws of hospitality. The word will return with bitter force when Dido feels betrayed by him.
11
ore … forti pectore et armisTricolon
his face … his brave heart and arms
Three things rising in admiration: his looks, his courage, his skill in war. The breathless exclamations show how dazzled she is.
12
genus esse deorumDramatic irony
that he is of the race of the gods
Dido is right: Aeneas is the son of Venus. The reader knows what she does not, that this divine family is the cause of her suffering.
13
degeneres animos timor arguitSententia
fear exposes a low-born spirit
Dido argues like a general: only cowards show fear, so Aeneas' courage proves his noble birth. A short, quotable maxim.
13–14
heu, quibus ille iactatus fatisExclamation
ah, by what fates he has been tossed
heu and the exclamations show that she pities Aeneas as well as admiring him, and his story of suffering has moved her.

6–7
Phoebea … AuroraPhoebus and Aurora
of Phoebus … the Dawn
Phoebus Apollo as god of the sun, and Aurora, goddess of the dawn: an epic way of saying that the next day had begun.

AnnaAnna
Anna
Dido's sister and closest companion. In some versions of the legend Anna, not Dido, was the one in love with Aeneas; Virgil makes her Dido's confidante and adviser.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Book 4 opens with Dido already wounded and burning. How does Virgil make her love seem like an illness or an injury from the very first lines?
  2. What does Dido admire in Aeneas in lines 10–14? What does this tell us about her own values?
  3. How far is Dido "male sana" (line 8) already? Look at the language of sleeplessness, fear and dreams.
Dido before Book 4 background

Dido was a princess of Tyre in Phoenicia. Her brother Pygmalion, king of Tyre, murdered her husband Sychaeus for his wealth; Sychaeus' ghost appeared to her in a dream, revealed the crime and showed her where treasure was hidden, and she escaped by sea with a band of followers. In North Africa she bought land from the local king Iarbas and founded Carthage. Venus tells this story to Aeneas in Book 1.

When Aeneas is shipwrecked on her coast, Dido receives the Trojans generously. At the banquet at the end of Book 1, Venus sends Cupid in the shape of Aeneas' son Ascanius to make Dido fall in love, and through Books 2 and 3 she listens to Aeneas tell the story of the fall of Troy and his wanderings. Book 4 begins the next morning: the damage has already been done.
