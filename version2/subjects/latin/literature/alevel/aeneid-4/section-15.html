si tangere portus infandum caput ac terris adnare necesse estConditional
if that unspeakable man must reach harbour and come to land
Dido opens by accepting what she cannot change: fate will bring Aeneas to Italy. The condition makes the curse that follows sound reasoned, not wild.
612–614

if he must reach … and if Jupiter's fates demand it, if this boundary is fixed
Three ways of saying the same thing build to the solemn image of a boundary stone, so Dido seems to accept destiny before she twists it.
613

unspeakable head
Dido will not name Aeneas. He is reduced to a cursed "head", a word used in formal curses and sacrifices.
615

by war and by arms
Two near synonyms double the threat, which gives the curse a heavy, ritual sound.
615
audacis populiWord choice
a bold people
The "bold people" will be the Rutulians and Latins under Turnus in the second half of the poem, so the curse already points to future events.
616

torn from the embrace of Iulus
The cruellest part of the curse is separation from his son. Avulsus, torn away, is a violent word for a father's loss.
617–620

A string of subjunctives turns wishes into commands, each one a separate penalty in the curse.
618
funeraEmphatic position
deaths
The word is held over to the start of line 618, so the shameful deaths of his men strike the reader with full force.
618
sub leges pacis iniquaeIrony
under the terms of an unjust peace
Peace is the goal of Aeneas' mission, yet Dido wishes him a peace that humiliates him. The word iniquae turns the reward into a punishment.
620

but let him fall before his day and lie unburied in the middle of the sand
Dying young and unburied was the worst fate a Roman could imagine. The image of a body on the open sand ends the curse on Aeneas with a picture of total disgrace.
621
hanc vocem extremam cum sanguine fundoEmphasis
I pour out these last words with my blood
Dido presents the curse as a final offering, poured out like a libation, and her blood seals it. Her death will give the words their power.
622
o TyriiApostrophe
O Tyrians
Dido turns from the gods to her own people and to all their descendants, so the curse widens from one man to two nations.
624
nullus amor populis nec foedera suntoLegal language
let there be no love between the peoples and no treaties
Sunto, an old future imperative, belongs to the language of laws and treaties. Dido legislates eternal enmity.
625
exoriare aliquis nostris ex ossibus ultorAddress to the unknown
arise, some avenger, from my bones
The second person verb is aimed at someone not yet born. Every Roman reader knew she was summoning Hannibal.
626
face … ferroqueAlliteration
with fire and sword
The hard f sounds give the threat a fierce ring and recall the torches she imagined carrying into the Trojan camp.
627
nunc, olim, quocumque dabunt se tempore viresAsyndeton
now, in future, whenever strength allows
Time words with no connectives stretch the curse across every age, so there is no point at which it will end.
628–629
litora litoribus contraria, fluctibus undas … arma armisPolyptoton
shore against shore, waves against water, arms against arms
litora litoribus and arma armis set a word against another form of itself, with waves against water between them, so the clashing words act out the clash of the two nations.
629
pugnent ipsique nepotesqueMetre
let them fight, themselves and their grandchildren
The line is hypermetric: the final -que runs on into the next line, so the war Dido calls for seems to go on beyond the end of her speech.

Questions to think about · 5
  1. How much of Dido's curse on Aeneas actually comes true, either later in the Aeneid or in the traditions about his death? What does that suggest about the power of her words?
  2. Dido's final lines look forward to centuries of war between Carthage and Rome. How would a Roman reader in the 20s BC have responded to this, and how does it change our view of Dido?
  3. Look at the language of the curse: the conditions, the jussive subjunctives, the legal-sounding sunto. How does Virgil make it sound like a formal, binding act?
  4. Looking back over both passages of Book 4 you have read, is Dido presented more as a victim or as a danger? Use evidence from the beginning and the end of the set text.
  5. Is the Aeneas of Book 4 a hero we admire? How do Virgil's portrayals of Dido and of the gods affect your answer?
The curse and the Punic Wars background

Dido's curse falls on two levels. On Aeneas himself: he will be harried in war in Italy, separated from Iulus, forced to beg for help and to accept an unequal peace, and will die before his time, unburied. Much of this happens in the second half of the Aeneid (Aeneas has to leave Iulus to seek help from Evander in Book 8), and later tradition said that Aeneas died after a short reign, his body lost in the river Numicus.

On their peoples: she demands that the Tyrians hate the Trojans' descendants for ever, with no love or treaty between them, and calls on an avenger to rise from her bones. Every Roman reader knew who that was: Hannibal, who invaded Italy in 218 BC and came close to destroying Rome. The Punic Wars ended with the destruction of Carthage in 146 BC. For Virgil's first readers, the enmity of Carthage and Rome had its origin here, in a queen's grief and a hero's duty.

Immediately after this curse, Dido sends for Anna, climbs the pyre and kills herself with Aeneas' sword (lines 630–705).
