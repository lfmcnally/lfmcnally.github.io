99quin potius pacem aeternam pactosque hymenaeos
100exercemus? habes tota quod mente petisti:
101ardet amans Dido traxitque per ossa furorem.
102communem hunc ergo populum paribusque regamus
103auspiciis; liceat Phrygio servire marito
104dotalesque tuae Tyrios permittere dextrae.'
'Why do we not instead make a lasting peace and an agreed marriage? You have exactly what you set your whole heart on: Dido is burning with love and has drawn the madness right through her bones. Let us, then, rule this people in common, with equal authority between us; let her be allowed to serve a Trojan husband, and hand her Tyrians over to you as her dowry.'
105olli (sensit enim simulata mente locutam,
106quo regnum Italiae Libycas averteret oras)
107sic contra est ingressa Venus: 'quis talia demens
108abnuat aut tecum malit contendere bello?
109si modo quod memoras factum fortuna sequatur.
Venus knew well enough that Juno had spoken with false intent, meaning to turn the kingdom of Italy aside to the shores of Libya instead, and answered her in turn: 'Who would be mad enough to refuse an offer like that, or choose to go to war with you — if only fortune follows through on what you propose?
110sed fatis incerta feror, si Iuppiter unam
111esse velit Tyriis urbem Troiaque profectis,
112miscerive probet populos aut foedera iungi.
113tu coniunx, tibi fas animum temptare precando.
114perge, sequar.'
But I am adrift, uncertain about the fates, not knowing whether Jupiter wants one single city for both Tyrians and the exiles from Troy, or would approve of the two peoples mingling, or of treaties joining them. You are his wife; it is right for you to sound out his mind by pleading. Lead on; I will follow.'

99–100
quin potius pacem aeternam pactosque hymenaeos exercemus?Rhetorical question
why don't we work instead for lasting peace and an agreed marriage?
Juno frames her offer as a question, which makes it sound friendly and reasonable rather than a demand.
99
pacem … pactosqueAlliteration
peace … agreed
The repeated p sounds link peace with the marriage pact, and the near-pun on pax and pactus makes the deal sound neat and settled.
99
pacem aeternamIrony
lasting peace
Readers know that Carthage and Rome will fight three wars. Juno's "eternal peace" is bitterly ironic.
100
tota quod mente petistiEmphasis
what you sought with your whole mind
Juno flatters Venus by telling her she has already won, so agreeing seems the obvious next step.
101
ardet amans DidoFire imagery
Dido is burning with love
The fire image returns: Dido "burns", which links back to uritur in line 68.
101
traxitque per ossa furoremMetaphor
and has drawn madness through her bones
Love is pictured as a poison or fever that has soaked right through to her bones: it cannot now be cured.
102–103
communem hunc ergo populum paribusque regamus auspiciisBalance
let us rule this people in common, with equal auspices
Juno offers equal shares, but the people would be ruled from Carthage, which is her real aim.
103
liceat Phrygio servire maritoWord choice
let her be allowed to be a slave to a Phrygian husband
servire, "to be a slave", is a harsh word for a queen's marriage, and Phrygio is slightly scornful. Juno's contempt for both of them shows through.
104
dotalesque tuae Tyrios permittere dextraeCharacterisation
and hand over her Tyrians to your power as a dowry
Dido's whole people are to be handed over like property, a reminder that the gods treat mortals as bargaining pieces.
105
sensit enim simulata mente locutamParenthesis
for she realised that Juno had spoken with a false mind
Virgil tells the reader directly that Juno is lying, so we watch the rest of the exchange knowing that both goddesses are playing a game.
106
quo regnum Italiae Libycas averteret orasPurpose
so that she could turn the kingdom of Italy aside to the shores of Libya
The aside states Juno's real purpose: to divert Rome's destined empire to Carthage.
107–108
quis talia demens abnuatRhetorical question
who would be mad enough to refuse such terms?
Venus answers question with question. Her courteous tone hides the fact that she is not really agreeing to anything.
109
si modo quod memoras factum fortuna sequaturCondition
if only fortune would follow what you describe
si modo, "if only", is Venus' escape clause: she agrees in principle, but only if fate allows it, which she knows it will not.
110
sed fatis incerta ferorFate
but I am carried along, uncertain because of the fates
Venus pretends not to know Jupiter's will, though she heard it in Book 1. Her uncertainty is a polite fiction.
110–112
unam esse velit … miscerive probet populos aut foedera iungiTricolon
wants there to be one city … approves the mixing of peoples or the joining of treaties
Three ways of uniting the peoples, each more formal than the last, show Venus listing the possibilities without accepting any of them.
113
tu coniunxEmphatic position
you are his wife
Venus puts the responsibility back on Juno: as Jupiter's wife, Juno can ask him herself. The short phrase is pointed.
114
perge, sequarAsyndeton
go on, I will follow
Two short verbs with no connecting word end the speech briskly, and the half-line leaves the moment hanging.
Notes on the text 2
99
hymenaeosHymenaeus
marriage
Hymen or Hymenaeus was the god of weddings, and his name was used for the wedding song and for marriage itself.
103–104
Phrygio … TyriosPhrygians and Tyrians
Phrygian … Tyrians
The Trojans are called Phrygians, after the region of Asia Minor around Troy; Dido's people are Tyrians, from Tyre in Phoenicia, where she came from.
Questions to think about · 3
  1. Juno's offer (99–104) sounds generous. What does she really hope to gain, and how does Virgil let the reader know?
  2. Venus replies politely but uncertainly (107–114). How does she manage to agree without committing herself?
  3. Both goddesses talk about Dido as if she were a piece in a game. How does this affect your sympathy for her?
