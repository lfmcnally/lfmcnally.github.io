'I truly believe that it was with the gods as our protectors, and with Juno's favour, that the Trojan ships held this course before the wind. What a city you will see rise here, sister, what a kingdom, from such a marriage! With the Trojans' arms marching beside our own, to what heights will Carthage's glory raise itself!

45
dis … auspicibus … Iunone secundaDramatic irony
with the gods as guides … and Juno favourable
Anna thinks the gods favour the match. The reader knows that Juno does support it, but only to keep Aeneas from Italy.
45
auspicibusWord choice
as guides
Auspices were signs read from birds before any public act. Anna presents the Trojans' arrival as divinely approved, like an official undertaking.
46
Iliacas … carinasEpic epithet
the Trojan keels
The ships are "Ilian", from Ilium, Troy. Synecdoche (keels for ships) and the grand adjective give her argument an epic tone.
47
quam tu urbem, soror, hanc cernes, quae surgere regnaExclamation
what a city you will see, sister, what kingdoms rising
Anna's excitement builds through exclamations, painting a glorious future for Dido to imagine.
48
coniugio taliEmphatic position
from such a marriage
Anna already calls it a marriage. The word is placed at the start of the line, so Dido hears the idea she has resisted presented as natural.
48
Teucrum comitantibus armisCharacterisation
with the arms of the Teucrians alongside
Aeneas is presented as a military ally. Anna turns a love affair into a defence policy.
49
Punica … gloriaTragic irony
Punic glory
The Romans would destroy Carthage. Anna's vision of the two peoples rising together is the reverse of history.
49
attolletWord choice
will raise itself
Carthage's glory is pictured rising like a building, which looks ahead to the building work that stops once Dido is lost in love (lines 86–89).
50
tu modoEmphasis
you need only
Anna makes it sound easy: just a few prayers and some hospitality.
51
indulge hospitioEuphemism
indulge in hospitality
Anna dresses up love as the respectable duty of a host. The word hospitium will become bitter later in the book.
51
causasque innecte morandiMetaphor
weave reasons for delay
innecte, "weave", suggests a deliberate plot or trap. Dido is to hold Aeneas back with a web of excuses.
52
dum pelago desaevitAlliteration
while winter rages on the sea
The heavy d sounds suggest the force of the winter storms that will keep the Trojans in port.
52–53
hiems … aquosus Orion … quassataeque ratesTricolon

Three reasons in a row for Aeneas to stay, each one practical, so that delay seems only sensible.
54

she set her heart on fire with boundless love
Anna's words act like fuel on the fire already burning in Dido.
54–55

Questions to think about · 3
  1. Anna claims the gods and Juno favour the marriage. What does the reader know about Juno's motives that Anna does not?
  2. How does Anna make a love affair sound like a matter of state? Pick out the political language.
  3. Lines 54–55 describe the effect of Anna's speech. What has changed in Dido, and how does the language show it?
Juno and Carthage background

Juno is Carthage's patron goddess and the great enemy of Aeneas and the Trojans throughout the Aeneid. She knows the Fates have decreed that Aeneas' descendants will found Rome and one day destroy Carthage, and she is determined to delay or prevent it. A marriage between Aeneas and Dido would keep him in Africa and away from Italy.

Virgil's Roman readers knew how the story ended: Rome and Carthage fought three Punic Wars, and Carthage was destroyed in 146 BC. Anna's vision of Punic glory rising with Trojan help is full of irony for them.
