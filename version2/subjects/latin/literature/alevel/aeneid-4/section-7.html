The repeated non lists everything that has stopped, so the city's paralysis is built up item by item.

Juno is called "dear" to Jupiter, an ironic title for a goddess whose marriage is famously quarrelsome, and who is about to scheme behind his back.

Questions to think about · 3
  1. Lines 86–89 describe the building work coming to a halt. Why does Virgil include this here, and what does it say about Dido as a ruler?
  2. How does Juno's speech (93–98) combine flattery and sarcasm? What does she really want from Venus?
  3. Virgil calls Juno "cara Iovis coniunx" and "Saturnia" in the same breath. What do these names suggest about her power and her character?
Juno and Venus background

The two goddesses are old enemies. Juno, wife of Jupiter, hates the Trojans: Paris judged Venus more beautiful than her, and she knows that Aeneas' descendants are fated to destroy her favourite city, Carthage. Venus is Aeneas' mother and protects him at every turn; in Book 1 she sent Cupid, disguised as Ascanius, to make Dido fall in love with him.

Here Juno tries a new tactic. If she cannot stop Aeneas reaching Italy, perhaps she can keep him in Carthage by marrying him to Dido. Venus sees through the plan but goes along with it, because it keeps her son safe for now.
