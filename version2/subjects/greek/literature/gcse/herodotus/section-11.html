Pactyes and the Lydian revolt background

Lydia was a rich kingdom in western Asia Minor, with its capital at Sardis. Its last king, Croesus, was defeated by Cyrus the Great of Persia in about 546 BC. Cyrus left a Persian, Tabalus, in command of Sardis, and gave a Lydian called Pactyes the job of collecting the gold of Croesus and the Lydians.

As soon as Cyrus had gone, Pactyes took the gold down to the coast, used it to hire soldiers, and besieged Tabalus in Sardis. Cyrus sent a Median general, Mazares, with an army to crush the revolt, with orders to bring Pactyes back to him alive. Pactyes did not wait for him: he fled to the Greek city of Cyme, and that is where this story begins.

Suppliants and asylum background

A suppliant (ἱκέτης) put himself under the protection of a god, or of a more powerful person, by a recognised ritual: sitting at an altar or in a sanctuary, holding out a branch wound with wool, or kneeling and clasping the knees of the person he was begging. Zeus himself, as Zeus Hikesios, watched over suppliants, and to drag one away from an altar, or to hand one over to be killed, was a grave offence against the gods, which could bring pollution and disaster on a whole community.

A city that took in a suppliant took on that protection too, and this could set it against powerful neighbours who wanted him back. Greek writers returned to the dilemma again and again (Aeschylus' Suppliant Women is built around it), and it is easy to see why it still feels familiar: modern states face the same pull between the duty to protect people who ask for asylum and the cost of offending those they have fled from.
