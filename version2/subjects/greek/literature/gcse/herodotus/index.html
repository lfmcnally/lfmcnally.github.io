Herodotus

Over the years, Herodotus of Halicarnassus (484–425 BC) has been called both the ‘Father of History’ and the ‘Father of Lies’. He was criticised by Thucydides for telling stories rather than delivering an accurate account of the past; but Thucydides was a rival historian, so it was in his own interests to criticise the competition. Herodotus owed much to Homer and to oral stories, and it has been suggested that he often delivered his work as a reading or performance to an audience.

His research, ἱστορίη in Greek, gives us both our word ‘history’ and the title of his work, the Histories. Herodotus makes great use of digressions and stories to amplify his over-arching themes: Greeks against non-Greeks, liberty and slavery, and the reversal of fortune of both individuals and empires. He arranges his account artfully, sometimes setting stories with contrasting messages side by side, but he leaves it to the reader or listener to make the connections.

We have to remember that ‘history’ as a genre was something different from what we would recognise today, and that Herodotus’ aims were not those of a modern historian. Many of his stories have more to do with geography or local customs than with politics, although he touches on that too.

Herodotus wrote nine books about a great conflict between East and West, the Persian Wars of 490–479 BC, famous for battles like Thermopylae and Salamis, in which the Persian attempt to gain a foothold in Europe ended in a spectacular and unexpected Greek victory. His work is not simply a patriotic celebration of that victory: he is interested in the ἔργα μεγάλα τε καὶ θωμαστά, the ‘great and amazing deeds’, of Greeks and non-Greeks alike. His stories have a strong moral aspect. It is a world where gods operate as well as men, but the spirit of enquiry that drives the work is marked by the human curiosity typical of fifth-century Greek thought.

Herodotus travelled widely and recorded traditions from many countries; Book 2, for example, is an account of Egypt, where he questioned the priests and recorded what he learned. The Histories reach their climax in Books 8 and 9 with the Persian invasion of Greece in 480–479 BC and its failure, but he begins by considering the origins and rapid growth of the Persian Empire under Cyrus the Great, who reigned from 559 to 530 BC. The extracts you will read are from Books 1 and 9.

Herodotus wrote in the Ionic dialect, which differs slightly from the Attic Greek taught in school: Ionic has -σσ- where Attic has -ττ-, and η in place of long α, along with a number of other changes to vowels and consonants. For GCSE, some of the Ionic has been changed to make it more familiar, so the text you read mixes the two: Attic θάλατταν, for example, but Ionic ἐς for εἰς.

The three stories

These three extracts come from very different parts of the Histories, the great work in which Herodotus charts how the Greeks and Persians came to be at war early in the fifth century BC. But each shows characteristics typical of his way of telling a story.

First, they are self-contained anecdotes which crop up in the course of the main narrative. Their relevance to the narrative they emerge from varies, but clearly what makes Herodotus include them is that he enjoys them and thinks them worth passing on. Secondly, to a greater or lesser degree, they show the workings of powers beyond normal human experience: powers that are present in the world in which mortals live, and which often shape mortal lives, but which mortals struggle to explain or fully understand.

In the first, a famous musician, Arion, is miraculously saved from what seems certain death at the hands of thugs, and his attackers are later forced to confess. Herodotus, a literary artist himself, clearly enjoys the fact that Arion’s artistry saves him; but, as throughout his work, it also matters to him to show that injustice is exposed.

In the other two, Herodotus is drawn to the mysterious and sometimes surprising ways in which justice overseen by the gods works in the world of men. In the story of Euenius, as in that of Arion, deceit and the giving of justice both feature; but Herodotus shows that when mortals take justice into their own hands, the result does not necessarily match what the gods see as the right response.

This idea is taken further in the third story. The people of Cyme are put in a hard moral and political position by the arrival of Pactyes, a fugitive from the Persians, who claims his god-protected right to asylum as a suppliant. Are they to appease the Persians, who order them to surrender him, and ignore the divine protection owed to a fugitive? Or should they honour the suppliant’s claim and face the consequences at the hands of the Persians? They think it sensible to ask Apollo for clarity, but the god sees things differently: their attempt at piety is itself impiety. They should have known what to do, but were distracted by the political side of their dilemma. In our own times the story has gained a new relevance, as the countries of Europe have had to weigh the competing claims of morality and politics in deciding how to respond to people who have claimed asylum with them.

Some stylistic terms
Alliteration
Repetition of the same sound, usually at the start of a word.
Antithesis
Words of opposite meaning placed together to bring out a contrast.
Asyndeton
The leaving out of conjunctions.
Chiasmus
A mirroring pattern (ABBA), often to bring out a contrast.
Contrast
Two or more opposing ideas, often signposted in Greek with μέν and δέ.
Delay (demotion)
The opposite of promotion: an important word held back to the end of the sentence.
Doublet
A pair of words, often of similar meaning, used together for emphasis.
Dramatic irony
Where the reader knows something the characters do not.
Juxtaposition
Two words placed next to each other for emphasis, often for contrast.
Litotes
A positive statement made by denying its opposite.
Polyptoton
Repetition of a word from the same root in a different form.
Polysyndeton
A succession of conjunctions.
Promotion
A word or phrase moved to the front of its sentence or clause for emphasis.
Semantic field
A set of words related in meaning.
Superlative
‘Very’, ‘most’: often used for emphasis.
Tautology
Saying the same thing twice in different words.
Tricolon
Three words, phrases or clauses combining to make a single, powerful impression.
Variatio
Using different words of similar meaning to add variety, rather than repeating the same word.
