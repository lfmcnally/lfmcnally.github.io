Tales from Herodotus

Herodotus

Herodotus came from Halicarnassus, a Greek city on the coast of Asia Minor (modern Bodrum), and lived from about 484 to about 425 BC. His Histories, in nine books, tell how the Greeks and the Persian Empire came into conflict, building up to Xerxes’ invasion of Greece in 480–479 BC and its defeat at Salamis, Plataea and Mycale.

He says in his first sentence that he is setting out the results of his enquiry (ἱστορίη, the word that gives us ‘history’) so that the great deeds of Greeks and foreigners alike should not be forgotten. Cicero called him the father of history; other ancient critics thought him too ready to believe a good story. Both views have something in them. He travelled widely, questioned the people he met, and often reports more than one version of an event, but he also loves a remarkable tale and will break off his main narrative to tell one.

All three passages here are such breaks. Each is a complete story in a few dozen lines, and each says something about how justice works in a world where the gods take a hand in human affairs.

Herodotus wrote in Ionic, the dialect of the eastern Aegean, not the Attic of Athens that GCSE Greek is based on. The set text has been partly brought into line with Attic (θάλαττα, not Ionic θάλασσα), but some Ionic forms remain, such as ἐς for εἰς, μιν for αὐτόν and σφι for αὐτοῖς. They are explained where they occur.

The three stories

How Arion was saved by a dolphin (1.24). Told while Herodotus is describing Periander of Corinth. A famous singer, sailing home rich from Italy, is robbed by the crew of his own ship and forced into the sea; a dolphin carries him to land, and back in Corinth the sailors are caught out in their lie.

The story of Euenius (9.93–94). Told during the account of the battle of Mycale in 479 BC, because the Greeks’ seer there was Euenius’ son. A guardian of sacred sheep is blinded by his fellow citizens for letting wolves kill them; the gods punish the city, and Euenius is compensated, though not in the way the citizens intended.

Respect for suppliants enforced by an oracle (1.157–159). After Cyrus of Persia conquers Lydia, the Lydian Pactyes leads a revolt, fails, and takes refuge at Cyme. The Persians demand him back, and the people of Cyme ask Apollo what to do. The god’s answer is not the one anybody expected.

As you read, think about what the three have in common: wrongdoing that is found out, people who try to manage the gods or each other, and how hard mortals find it to understand what the gods want.

Terms for discussing style
Alliteration
The same sound repeated at the start of several words close together.
Antithesis
Opposed ideas set against each other, often in balanced phrases; in Greek, often marked by μέν … δέ.
Asyndeton
Clauses or words strung together with no connecting words.
Chiasmus
Words or ideas arranged in a mirror pattern, A B B A.
Delay
Holding back an important word to the end of a clause or sentence, so that it lands with weight.
Direct speech
A character’s actual words, quoted.
Dramatic irony
When the reader understands something that a character does not.
Framing
Placing two related words or ideas around others, at the start and end of a phrase or passage.
Juxtaposition
Placing two words side by side so that they react with each other, often for contrast.
Litotes
Understatement by denying the opposite: ‘not unaware’ for ‘well aware’.
Polyptoton
The same word repeated in different grammatical forms.
Polysyndeton
More connecting words than are needed (‘and … and … and’).
Promotion
Moving a word to the front of its clause for emphasis.
Superlative
The ‘most’ form of an adjective or adverb, often used for emphasis.
Tricolon
A group of three words, phrases or clauses, often building to a climax.
Questions on all three stories
  1. In each story someone tries to hide what they have done. How is the truth brought out, and by whom?
  2. Which character do you sympathise with most in each story, and does Herodotus seem to want you to?
  3. How do the gods show what they want in these stories, and how well do the humans understand them?
  4. Herodotus could have left all three stories out of his history. What do they add to it?
  5. Choose one moment from each story that you think Herodotus tells especially well, and explain how he does it.
