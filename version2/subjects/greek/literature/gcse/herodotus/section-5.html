  1. Why might Herodotus describe the custom of guarding the flock in such detail before telling us anything about Euenius?
  2. What does the choice of guards tell you about how the Apollonians regard the sheep?
Why this story? background

The tale of Euenius comes in Book 9 of the Histories, in the middle of the campaign of 479 BC. Before the battle of Mycale, fought on the coast of Asia Minor opposite Samos, the sacrifices for the Greek fleet were made by a seer called Deiphonus, and Herodotus stops to explain who Deiphonus' father was and how the family came by its gift of prophecy. The story has nothing to do with the war. It is there because Herodotus thinks it worth telling, and because it says something about how gods deal with men.

Apollonia and its sacred flock background

Apollonia was a Greek city near the Adriatic coast, in what is now Albania, founded around 600 BC by colonists from Corinth and Corcyra. Many Greek sanctuaries kept animals that belonged to a god: they could not be sold, eaten or used like ordinary livestock, and harming them was an offence against the god himself. This flock belongs to Helios, the Sun, who had less of a cult of his own than the Olympian gods; but Greek audiences knew another sacred flock of the Sun from Book 12 of the Odyssey, where Odysseus' starving crew kill and eat the Sun's cattle and are all destroyed for it.
