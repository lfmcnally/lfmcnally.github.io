Notes on the text 8
1
Ἀρίων, -ονος, ὁArion
Arion
A semi-legendary poet and musician of the seventh century BC.
1
κιθαρῳδόςThe cithara
cithara-player
A singer who accompanied himself on the cithara, a stringed instrument like a small harp. Where the lyre was thought rather rustic, the cithara was the instrument of professional musicians like Arion.
2
Περίανδρος, -ου, ὁPeriander
Periander
Autocrat of Corinth, 625–585 BC. The Greek word τύραννος, from which the English "tyrant" comes, was not necessarily negative: for many, despots like Periander brought prosperity and influence to their cities. He encouraged wider trade, both by founding colonies and by his close contact with other autocrats around the Greek world.
2–3
Κορίνθου … εἰς Ἰταλίαν τε καὶ ΣικελίανCorinth and the West
of Corinth … to Italy and Sicily
The wealth and status of Corinth rested largely on her trade with the colonies founded by herself and other Greek states to the west of her position on the Isthmus. Corinthian influence in Sicily and Italy made them logical places for Arion to go to develop the financial opportunities of his musical career.
2
Κόρινθος, -ου, ἡCorinth
Corinth
The city on the narrow neck of land (isthmus) linking the Peloponnese with the rest of mainland Greece. With a harbour on either shore of the isthmus, Corinth could trade both east and west, which gave it great wealth; enlightened despots such as Periander increased its prosperity.
3
Ἰταλία, -ας, ἡItaly
Italy
Much of the south and west coast of Italy had been colonised by settlers from Greek cities like Corinth, so that it was known as "Magna Graecia", Latin for "Great Greece". The "mother cities" kept close relations with their colonies.
3
Σικελία, -ας, ἡSicily
Sicily
The island immediately south of Italy. Like southern Italy, it was settled by colonists from Greek cities and was Greek-speaking.
5
Τάρας, -αντος, ὁTarentum
Tarentum
An important Greek city, founded by Spartan settlers, in the "arch" of the "boot" of Italy.

Arion and the dolphin background

As the mosaic from Henchir-Thyna in Tunisia, along with other representations on Greek vases and in figurines, shows, this story was, or became, very well known.

The association of the dolphin with Apollo, the god of music, is clear in the name of his famous sanctuary at Delphi (named from the Greek for "dolphin", δελφίς, -ῖνος). The Homeric Hymn to Apollo (lines 399–500) records that this manifestation of Apollo's divinity was imported from Crete, when the god took the form of a dolphin to gain passage with Cretan sailors on their way to the site of Delphi. There he overcame the local snake deity of the earth, Pytho, to establish himself as the principal god worshipped at the site; it is from this earlier divinity that the "Pythian" priestess of Apollo's oracle took her title. Given this close link between Apollo the god of music and the dolphin, it is no surprise that it is a dolphin which saves the life of the great musician Arion in this story. That Apollo, implicitly, intervenes directly to help the musician also confirms Arion's great status.

Music was of great significance in the public life of ancient Greece. When it accompanied songs recounting the stories which a community inherited and shared, music was fundamental to a society's sense of its common identity. In Athens, music was part of public life in drama, tragedy and comedy, and in the large choral performances known as dithyrambs; both featured in the festivals at the heart of the city's cultural, political and religious life. Among the most common virtuoso instruments were the aulos (a double pipe) and the cithara and the lyre, both stringed instruments like a small harp. Where the lyre was thought rather more rustic, the cithara was used by professional musicians like Arion, who would accompany his own singing on it.
