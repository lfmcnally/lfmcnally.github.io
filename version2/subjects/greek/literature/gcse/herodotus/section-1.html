1
Ἀρίων ὁ κιθαρῳδόςPromotion
Arion the cithara-singer
The story begins with Arion's name, and the very next words tell us what he is: a singer who plays the cithara. Herodotus puts his art first because it is his art that will save him.
2
ΠεριάνδρῳFraming
Periander
Periander appears in the first sentence and then vanishes until Arion comes back to him (line 25). The story is framed by the court that Arion leaves and returns to.
3–4
χρήματα μεγάλαKey word
a great deal of money
Arion's fortune is named before anything is said about the voyage, and the adjective μεγάλα is held over to the next line. The money is what turns an ordinary journey home into danger.
4
ὡρμᾶτοImperfect tense
he was setting out
After a run of aorists, the imperfect ὡρμᾶτο slows the pace: it sets the scene for the voyage, and tells us that the real action is still to come.
5–6
πιστεύων δὲ οὐδαμοῖς μᾶλλον ἢ ΚορινθίοιςDramatic irony
trusting nobody more than Corinthians
Herodotus spells out Arion's reasoning as a comparison, so that we know exactly whom he trusts most. Anyone who already knows the story can see what is coming: the men he trusts above all are the ones who will turn on him.
6
Κορινθίοις … ΚορινθίωνRepetition
Corinthians … Corinthian
Two phrases in a row end on the same word. The crew's nationality is hammered home without a word of comment, and the reader is left to draw the conclusion.
Notes on the text 6
1
Ἀρίων, -ονος, ὁArion
Arion
A poet and musician from Methymna on Lesbos, active around 600 BC. Herodotus says he was the finest cithara-singer of his day and the first to compose the dithyramb, a choral song for Dionysus, as a work of art.
1
κιθαρῳδόςThe cithara
cithara-singer
The cithara (κιθάρα) was the large concert version of the lyre, the instrument of professional performers. A κιθαρῳδός sang to his own accompaniment on it.
2
Περίανδρος, -ου, ὁPeriander
Periander
Ruler of Corinth from about 627 to 587 BC, under whom the city was rich and powerful. A τύραννος was a man who held sole power in a city without inheriting it as a king; the word did not necessarily mean that he was cruel.
2
Κόρινθος, -ου, ἡCorinth
Corinth
A wealthy trading city on the Isthmus, the strip of land joining the Peloponnese to central Greece, with harbours on both sides.
3
Ἰταλίαν τε καὶ ΣικελίανThe Greek West
Italy and Sicily
Southern Italy and Sicily were full of Greek cities, several of them Corinthian colonies (Syracuse was one). They were rich places where a famous performer could earn a fortune.
5
Τάρας, -αντος, ὁTarentum
Tarentum
Taras (modern Taranto), a Spartan colony on the gulf in the instep of Italy, and a natural place to take ship for Greece.
Questions to think about · 2
  1. Herodotus mentions Arion's fortune before he says anything about the voyage. What does that lead you to expect?
  2. Arion picks his ship because the crew are Corinthians. How does Herodotus make sure we notice that choice?
About this story background

Herodotus tells the story of Arion in Book 1 of the Histories (1.23–24), as an aside while he is describing Periander, ruler of Corinth. He says that the Corinthians and the people of Lesbos, Arion's home island, both tell it, and that in his day there was a small bronze figure at Taenarum of a man riding a dolphin, which Arion had dedicated there.

The tale was famous throughout antiquity. Dolphins were thought to love music, and they had a special connection with Apollo, the god of music: one story explained the name of his oracle at Delphi by saying that he had once appeared there in the form of a dolphin (δελφίς). A musician rescued by a dolphin is a musician the gods are looking after.

Watch how Herodotus tells it. The narrative is quick and plain, and most of the drama is carried by where he puts his words, by his participles, and by a handful of details that he chooses to repeat.
