Notes on the text 3
20
νόμον τὸν ὄρθιοννόμος
the high-pitched strain
Here νόμος means a musical "strain" or set piece for solo singer and cithara, not "law". The ὄρθιος νόμος was a piece pitched high and clear.
23
δελφίςThe dolphin
dolphin
Dolphins were associated with Apollo, patron of music, so a Greek audience would readily see divine help in the rescue of a great musician.
23
Ταίναρον, -ου, τόTaenarum
Cape Taenarum
The southernmost point of mainland Greece (modern Cape Matapan), at the tip of the middle prong of the Peloponnese, with a sanctuary of Poseidon. Corinth lay far to the north.

The Arion legend background

Arion came from Methymna, on the island of Lesbos, and was remembered as the greatest citharode (a singer who accompanied himself on the cithara) of the seventh century BC. Herodotus, who tells his story just before this passage, says that he was the first person known to have composed a dithyramb, a choral song in honour of Dionysus, to have given it a name and to have produced it at Corinth. Later Greeks regarded him as the man who turned the dithyramb into an organised art form, one of the roots of Athenian drama.

His rescue by a dolphin became one of the best-known stories of the ancient world. Herodotus ends his account by mentioning a small bronze figure of a man on a dolphin, dedicated by Arion at Taenarum, and the tale was retold by later writers such as Plutarch and pictured in Roman mosaics.
