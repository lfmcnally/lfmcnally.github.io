Lesson 1 · Xenia

Before any character in the Odyssey speaks, the poem shows us a house full of guests behaving like owners. Hospitality is the poem’s first and quickest test of character, and it stays that way to the end.

What you need to know
  • what xenia is, and why it was a matter of survival rather than manners
  • the sequence a host was expected to follow, and the duties a guest owed in return
  • every hospitality scene in Books 1–4, and what each tells us about the people in it
  • why the suitors are the worst breach of xenia in the poem, and what that sets up

Where it happens in Book 1

1.97–108 the suitors in the courtyard   1.104–113 the stranger at the door   1.113–135 the welcome   1.170–212 questions and the claimed bond   1.272–279 bath, gift and revelation

What xenia is
Section 1

Xenia is the Greek system of hospitality: how a stranger was received, and the lasting bond that receiving them created. In Homer it is one of the basic rules holding society together, and the gods enforce it.

It grew out of hard practical facts. There were no inns, no police and no way of sending word ahead, so a traveller depended entirely on whoever opened the door. Everyone travelled, to trade, to fight, to seek news or a marriage, so today’s host was tomorrow’s stranger and the rule protected everyone. And a stranger might be dangerous, or might be a god, which made treating every stranger well the only safe policy.

The words
xenia
hospitality; guest-friendship; the whole system of receiving strangers and the bond it creates
xenos
stranger, guest and host, all in one word. Both sides of the relationship are bound by it
Zeus Xenios
Zeus as protector of strangers and guests. Mistreating a guest is an offence against him
theoxeny
the possibility that a stranger is a god in disguise. In Book 1 this is literally true
guest-gift
the parting gift that seals the relationship and creates a debt to be repaid
Common mistake

Xenia is not politeness. It is a binding social and religious obligation with fixed rules on both sides, and the poem punishes those who break it.

The rules
Section 2

Homeric welcomes follow a recognisable order, and the order matters. The stranger is fed before anyone asks who they are, which shows that hospitality is owed to any stranger and not only to people who turn out to be important. Book 1 supplies an example of almost every step.

What a host had to do

  1. Notice the stranger. A guest left standing at the door is already a failure. Only Telemachus sees Athene waiting; the suitors do not. 1.104–113
  2. Go to them and take their hand or weapon. Telemachus takes Athene’s spear and stands it in the rack beside his father’s. 1.113–121
  3. Seat them, away from noise. A fine chair with a footstool, apart from the suitors’ racket. 1.130–135
  4. Food and drink before anything else. Water for the hands, bread, meat and wine, brought by servants.
  5. Only after the meal, ask who they are. The set questions: name, home, ship. 1.170–177
  6. Offer a bath and a bed. Telemachus offers a bath before Athene leaves. from 1.272
  7. Give a guest-gift on departure. Telemachus offers a keepsake; Athene defers it to her return. 1.272–279
  8. Escort or provisions for the journey on. Nestor gives Telemachus a chariot, horses and his own son as guide. Book 3

What a guest owed in return

  1. Accept what is offered graciously and take no more than is given.
  2. Give an honest account of yourself when asked.
  3. Respect the household: its property, its servants and its women.
  4. Do not outstay your welcome.
  5. Repay the favour later, to the host or to the host’s children.

Gifts

Gifts are the part students most often underrate. A guest-gift is not a souvenir. It is the visible proof that a bond now exists between two households, it creates a debt, and it displays the host’s wealth as it travels with the guest. The best gift is one the guest can actually use and show, which is why Menelaus swaps horses for a silver bowl once Telemachus points out that Ithaca has no room for horses (Book 4).

The bond also outlives the people who made it: sons inherit their fathers’ guest-friends, which is exactly what Athene exploits when, as Mentes, she claims old friendship with Odysseus and Laertes. 1.178–212

Xenia in Book 1
Section 3 · Prescribed text

Book 1 contains one complete, model hospitality scene and one long, continuing breach. Read them against each other.

3.1The suitors in the courtyard1.97–108

Before any character speaks, the poem shows the suitors sprawled on hides outside the palace, playing games, waited on by servants, with the household’s meat being prepared for them. They are guests behaving as owners. The effect is to set the standard of bad xenia before the audience has seen good xenia, so that everything Telemachus does next is measured against it.

3.2The stranger at the door1.104–113
  • Athene, disguised as Mentes, a Taphian chieftain and old friend of the family, stands at the entrance.
  • The suitors do not notice her. Telemachus, sitting miserably among them, sees her and is ashamed that a guest has been kept waiting.
  • Noticing is the first duty of a host, and it is the first thing that separates Telemachus from the suitors.
3.3The welcome1.113–135
  1. Telemachus goes straight to her and takes her right hand and her spear.
  2. He puts the spear in the rack beside his father’s spears, treating her weapon as an honoured friend’s.
  3. He leads her to a fine chair, spreads a cloth on it, sets a footstool, and seats her apart from the suitors so their noise will not spoil her meal.
  4. A servant brings water to wash; bread is set out; meat is carved and served; wine is poured.
  5. Only when they have eaten does he ask who she is.

Every element of the conventional sequence is present and in the right order, for a guest Telemachus cannot identify. That is the point: he does it because the rule applies to everyone.

3.4The questions and the claimed bond1.170–212

Telemachus asks the set questions: who are you, where from, what ship brought you. “Mentes” answers with a full account: a trading voyage, a cargo of iron, a crew waiting at the ship, and old friendship with Odysseus and with Laertes. The claim of friendship activates the inherited bond. A stranger who can name a family connection is no longer really a stranger.

3.5Bath, gift and revelation1.272–279

As the guest prepares to leave, Telemachus offers a bath and a valuable keepsake. Athene declines for now, saying he can give it when she returns, and vanishes like a bird. He offered everything a host should; the gift goes unclaimed only because the guest was never mortal. Theoxeny has been made literal.

What Book 1 establishes
  • Telemachus is a good host, and therefore a good man, before he has done anything else in the poem.
  • The suitors are bad guests before any of them has spoken.
  • Hospitality is the poem’s first and quickest test of character, and it stays that way.
Xenia in Books 2–4
Section 4 · Context

Book 2 · Antinous at the assembly

Antinous blames Penelope for the suitors’ long stay: she strung them along, so it is her fault they are still there. Notice the assumption underneath, that guests are entitled to stay until they get what they came for. That is the reverse of a guest’s duty to leave.

Book 3 · Nestor at Pylos

  • Telemachus and Mentor arrive in the middle of a sacrifice. Nestor’s son Pisistratus comes to them at once, seats them, gives them meat and wine, and invites them to join the family’s prayers.
  • Only afterwards does Nestor ask who they are: food before questions.
  • On departure Nestor gives a chariot, horses and Pisistratus as escort to Sparta: gift and safe passage, the final steps.

Book 4 · Menelaus at Sparta

  • A servant, Eteoneus, sees two strangers arrive during a wedding feast and asks whether to admit them or send them on.
  • Menelaus is angry at the question. He and Helen only got home because other people fed and sheltered them. This is the clearest statement in the Telemachy of why xenia exists.
  • The guests are bathed, dressed and seated beside the king, and fed before anything is asked. Helen recognises Telemachus from his likeness to Odysseus, and the questions follow.
  • Menelaus offers horses and a chariot. Telemachus declines tactfully, since Ithaca cannot keep horses, and Menelaus, pleased, gives a silver mixing-bowl with a gold rim instead.
Worth knowing

Helen sits at the table. The Trojan War began when Paris, a guest in Menelaus’s house, left with his host’s wife. The greatest war in Greek memory started as a breach of xenia. The poem does not say so in Book 4; it expects you to know.

Why the suitors matter most

Every act of good hospitality in Books 1–4 is placed against the suitors. They break every rule a guest is bound by, in the house of a man whose own hospitality was famous, and by Book 4 they are plotting to kill the host’s son, which is as far as a guest can go.

In a world where Zeus protects hosts and guests, they have put themselves outside divine protection. The eagle omen in Book 2 and the repeated promises of Odysseus’s return are the poem signalling, well in advance, that this will be punished.

Xenia is the engine of the plot rather than a background custom: the whole second half of the poem is the consequence of what the suitors do here.
Scholarly views to argue with
Section 7

These are positions, not facts. Decide how far the text supports each one, and mark your verdict.

Moses Finley
The World of Odysseus (1954)
Hospitality and gift-giving are at bottom an economic and political system. No gift is free; every gift creates a debt, and the network of guest-friends is how aristocratic households travel, trade and call on allies.
For
The guest-gift is described with care every time, Telemachus negotiates over Menelaus’s gift, and Athene’s claim of inherited friendship works because such bonds carry obligations.
Against
Menelaus’s rebuke of Eteoneus grounds hospitality in fellow-feeling and memory of his own need, not in advantage. Telemachus gains nothing from welcoming an unknown stranger, and does it anyway.
How far does the text support this?
Steve Reece
The Stranger’s Welcome (1993)
Hospitality scenes follow a set sequence of steps, and Homer varies the pattern deliberately. Where a step is missing, delayed or out of order, the poet is telling us something about the host.
For
Telemachus performs every step in order; the suitors fail the first step, noticing, before a word is spoken; Eteoneus’s hesitation is a delayed step that Menelaus corrects.
Against
If the pattern is fixed, a good host may simply be following a script. Is Telemachus admirable, or just well drilled? The poem seems to treat knowing and following the script as itself the virtue.
How far does the text support this?
The older view
Some readers have treated Homeric hospitality as simple good manners, the courtesy of a heroic age.
Test it
The poem makes that hard to sustain: hospitality is enforced by Zeus, its breach drives the plot, and the war itself began with a violated guest-friendship. Ask yourself whether anything in Books 1–4 is presented as mere manners.
Summary
Lesson 1 · Xenia
The lesson in eight lines
  • Xenia is guest-friendship: a binding obligation protected by Zeus, not politeness.
  • xenos means stranger, guest and host; both sides are bound. Theoxeny: the stranger might be a god.
  • Host’s sequence: notice, greet, seat, food before questions, bath and bed, gift, escort.
  • Guest’s duties: accept, answer honestly, respect the house, do not overstay, repay later.
  • Book 1: suitors as owners 1.97–108; stranger unnoticed except by Telemachus 1.104–113; the model welcome 1.113–135; questions and claimed bond 1.170–212; gift offered, goddess revealed 1.272–279.
  • Books 2–4: Antinous’s reversed logic; Nestor feeds first and gives escort; Menelaus rebukes Eteoneus and adapts the gift; Helen and Paris as the original breach.
  • The suitors break every guest rule and put themselves outside Zeus’s protection. Their punishment is set up here.
  • Finley reads xenia as exchange; Reece as a patterned scene. Both can be tested against Menelaus’s reasoning and Telemachus’s unrewarded welcome.
