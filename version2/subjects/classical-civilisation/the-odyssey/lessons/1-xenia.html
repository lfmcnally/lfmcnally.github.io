Lesson 1 · Xenia

Before any character in the Odyssey speaks, the poem shows us a house full of guests behaving like owners. Hospitality is the poem’s first and quickest test of character, and it stays that way to the end.

What you need to know
  • what xenia is, and why it was a matter of survival rather than manners
  • the sequence a host was expected to follow, and the duties a guest owed in return
  • every hospitality scene in Books 1–4, and what each tells us about the people in it
  • why the suitors are the worst breach of xenia in the poem, and what that sets up

Where it happens in Book 1

1.97–108 the suitors in the courtyard   1.104–113 the stranger at the door   1.113–135 the welcome   1.170–212 questions and the claimed bond   1.272–279 bath, gift and revelation

Have your Book 1 workbook open alongside this page. Quotations on this page give the workbook’s own line numbers.

What xenia is
Section 1

Xenia is the Greek system of hospitality: how a stranger was received, and the lasting bond that receiving them created. In Homer it is one of the basic rules holding society together, and the gods enforce it.

It grew out of hard practical facts. There were no inns, no police and no way of sending word ahead, so a traveller depended entirely on whoever opened the door. Everyone travelled, to trade, to fight, to seek news or a marriage, so today’s host was tomorrow’s stranger and the rule protected everyone. And a stranger might be dangerous, or might be a god, which made treating every stranger well the only safe policy.

The words
xenia
hospitality; guest-friendship; the whole system of receiving strangers and the bond it creates
xenos
stranger, guest and host, all in one word. Both sides of the relationship are bound by it
Zeus Xenios
Zeus as protector of strangers and guests. Mistreating a guest is an offence against him
theoxeny
the possibility that a stranger is a god in disguise. In Book 1 this is literally true
guest-gift
the parting gift that seals the relationship and creates a debt to be repaid

Common mistake. Xenia is not politeness. It is a binding social and religious obligation with fixed rules on both sides, and the poem punishes those who break it.

The rules
Section 2

Homeric welcomes follow a recognisable order, and the order matters. The stranger is fed before anyone asks who they are, which shows that hospitality is owed to any stranger and not only to people who turn out to be important. Book 1 supplies an example of almost every step.

What a host had to do

  1. Notice the stranger. A guest left standing at the door is already a failure. Only Telemachus sees Athene waiting; the suitors do not. 1.104–113
  2. Go to them and take their hand or weapon. Telemachus takes Athene’s spear and stands it in the rack beside his father’s. 1.113–121
  3. Seat them, away from noise. A fine chair with a footstool, apart from the suitors’ racket. 1.130–135
  4. Food and drink before anything else. Water for the hands, bread, meat and wine, brought by servants.
  5. Only after the meal, ask who they are. The set questions: name, home, ship. 1.170–177
  6. Offer a bath and a bed. Telemachus offers a bath before Athene leaves. from 1.272
  7. Give a guest-gift on departure. Telemachus offers a keepsake; Athene defers it to her return. 1.272–279
  8. Escort or provisions for the journey on. Nestor gives Telemachus a chariot, horses and his own son as guide. Book 3

What a guest owed in return

  1. Accept what is offered graciously and take no more than is given.
  2. Give an honest account of yourself when asked.
  3. Respect the household: its property, its servants and its women.
  4. Do not outstay your welcome.
  5. Repay the favour later, to the host or to the host’s children.

Gifts

Gifts are the part students most often underrate. A guest-gift is not a souvenir. It is the visible proof that a bond now exists between two households, it creates a debt, and it displays the host’s wealth as it travels with the guest. The best gift is one the guest can actually use and show, which is why Menelaus swaps horses for a silver bowl once Telemachus points out that Ithaca has no room for horses (Book 4).

The bond also outlives the people who made it: sons inherit their fathers’ guest-friends, which is exactly what Athene exploits when, as Mentes, she claims old friendship with Odysseus and Laertes. 1.178–212

Section 3 · Prescribed text

Scene 1 · The suitors in the courtyard

She found the insolent Suitors sitting in front of the door on hides of oxen they themselves had slaughtered, playing with counters; their squires and pages were busy round them, some blending wine and water in the mixing-bowls, and others carving meat in lavish portions and wiping down the tables with sponges before they set them ready.

Book 1, workbook lines 97–102

Unpacking the passage

“insolent”
The first word used of the suitors judges them before any of them has spoken.
“oxen they themselves had slaughtered”
The cattle belong to Odysseus. The suitors are killing their host’s animals without being offered them, which a guest should never do.
“playing with counters”
They are passing the time as if the house were theirs, with no sign that they are waiting for an invitation or intend to leave.
“their squires and pages”
The household’s servants are working for the guests. The suitors are behaving as owners of the house.

This sets the standard of bad xenia before the audience has seen good xenia, so that everything Telemachus does next is measured against it.

Scene 2 · The stranger at the door

The first to see her was Telemachus the godlike youth, who was sitting disconsolate among the Suitors … he caught sight of Athene and set off at once for the porch, ashamed that a stranger should be kept standing at the gates. He went straight up to his visitor, grasped his right hand, took his bronze spear and gave him cordial greetings. ‘Welcome, friend!’ he said. ‘You can tell us what has brought you here when you have had some food.’

Book 1, workbook lines 103–112

Unpacking the passage

“The first to see her”
Noticing the stranger is the host’s first duty. The suitors are sitting in the same courtyard and do not see her at all.
“ashamed that a stranger should be kept standing”
Telemachus feels the failure as his own, even though he is not the one who ignored her.
“grasped his right hand, took his bronze spear”
Taking the guest’s weapon is a sign of trust on both sides, and shows that the guest is safe in the house.
“when you have had some food”
Telemachus states the rule aloud: the guest is fed before anyone asks who they are.

Scene 3 · The welcome

Once inside the lofty hall, he took her spear and put it by one of the great pillars in a wooden rack among the many spears of the patient Odysseus. He then conducted her to a beautiful carved chair, over which he spread a linen cloth, and seated her there with a stool for her feet. For himself he drew up an ornate easy-chair, well away from the crowd of Suitors, for fear that his guest might take offence at the uproar …

Book 1, workbook lines 113–120

Unpacking the passage

“among the many spears of the patient Odysseus”
The guest’s spear is placed beside the master’s own, treating the stranger as a friend of the family. It is also Athene’s spear beside Odysseus’s, which the audience knows and Telemachus does not.
“a beautiful carved chair … a linen cloth … a stool for her feet”
Homer lists each item. The best seat in the house is a visible form of honour (timē).
“well away from the crowd of Suitors”
Telemachus protects his guest from the people who should themselves be behaving as guests.

A maid then brings water in a golden jug for washing, the housekeeper brings bread, and a steward serves meat and wine (workbook lines 123–130). Every step is done in the right order, for a guest Telemachus cannot identify. That is the point of the scene: he does it because the rule applies to everyone.

Scene 4 · The questions and the claimed bond

‘But tell me honestly who you are and where you come from. What is your native town? Who are your parents? And since you certainly cannot have come on foot, what kind of vessel brought you here? … is this your first visit to Ithaca, or has my father received you before – he used to entertain in our house just as often as he visited abroad.’

Book 1, workbook lines 151–157

Unpacking the passage

“who you are and where you come from”
These are the set questions a host asks, and they come only after the meal.
“has my father received you before”
Telemachus is asking whether a guest-friendship already exists between the two families, since such bonds were inherited.
“he used to entertain in our house”
Odysseus was known as a generous host, which makes the suitors’ behaviour in his house worse.

“Mentes” replies with a trading voyage, a cargo of iron and a ship waiting in Rheithron Cove, and says that “the ties between [our families] go a long way back, as the old lord Laertes would tell you” (workbook lines 158–167). A stranger who can name a family connection is no longer really a stranger.

Scene 5 · Bath, gift and revelation

‘… stay a little longer so that you can bathe and refresh yourself. Then you can go to your ship in a happy frame of mind, taking with you as a keepsake from myself something precious and beautiful, the sort of present that one gives to a guest who has become a friend.’

‘No,’ said the bright-eyed goddess. ‘I am eager to be on my way; do not detain me now. As for the gift you kindly suggest, let me take it home with me on my way back. Make it the best you can find, and you won’t lose by the exchange.’

Book 1, workbook lines 270–277

Unpacking the passage

“bathe and refresh yourself”
The bath is the next step in the host’s sequence, offered before the guest leaves.
“a guest who has become a friend”
The gift turns a single visit into a lasting bond between two households.
“you won’t lose by the exchange”
Gifts were expected to be repaid. Athene speaks as a guest who will return the favour.

Athene then vanishes “like a bird through a hole in the roof” (workbook line 279), and Telemachus realises that a god has been with him. He offered everything a host should, and the gift goes unclaimed only because the guest was never mortal. In this scene the possibility of theoxeny turns out to be true.

What Book 1 establishes

  • Telemachus is a good host, and therefore a good man, before he has done anything else in the poem.
  • The suitors are bad guests before any of them has spoken.
  • Hospitality is the poem’s first and quickest test of character, and it stays that way.
Xenia in Books 2–4
Section 4 · Context

Book 2 · Antinous at the assembly

Antinous blames Penelope for the suitors’ long stay: she strung them along, so it is her fault they are still there. Notice the assumption underneath, that guests are entitled to stay until they get what they came for. That is the reverse of a guest’s duty to leave.

Book 3 · Nestor at Pylos

  • Telemachus and Mentor arrive in the middle of a sacrifice. Nestor’s son Pisistratus comes to them at once, seats them, gives them meat and wine, and invites them to join the family’s prayers.
  • Only afterwards does Nestor ask who they are: food before questions.
  • On departure Nestor gives a chariot, horses and Pisistratus as escort to Sparta: gift and safe passage, the final steps.

Book 4 · Menelaus at Sparta

  • A servant, Eteoneus, sees two strangers arrive during a wedding feast and asks whether to admit them or send them on.
  • Menelaus is angry at the question. He and Helen only got home because other people fed and sheltered them. This is the clearest statement in the Telemachy of why xenia exists.
  • The guests are bathed, dressed and seated beside the king, and fed before anything is asked. Helen recognises Telemachus from his likeness to Odysseus, and the questions follow.
  • Menelaus offers horses and a chariot. Telemachus declines tactfully, since Ithaca cannot keep horses, and Menelaus, pleased, gives a silver mixing-bowl with a gold rim instead.

Worth knowing. Helen sits at the table. The Trojan War began when Paris, a guest in Menelaus’s house, left with his host’s wife. The greatest war in Greek memory started as a breach of xenia. The poem does not say so in Book 4; it expects you to know.

Why the suitors matter most

Every act of good hospitality in Books 1–4 is placed against the suitors. They break every rule a guest is bound by, in the house of a man whose own hospitality was famous, and by Book 4 they are plotting to kill the host’s son, which is as far as a guest can go.

In a world where Zeus protects hosts and guests, they have put themselves outside divine protection. The eagle omen in Book 2 and the repeated promises of Odysseus’s return are the poem signalling, well in advance, that this will be punished.

Xenia therefore drives the plot of the poem: the second half, in which Odysseus returns and punishes the suitors, is the consequence of how they behave here.

Scholarly views to argue with
Section 7

These are positions, not facts. Decide how far the text supports each one, and mark your verdict.

Moses Finley
The World of Odysseus (1954)
Hospitality and gift-giving are at bottom an economic and political system. No gift is free; every gift creates a debt, and the network of guest-friends is how aristocratic households travel, trade and call on allies.
For
The guest-gift is described with care every time, Telemachus negotiates over Menelaus’s gift, and Athene’s claim of inherited friendship works because such bonds carry obligations.
Against
Menelaus’s rebuke of Eteoneus grounds hospitality in fellow-feeling and memory of his own need, not in advantage. Telemachus gains nothing from welcoming an unknown stranger, and does it anyway.
How far does the text support this?
Steve Reece
The Stranger’s Welcome (1993)
Hospitality scenes follow a set sequence of steps, and Homer varies the pattern deliberately. Where a step is missing, delayed or out of order, the poet is telling us something about the host.
For
Telemachus performs every step in order; the suitors fail the first step, noticing, before a word is spoken; Eteoneus’s hesitation is a delayed step that Menelaus corrects.
Against
If the pattern is fixed, a good host may simply be following a script. Is Telemachus admirable, or just well drilled? The poem seems to treat knowing and following the script as itself the virtue.
How far does the text support this?
The older view
Some readers have treated Homeric hospitality as simple good manners, the courtesy of a heroic age.
Test it
The poem makes that hard to sustain: hospitality is enforced by Zeus, its breach drives the plot, and the war itself began with a violated guest-friendship. Ask yourself whether anything in Books 1–4 is presented as mere manners.
Summary
Lesson 1 · Xenia
Key points
  • Xenia is guest-friendship, a binding social and religious obligation protected by Zeus, with fixed duties on both sides.
  • The word xenos can mean stranger, guest or host, and both sides of the relationship are bound by its rules. Theoxeny is the possibility that a stranger is a god in disguise.
  • A host was expected to notice the stranger, greet them, give them a seat, feed them before asking any questions, offer a bath and a bed, give a gift on departure and help them on their way.
  • A guest was expected to accept what was offered, give an honest account of themselves, respect the household, leave in good time and repay the favour later.
  • In Book 1 the suitors behave as owners of the house 1.97–108, and only Telemachus notices the stranger at the door 1.104–113. He then gives a model welcome 1.113–135, asks the set questions, which lead to Athene’s claim of an inherited bond 1.170–212, and offers a gift before she leaves as a bird 1.272–279.
  • In Books 2–4 Antinous argues that the suitors are entitled to stay; Nestor feeds his guests before questioning them and sends them on with an escort; Menelaus rebukes Eteoneus for hesitating and adapts his gift to suit Telemachus; and Helen’s presence recalls Paris’s breach of xenia, which began the war.
  • The suitors break every rule a guest is bound by, which puts them outside Zeus’s protection and prepares for their punishment in the second half of the poem.
  • Finley reads xenia as a system of exchange and obligation, while Reece reads hospitality scenes as a set pattern that Homer varies. Both views can be tested against Menelaus’s reasons for welcoming strangers and against Telemachus’s welcome of a guest who can offer him nothing.
