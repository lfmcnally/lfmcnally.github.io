Lesson 6 · Disguise and recognition

Almost no one in the Odyssey is who they appear to be, at least at first. Gods walk among mortals looking like old friends. A hero enters a city dressed as a beggar. A dream wears a sister’s face.

What you need to know
  • why disguise is so common in the Odyssey, for gods and for mortals
  • the three ways a hidden identity is recognised: by likeness, by sign, by revelation
  • every disguise and recognition in Books 1–4, and what each changes
  • how the Telemachy prepares the audience for the disguises still to come

Where it happens in Book 1

1.96–105 Athene becomes Mentes   1.178–212 a false life story   1.278–279 departure as a bird   1.348–366 the secret kept

A poem of hidden identities
Section 1

The poem is built on the gap between how someone looks and who they are, and on the moments when that gap closes. Every disguise sets up a recognition, and the poem’s most powerful scenes are the ones where a hidden identity is finally seen. The Telemachy contains the first of them.

The words
disguise
any deliberate concealment of identity: a god in mortal form, a hero in rags, a name withheld
anagnorisis
recognition: the moment a hidden identity is discovered; the term later Greek critics used for it
theoxeny
a god disguised as a guest: the reason the rule of hospitality applies to everyone (see Lesson 1)

Three routes to recognition

By likenessA family resemblance gives someone away, as Telemachus’s does twice, in Books 3 and 4.
By signAn object, a scar or a shared secret proves who someone is. Mostly in the later books.
By revelationThe disguised person, usually a god, simply drops the disguise, often in a way that leaves no doubt.

Highlighted: the two routes the Telemachy uses.

Why the gods hide
Section 2

Athene could arrive on Ithaca in a blaze of light and order Telemachus to sail. She does not. She appears as a plausible old friend and lets Telemachus reach his decision through conversation. The disguise does several things at once.

  1. It tests his hospitality without his knowing he is being tested.
  2. It lets the plan seem to be his own, so the decision remains his.
  3. It keeps the poem’s mortals responsible for their choices: helped, not puppeted.
  4. It gives the later recognition its weight. When Athene departs as a bird, Telemachus learns that a god took an interest in him, and that changes how he thinks about himself.
Common mistake

The gods do not disguise themselves because they have to. They could appear openly. They choose disguise because of what it allows the mortals to do.

Disguise and recognition in Book 1
Section 3 · Prescribed text
3.1Athene becomes Mentes1.96–105

The goddess takes the appearance of a Taphian chieftain and old friend of Odysseus. The disguise is specific and credible: a name, a homeland, a reason to be passing through. Telemachus has no cause to doubt it and does not.

3.2A false life story1.178–212

Asked who she is, “Mentes” supplies a full account: a trading voyage, a cargo of iron, a crew waiting at the ship, old friendship with Laertes. A disguise in the Odyssey is never just a face. It comes with a story, and the story is what makes it work.

3.3Departure as a bird1.278–279

Her advice given, Athene vanishes, flying off like a bird. This is the Telemachy’s first recognition by revelation. Telemachus realises his guest was divine, and the poem marks the change in him at once, in his new confidence with his mother and the suitors.

3.4The secret kept1.348–366

Asked by Eurymachus about the visitor, Telemachus says only that it was an old friend of his father’s. He has recognised a god and chooses not to say so. The suitors, who saw the same stranger, recognised nothing.

Setting up the rest of the poem

The Telemachy teaches the audience how disguise and recognition work before the biggest example arrives. A god has been recognised by a bird’s flight, a son by his father’s face, a hero, in a story, by a woman’s sharp eye. Each prepares the ground for Odysseus’s own return, when he will come home in a disguise no one on Ithaca expects and be recognised one person at a time.

In a poem about homecoming, being recognised is how you know you are home.
Scholarly views to argue with
Section 7

These are positions, not facts. Decide how far the text supports each one, and mark your verdict.

Sheila Murnaghan
Disguise and Recognition in the Odyssey (1987)
Disguise and recognition are the poem’s central structure, not a decorative device. Identity in the Odyssey is social: a person is who they are recognised to be, and recognition scenes are where the social order is tested and restored.
For
Telemachus is recognised twice by his father’s face and gains standing each time; Athene’s revelations raise him in his own and Nestor’s eyes; Odysseus, unrecognised on Calypso’s island, is as good as no one.
Against
If identity is only what others recognise, Odysseus has no identity while he is away, which the poem plainly does not believe. His fame survives his absence. Recognition may confirm identity without constituting it.
How far does the text support this?
Aristotle
Poetics
Recognition, anagnorisis, is one of the most powerful things a plot can do, because it produces a reversal in the characters’ situation and in the audience’s feeling at the same moment.
For
The bird at 1.278–279 turns Telemachus from a passive boy into someone who asserts authority in the same scene; Helen’s recognition of Telemachus turns a stranger into an honoured guest instantly.
Against
In Books 1–4 the recognitions are small and mostly happy. The great reversals Aristotle has in mind are still to come, so his idea describes what the Telemachy sets up more than what it delivers.

Summary
Lesson 6 · Disguise and recognition
The lesson in seven lines
  • Disguise creates the question; recognition answers it. The poem runs on the distance between the two.
  • Three routes to recognition: likeness, sign, revelation. Books 1–4 use the first and third.
  • Gods disguise themselves by choice: to test, to persuade indirectly, to keep mortals responsible, and to give the revelation weight.
  • Book 1: Mentes with a full false history 1.96–105 1.178–212; the bird 1.278–279 and Telemachus’s changed behaviour; the secret kept from Eurymachus.
  • Books 2–4: Mentor; likeness noted by Nestor and Helen; the bird at Pylos; the beggar in Troy; sealskins and Proteus; the dream as Iphthime.
  • Books 1–4 train the audience to read disguises before Odysseus uses one.
  • Murnaghan makes recognition constitutive of identity; Aristotle makes it the engine of reversal. Test both against Odysseus’s surviving fame and the small scale of the Telemachy’s recognitions.
