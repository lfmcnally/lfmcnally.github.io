Lesson 6 · Disguise and recognition

Many characters in the Odyssey are not who they first appear to be: gods visit mortals in the form of old friends, Odysseus enters Troy dressed as a beggar, and Athene sends Penelope a dream in the shape of her sister.

What you need to know
  • why disguise is so common in the Odyssey, for gods and for mortals
  • the three ways a hidden identity is recognised: by likeness, by sign, by revelation
  • every disguise and recognition in Books 1–4, and what each changes
  • how the Telemachy prepares the audience for the disguises still to come

Where it happens in Book 1

1.96–105 Athene becomes Mentes   1.178–212 a false life story   1.278–279 departure as a bird   Bk 1 the secret kept from Eurymachus

A poem of hidden identities
Section 1

Almost no one in the Odyssey is who they appear to be, at least at first. Gods walk among mortals looking like old friends, a hero enters a city dressed as a beggar, a dream wears a sister’s face, and the sea-god Proteus will not hold a single shape. The poem is built on the gap between how someone looks and who they are, and on the moments when that gap closes. Every disguise sets up a recognition, and the poem’s most powerful scenes are the ones where a hidden identity is finally seen. The Telemachy contains the first of them.

The words
disguise
any deliberate concealment of identity: a god in mortal form, a hero in rags, a name withheld
recognition (anagnorisis)
the moment a hidden identity is discovered; anagnorisis is the term later Greek critics used for it
theoxeny
a god disguised as a guest: the reason the rule of hospitality applies to everyone (see Lesson 1)

Three routes to recognition

By likenessA family resemblance gives someone away, as Telemachus’s does twice, in Books 3 and 4.
By signAn object, a scar or a shared secret proves who someone is. This route belongs mostly to the later books.
By revelationThe disguised person, usually a god, simply drops the disguise, often in a way that leaves no doubt.

Highlighted: the two routes the Telemachy uses.

Why the gods hide
Section 2

Athene could arrive on Ithaca in a blaze of light and order Telemachus to sail. Instead she appears as a plausible old friend and lets Telemachus reach his decision through conversation. The disguise does several things at once.

  1. It tests his hospitality without his knowing he is being tested.
  2. It lets the plan seem to be his own, so the decision remains his.
  3. It keeps the poem’s mortals responsible for their choices: the gods help them without controlling them.
  4. It gives the later recognition its weight. When Athene departs as a bird, Telemachus learns that a god took an interest in him, and that changes how he thinks about himself.

Common mistake. Students sometimes write that the gods have to disguise themselves. They could appear openly whenever they wished, and they choose disguise because of what it allows the mortals to do.

Disguise and recognition in Book 1
Section 3 · Prescribed text

Book 1 contains one complete disguise, from the moment Athene takes on a mortal form to the moment she drops it, and one recognition that Telemachus keeps to himself. Follow it through in four steps.

Scene 1 · Athene becomes Mentes

She flashed down from the heights of Olympus, and on reaching Ithaca she took her stand on the threshold of the court in front of Odysseus’ house; and to look like a visitor she assumed the appearance of a family friend, the Taphian chieftain Mentes, bronze spear in hand.

Book 1, workbook lines 92–96

Unpacking the passage

“to look like a visitor”
The disguise has a purpose. Athene arrives as a guest, so the first thing the household must do is decide how to receive her.
“a family friend”
She chooses a face that Telemachus has every reason to trust and welcome.
“the Taphian chieftain Mentes”
The disguise has a name, a people and a rank. It is specific enough to be believed.
“bronze spear in hand”
A travelling chieftain would carry a spear, so the detail fits the disguise. The audience also knows it is the spear Athene uses to break the ranks of warriors (workbook lines 89–92).

The disguise is specific and credible: a name, a homeland and a reason to be passing through. Telemachus has no cause to doubt it, and he does not.

Scene 2 · The Stranger’s Tale

‘I will tell you everything honestly,’ answered the bright-eyed goddess Athene. ‘My father was the wise prince Anchialus. My own name is Mentes, and I am chieftain of the sea-faring Taphians. As for my arrival in Ithaca, I came with my own ship and crew across the wine-dark sea. We are bound for the foreign port of Temesa with a cargo of gleaming iron, which we mean to trade for bronze. … As for our families, the ties between them go a long way back, as the old lord Laertes would tell you if you went and asked him.’

Book 1, workbook lines 158–167

Unpacking the passage

“I will tell you everything honestly”
The goddess promises honesty at the start of a story that is false from beginning to end. The audience knows this, and Telemachus does not.
“My father was the wise prince Anchialus”
She answers the questions Telemachus has just asked, about parents, home town and ship, one by one, as a real guest would.
“a cargo of gleaming iron, which we mean to trade for bronze”
Ordinary practical details of a trading voyage make the story easy to believe.
“as the old lord Laertes would tell you”
She names someone who could confirm the friendship, which makes the claim sound safe to check.

A disguise in the Odyssey comes with a story as well as a face, and the story is what makes it work. “Mentes” supplies a full account: a trading voyage, a cargo of iron, a crew waiting at the ship and an old friendship with Laertes.

Scene 3 · Departure as a bird

The goddess spoke and the next moment she was gone, vanishing like a bird through a hole in the roof. In Telemachus’ heart she had implanted spirit and daring, and had brought the image of his father to his mind even more strongly than before. He felt the change and was overcome with awe, for he realized a god had been with him. Then, godlike himself, he rejoined the Suitors.

Book 1, workbook lines 278–283

Unpacking the passage

“vanishing like a bird through a hole in the roof”
Athene drops the disguise in a way no mortal guest could. This is recognition by revelation.
“implanted spirit and daring”
The visit has changed Telemachus as well as informing him.
“he realized a god had been with him”
The moment of recognition itself. Telemachus works out who his guest was only after she has gone.
“godlike himself”
Knowing that a god has taken an interest in him changes how Telemachus carries himself when he goes back among the suitors.

This is the Telemachy’s first recognition by revelation. Telemachus realises his guest was divine, and the poem shows the change in him at once, in his new confidence with his mother and with the suitors.

Scene 4 · The secret kept from Eurymachus

‘… do tell us something about that guest of yours. Where did the man come from? … He looked distinguished, but he jumped up and was gone so suddenly that he gave one no time to get to know him.’

‘… As for my guest, he is an old friend of my father from Taphos. He introduced himself as Mentes, the son of wise Anchialus, and chieftain of the sea-faring Taphians.’ This is what Telemachus said, but in his heart he knew her for an immortal goddess.

Book 1, workbook lines 353–366

Unpacking the passage

“He looked distinguished, but he jumped up and was gone so suddenly”
Eurymachus saw the same stranger and noticed the sudden departure, but he did not recognise a god.
“He introduced himself as Mentes”
Telemachus repeats the stranger’s own account almost exactly (compare workbook lines 159–160) and adds nothing of what he has realised.
“in his heart he knew her for an immortal goddess”
The narrator tells us plainly that Telemachus has recognised Athene and has chosen to keep it to himself.

When Eurymachus asks about the visitor, Telemachus says only that it was an old friend of his father’s. He has recognised a god and chooses not to say so, while the suitors, who saw the same stranger, recognised nothing.

Disguise and recognition in Books 2–4
Section 4 · Context

Book 2

  • Athene appears as Mentor, another trusted friend of Odysseus, and helps arrange the ship. She uses a different mortal face and the same method.

Book 3

  • Nestor remarks that Telemachus resembles Odysseus in look and speech. This is recognition by likeness, and it raises the young man’s standing.
  • Athene departs as a bird in front of the whole court. In Book 1 Telemachus recognised her alone; this revelation is public, and Nestor orders a sacrifice.

Book 4

  • Helen names Telemachus on sight, from his likeness to his father, before anyone has told her who he is, and Menelaus agrees. Telemachus is not in disguise, and he has not yet introduced himself; his face identifies him.
  • Helen recalls Odysseus entering Troy disguised as a beggar. She recognised him and no one else did. The story rehearses the disguise the audience will see him use later on Ithaca.
  • To learn his way home, Menelaus had to catch Proteus, a god who changes shape at will. To catch him, Menelaus first disguised himself and his men as seals under sealskins, and he won by holding on until Proteus ran out of shapes.
  • Athene sends Penelope a dream wearing the face of her sister Iphthime. Penelope half-recognises it as more than a dream and asks about Odysseus, and the phantom refuses to answer. Recognition is offered and then withheld.

Setting up the rest of the poem

The Telemachy teaches the audience how disguise and recognition work before the biggest example arrives. A god has been recognised by a bird’s flight, a son by his father’s face, and a hero, in a story, by a woman’s sharp eye. Each prepares the ground for Odysseus’s own return, when he will come home in a disguise no one on Ithaca expects and be recognised one person at a time.

Because the poem is about homecoming, recognition matters a great deal: Odysseus’s return is only complete once the people of his household know who he is.

Scholarly views to argue with
Section 7

These are positions for you to test against the text. Decide how far the text supports each one, and mark your verdict.

Sheila Murnaghan
Disguise and Recognition in the Odyssey (1987)
Disguise and recognition are the poem’s central structure. Identity in the Odyssey is social: a person is who they are recognised to be, and recognition scenes are where the social order is tested and restored.
For
Telemachus is recognised twice by his father’s face and gains standing each time; Athene’s revelations raise him in his own and Nestor’s eyes; Odysseus, unrecognised on Calypso’s island, is as good as no one.
Against
If identity is only what others recognise, Odysseus has no identity while he is away, which the poem plainly does not believe. His fame survives his absence. Recognition may confirm identity without constituting it.
How far does the text support this?
Aristotle
Poetics
Recognition, anagnorisis, is one of the most powerful things a plot can do, because it produces a reversal in the characters’ situation and in the audience’s feeling at the same moment.
For
The bird at 1.278–279 turns Telemachus from a passive boy into someone who asserts authority in the same scene; Helen’s recognition of Telemachus turns a stranger into an honoured guest instantly.
Against
In Books 1–4 the recognitions are small and mostly happy. The great reversals Aristotle has in mind are still to come, so his idea describes what the Telemachy sets up more than what it delivers.

Summary
Lesson 6 · Disguise and recognition
Key points
  • Every disguise in the poem sets up a later recognition, and much of its tension comes from the gap between who a character appears to be and who they really are.
  • Recognition can happen by likeness, by sign or by revelation. Books 1–4 use likeness and revelation; recognition by sign comes later in the poem.
  • The gods choose to disguise themselves in order to test mortals, to persuade them indirectly, to leave them responsible for their own decisions, and to give the eventual revelation more weight.
  • In Book 1 Athene appears as Mentes with a full false history 1.96–105 1.178–212 and leaves like a bird 1.278–279, after which Telemachus behaves with new confidence and keeps what he has realised from Eurymachus.
  • In Books 2–4 Athene appears as Mentor; Nestor and Helen both recognise Telemachus by his likeness to his father; Athene leaves Pylos as a bird; Helen tells of Odysseus disguised as a beggar in Troy; Menelaus hides under sealskins to catch Proteus; and Athene sends Penelope a dream in the form of her sister Iphthime.
  • These episodes prepare the audience for Odysseus’s own disguise when he returns to Ithaca.
  • Murnaghan argues that being recognised is what gives a person their identity in the poem, while Aristotle sees recognition as the moment that brings about a reversal. Both views can be tested against the survival of Odysseus’s fame while he is absent, and against how small the recognitions in the Telemachy are.
