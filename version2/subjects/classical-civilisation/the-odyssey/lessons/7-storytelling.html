Lesson 7 · Storytelling

The Odyssey is a story that constantly tells stories. A bard sings in the hall, old men narrate the past, and a disguised goddess invents a life history on the spot. In this world telling is a form of action, with purposes and consequences, so the question to ask of every story is who is telling it and whether to believe them.

What you need to know
  • who the bard was in Homeric society, and how oral poetry worked
  • the four layers of telling in the poem: poet, bard, character, liar
  • the storytellers of Books 1–4 and what each gains by telling
  • how far the stories inside the poem can be trusted

Where it happens in Book 1

1.1–10 the invocation of the Muse   from 1.138 Phemius sings for the suitors   1.178–212 Athene’s invented life   the song of the returns, Penelope and Telemachus (workbook lines 284–319)

A poem full of tellers
Section 1

A bard sings in the hall in Book 1. Nestor spends most of Book 3 narrating the past. Helen and Menelaus compete to tell the better tale in Book 4. Even Athene, disguised, invents a life history on the spot.

Odysseus becomes the poem’s greatest storyteller in the books that follow, but long before that the Telemachy has shown that in this world telling is a form of action, with purposes and consequences. The poem is also aware of itself. It opens by asking the Muse to tell the tale through the poet, so from the first line it is a story about how stories get told.

The words
aoidos
the bard: a professional singer who performed tales of gods and heroes to music, from memory, usually at feasts. Phemius on Ithaca is one; Demodocus at Phaeacia is another
the Muse
the goddess of inspiration whom the poet asks to sing through him. Invoking her claims a divine source for what follows
oral tradition
composition and transmission by performance instead of writing, which explains the repeated phrases and standard scenes
unreliable narrator
a teller whose version is shaped by what they want the listener to believe
Four layers of telling
Section 2

It helps to separate who is speaking before deciding what to believe.

  1. The poet narrates the poem and claims the Muse’s authority. He asks to be believed.
  2. The bard inside the poem, Phemius, sings professionally to an audience in the hall. He asks to be enjoyed and rewarded, and he sings for whoever holds the hall.
  3. Characters tell stories about the past: Nestor, Menelaus, Helen. Their accounts are coloured by memory and motive. They ask to be understood in a certain way.
  4. Liars tell false stories on purpose. Athene’s life as Mentes is a fiction with a job to do. She asks not to be found out.

Common mistake. When Helen or Menelaus narrates, you are hearing that character’s version, shaped by what they want the listener to think. Do not treat it as the poem telling you what happened.

Storytelling in Book 1
Section 3 · Prescribed text

Book 1 contains all four layers of telling: the poet calling on the Muse, the bard singing in the hall, characters reacting to his song, and a goddess telling a false story about herself.

Scene 1 · The invocation

Tell me, Muse, the story of that resourceful man who was driven to wander far and wide after he had sacked the holy citadel of Troy. He saw the cities of many people and he learnt their ways. He suffered great anguish on the high seas in his struggles to preserve his life and bring his comrades home. … Tell us this story, goddess daughter of Zeus, beginning at whatever point you will.

Book 1, workbook lines 1–10

Unpacking the passage

“Tell me, Muse”
The poem’s first words are a request to be told the story. The poet presents himself as the channel for a goddess, which claims divine authority for everything that follows.
“that resourceful man”
The subject is described before he is named. The poem defines its hero by his quality, the man of many turns.
“Tell us this story”
The request moves from “me” to “us”, so the audience is included as listeners alongside the poet.
“beginning at whatever point you will”
The Muse chooses where the story starts. Even the order of telling is presented as a decision someone makes.

This is the first layer of telling: the poet, who asks to be believed. From its opening line the poem draws attention to the act of telling a story.

Scene 2 · Phemius sings for the suitors

… when all had satisfied their hunger and thirst, the Suitors turned their thoughts to other activities, music and dancing, which add to the pleasures of a banquet. A herald brought a beautiful lyre and handed it to Phemius, the minstrel whom they had forced into their service. He had just struck the first notes for some delightful song …

Book 1, workbook lines 135–140

Unpacking the passage

“which add to the pleasures of a banquet”
Song belongs to the feast. The bard performs at the meal, as the glossary describes.
“forced into their service”
Phemius is a professional who plays because he must. His audience is the men wrecking the house.
“some delightful song”
The bard’s job is to please his listeners, whoever they are.

The poem shows the bard’s position as dependent: he sings for the people in power, whoever they happen to be. Later in the book Telemachus himself calls it “a lovely thing to listen to a minstrel such as we have here, with a voice like a god’s” (workbook lines 325–326).

Scene 3 · Athene’s invented life

‘I will tell you everything honestly,’ answered the bright-eyed goddess Athene. ‘My father was the wise prince Anchialus. My own name is Mentes, and I am chieftain of the sea-faring Taphians. … We are bound for the foreign port of Temesa with a cargo of gleaming iron, which we mean to trade for bronze. … As for our families, the ties between them go a long way back, as the old lord Laertes would tell you if you went and asked him.’

Book 1, workbook lines 158–167

Unpacking the passage

“I will tell you everything honestly”
The false story opens with a promise of honesty, answering Telemachus’s own request to “tell me honestly who you are”.
“a cargo of gleaming iron, which we mean to trade for bronze”
The fiction is full of concrete detail: a father’s name, a people, a port and a cargo. Detail makes it convincing.
“as the old lord Laertes would tell you”
She names a witness from Telemachus’s own family, which ties the invented visitor into the household’s past.

This is a complete false story, delivered smoothly, and no one questions it. It has a job to do: it wins Telemachus’s trust so that her advice will move him. The first character in the Telemachy to tell a story about themselves is a god lying for good reasons, and she is the fourth layer of telling, the liar who asks not to be found out.

Scene 4 · The song of the returns

He found them listening in silence to a song which their famous bard was singing to them about the Achaeans’ return from Troy and the disasters that Pallas Athene made them suffer. …

‘Phemius,’ she said, ‘with your knowledge of the ballads that poets have made about the deeds of men or gods you could enchant us with many other tales, rather than this. Choose one of those now for your audience here, and let them drink their wine in peace. But give us no more of your present song. It is too sad; it never fails to wring my heart.’

Book 1, workbook lines 284–300

Unpacking the passage

“listening in silence”
The suitors are absorbed in the song. A bard holds the attention of the hall.
“the Achaeans’ return from Troy”
Phemius sings of the grim homecomings, the subject the Odyssey itself belongs to, while Odysseus has still not come home.
“many other tales”
Penelope knows a bard carries a whole stock of stories and can choose among them.
“it never fails to wring my heart”
A song about the past can hurt a listener who is living through its consequences.

Scene 5 · Telemachus defends the bard

But the prudent Telemachus intervened. ‘Mother,’ he said, ‘why grudge our loyal bard the right to entertain us as the spirit moves him? Surely it is not the poets who are responsible for what happens, but Zeus himself, who deals with each of us toilers on earth as he sees fit? We cannot blame Phemius if he chooses to sing of the Danaan’s tragic fate, for it is always the latest song that an audience applauds the most.’

Book 1, workbook lines 303–309

Unpacking the passage

“as the spirit moves him”
Telemachus defends the singer’s freedom to choose his subject, against the wishes of his own mother.
“it is not the poets who are responsible for what happens”
The bard is not to blame for the sorrow in his songs. Telemachus places responsibility for what happens on Zeus.
“it is always the latest song that an audience applauds the most”
Audiences most want to hear the newest song. The Odyssey was once the newest song.

This is the closest the Telemachy comes to a statement about poetry: bards deserve respect, their subject is not their fault, and the newest song is the one people most want to hear. Penelope goes back upstairs and weeps for Odysseus until she sleeps (workbook lines 315–319).

What Book 1 establishes

  • The poem calls attention to its own telling from the first line.
  • The bard inside the poem is dependent on whoever holds the hall, and he is still owed respect.
  • The first story told by a character is a deliberate fiction, and it works.
Storytelling in Books 2–4
Section 4 · Context

Book 3 · Nestor, the long memory

Asked for news, Nestor gives history instead: the quarrels after Troy, the separate departures, Agamemnon’s death and Orestes’s revenge. He tells at length and with purpose, holding Orestes up as a model. His stories are true, as far as the poem lets us judge, but they are also arguments aimed at Telemachus. Nestor is the teller as teacher.

Book 4 · Two versions of Troy

  • Before the stories begin, Helen puts a drug in the wine that takes away grief, so that painful memories can be told without pain. The Telemachy knows that stories about the past hurt.
  • Helen tells how she alone recognised Odysseus in Troy and kept his secret. Her story makes her his ally.
  • Menelaus tells how, inside the Wooden Horse, Odysseus held a man silent while Helen tried to lure the Greeks out by imitating their wives. His story makes her the danger.
  • Both praise Odysseus. They do not praise Helen equally, and the poem lets the two stories sit side by side without judging. The audience is left to weigh them.

Book 4 · News at third hand

Menelaus’s account of his own return, with Egypt, the sealskins and the shape-changing god, is the longest first-person adventure story in the Telemachy. It also contains the only hard news of Odysseus that anyone brings Telemachus, and it is delivered as a story within a story: Menelaus tells what Proteus told him. Even the truth arrives as someone’s narrative.

Can the stories be trusted?
Section 6

Some of the stories can be trusted more than others. Athene’s is a deliberate fiction. Helen’s and Menelaus’s cannot both be the whole truth. Nestor’s may be accurate but is plainly selective.

The poem invites its audience to notice who is speaking, to whom, and why, and it rewards listeners who do. This is preparation for what comes later. The poem’s greatest storyteller, Odysseus himself, has not yet taken the floor, and when he does the audience will need exactly these habits.

Remember. For any story in the poem, ask three questions: who is speaking, to whom, and why.

Scholarly views to argue with
Section 7

These are positions for you to test against the text. Decide how far the text supports each one, and mark your verdict.

Milman Parry and Albert Lord
Studies in oral composition (1930s–1960)
Homeric poetry was composed orally, in performance, from stock phrases and scene-patterns that let a singer build a vast poem without a script. Repetition is the tradition’s toolkit and should not be read as laziness on the poet’s part.
For
The repeated epithets and formulas; the standard hospitality sequence; Phemius composing in the hall exactly as Homer would have done.
Against
If the poem is assembled from formulas, how much is design and how much is habit? Critics who stress the Odyssey’s careful structure, its in medias res opening and its parallel scenes, think Parry and Lord underrate the poet’s control.
How far does the text support this?
Irene de Jong
A Narratological Commentary on the Odyssey (2001)
The stories characters tell are embedded narratives with their own narrators, audiences and purposes, and the poem uses them deliberately to characterise the tellers as much as to convey information.
For
Nestor teaches through Orestes; Helen and Menelaus each shape Troy to flatter themselves; Athene’s false biography is built to move Telemachus.
Against
A reader could object that this treats a traditional oral poem as if it were a modern novel. Are Helen and Menelaus consciously unreliable, or is the poet simply preserving two traditional versions side by side?
How far does the text support this?
Gregory Nagy
The Best of the Achaeans (1979)
Kleos is song. A hero’s fame exists only as long as bards perform it, so the poem is about heroes and is also itself the instrument of their glory.
For
Telemachus’s lament that no one will tell his father’s story; Phemius singing the returns; the poem’s self-conscious invocation of the Muse.
Against
If fame depends on singers, it depends on their honesty and their audience. Phemius sings for the suitors. Whose glory is a bard in a hostile hall preserving?
How far does the text support this?
Summary
Lesson 7 · Storytelling
Key points
  • Telling is a form of action in the Odyssey, with purposes and consequences, and the poem opens by asking the Muse to tell it.
  • There are four layers of telling: the poet, who asks to be believed; the bard, who asks to be enjoyed; characters, who ask to be understood in a certain way; and liars, who ask not to be caught.
  • In Book 1 the poet invokes the Muse 1.1–10 and Phemius sings for the suitors from 1.138. When he sings of the returns, Penelope weeps and asks for another song, and Telemachus defends the bard’s right to choose his subject. Athene, as Mentes, tells an invented life story that no one questions 1.178–212.
  • In Books 2–4 Nestor teaches Telemachus through history, Helen drugs the wine so that grief can be spoken of without pain, Helen and Menelaus tell two versions of Troy that cannot both be the whole truth, and the news of Odysseus arrives at third hand, from Proteus through Menelaus.
  • The stories are not all equally reliable. The poem trains its audience to ask who is speaking, to whom, and why, which prepares them for Odysseus as a storyteller.
  • Parry and Lord on oral formula, de Jong on embedded narrators and Nagy on kleos as song can each be tested against Phemius singing for the men wrecking the house.
