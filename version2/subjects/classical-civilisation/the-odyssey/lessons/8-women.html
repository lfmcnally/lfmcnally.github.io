Women in Society

What women could do, and what the poem shows them doing.
Topic Tester →
Lesson 8Book 1 · prescribedBooks 2–4 · context

Start here

Homeric society was run by men in public, and women’s sphere was the household. The Odyssey takes its women seriously as agents while showing very clearly the limits placed on them, and in the Telemachy their power works through the household, through marriage, through private speech and, above all, through intelligence.

What you need to know
  • the position of women in Homeric society: the household, marriage, and the limits on public life
  • how the mortal women of Books 1–4 compare: Penelope, Helen, Eurycleia, Clytemnestra
  • how goddesses act under none of the same limits, and why the contrast matters
  • whether the poem is sympathetic to its women or simply reflects the values of its time

The household world

Homeric society was run by men in public. The assembly in Book 2 is entirely male, and kings, warriors, bards and hosts are men. When Telemachus tells his mother that speech in the hall is men’s business, he is stating a rule everyone present accepts.

Women’s sphere was the oikos, the household: its stores, its servants, its weaving, and the raising of children. Women in the poem still have power, but it works differently: through the household, through marriage, through speech in private and, above all in the Telemachy, through intelligence. The Odyssey takes women seriously as agents while showing very clearly the limits placed on them.

The words
oikos
the household as a whole: the unit a woman ran and a man owned
dowry
wealth given in marriage. Marriage was arranged between men, and a widow could be sent back to her father to be remarried, which is exactly what Athene and later Antinous propose for Penelope
weaving
the defining women’s skill, practical and symbolic: the household’s cloth, and in Penelope’s hands its cleverest weapon
double standard
fidelity expected of wives and not of husbands, which the poem shows without comment

Common mistake. Students often call Penelope passive because she waits. In her situation waiting is an active choice, sustained for twenty years against constant pressure, and she defends it with a trick that outwits a hundred men for three of those years.

The women of Books 1–4

Pe
Penelope: the constrained centre
Wife of Odysseus and mother of Telemachus. The most important mortal in the Telemachy.

Almost every choice Penelope makes is hemmed in. She cannot remarry without accepting that Odysseus is dead. She cannot refuse the suitors outright without inviting violence against her son. She cannot leave without ending the household. What she can do is delay, and she does it brilliantly: the three-year web trick holds the suitors off by wit alone and fails only through a servant’s betrayal.

In Book 1 she is also shown being overruled. When she asks Phemius to change his song, Telemachus sends her upstairs and tells her that speech in the hall belongs to men. She goes, and the poem notes that she took his words to heart. Her son’s first act of authority is exercised over her.

He
Helen: the woman who came back
Wife of Menelaus, queen of Sparta. The cause of the Trojan War.

Helen left her husband for a guest of the house, and ten years of fighting followed. In Book 4 she is home in Sparta, reconciled with Menelaus, gracious, intelligent and entirely at ease. She recognises Telemachus on sight, drugs the wine so that grief can be spoken of without pain, and tells a story that casts her as Odysseus’s secret ally. Her husband’s version of Troy casts her as the danger, and the poem lets both stand. Helen is the poem’s test of how far a woman can be blamed and forgiven at once.

Cl
Clytemnestra: the counter-example
Wife of Agamemnon. She never appears, but she is named in every book that mentions his death.

Seduced by Aegisthus while her husband was at Troy, Clytemnestra is the wife who did not wait. Nestor remarks in Book 3 that she was a woman of good sense until Aegisthus wore her down. She is the standing warning of what Penelope’s situation could have become: the anti-Penelope.

Eu
Eurycleia: the loyal servant
Odysseus’s old nurse, a slave, and one of the household’s most trusted members.

Eurycleia was bought as a girl by Odysseus’s father Laertes, who honoured her like a wife but never slept with her, to spare his own wife’s anger. She lights Telemachus to bed in Book 1 from 1.372, folds his clothes, and is the one person he swears to silence before he sails in Book 2. Even her short biography is defined by marriage and its rules, and her standing rests entirely on service and loyalty.

Goddesses

Against the mortal women stand female gods who act under none of the same constraints. Athene travels where she likes, disguises herself as men, sits in the divine council and argues with Zeus, and drives the entire plot of the Telemachy. Calypso holds the poem’s hero captive for seven years by her own will. Eidothea, a sea-nymph, tells Menelaus how to trap her own father.

The contrast is pointed. A goddess speaks in council, while a queen is sent upstairs. A nymph keeps a hero, while a wife waits for one. The poem does not comment, but it puts the two kinds of female power side by side and lets the audience notice.

Key idea. Female power in the poem is either divine or indirect, and mortal women only have the indirect kind.

Women in Book 1

Book 1 moves from goddesses who act freely to mortal women who are advised about, overruled and served. Read the scenes in order and notice how the limits tighten.

Scene 1 · Calypso

All the survivors of the war had reached their homes by now and so put the perils of battle and the sea behind them. Odysseus alone was prevented from returning to the home and wife he yearned for by that powerful goddess, the Nymph Calypso, who longed for him to marry her, and kept him in her vaulted cave.

Book 1, workbook lines 11–15

Unpacking the passage

“the home and wife he yearned for”
Penelope is present only as the wife Odysseus wants to return to. She is not yet named.
“that powerful goddess”
The first woman named in the poem is described by her power.
“longed for him to marry her, and kept him”
Calypso acts on her own desire and holds a man against his will. No mortal woman in the poem could do this.

Before Penelope is mentioned, the audience meets female power at its least constrained.

Scene 2 · Athene in council

The goddess of the flashing eyes, Athene, answered him at once: ‘Father of ours, Son of Cronos, King of Kings, Aegisthus’ end is just what he deserved. May all who act as he did share his fate! It is for Odysseus that my heart is wrung, the wise and unlucky Odysseus, who has been parted so long from all his friends and is pining on a lonely island far away in the middle of the seas. … Yet your Olympian heart is quite unmoved.’

Book 1, workbook lines 43–56

Unpacking the passage

“answered him at once”
Athene speaks up in the gods’ assembly straight after Zeus, with no hesitation.
“It is for Odysseus that my heart is wrung”
She turns the council’s discussion away from Aegisthus and towards the case she wants to argue.
“Yet your Olympian heart is quite unmoved”
She openly reproaches the king of the gods.

Zeus agrees that they should “contrive a plan to bring him home” (workbook lines 70–71), and Athene sets out what she will do (workbook lines 74–86). This is a woman’s voice in an assembly, and it is heard only because she is a god. In Book 2 the Ithacan assembly is entirely male.

Scene 3 · Sending Penelope home

‘Tell the Suitors to leave and go home. As for your mother, if she is set on marrying, let her go back to her father’s house. He is a man of consequence, and the family will arrange a marriage and see that she has a generous dowry, as is only right for a beloved daughter.’

Book 1, workbook lines 240–244

Unpacking the passage

“let her go back to her father’s house”
A widow who remarries is passed back to her father’s authority.
“the family will arrange a marriage”
The marriage is arranged by her family on her behalf.
“a generous dowry”
Marriage involves a transfer of wealth, arranged between men.

The mechanics of a widow’s remarriage are laid out by a god in disguise, speaking to Penelope’s son. Athene adds that if Telemachus hears his father is dead, he should “give your mother in marriage to a new husband” (workbook lines 255–256).

Scene 4 · Penelope comes down

In her room upstairs, Penelope, the gracious daughter of Icarius, took in the words of his stirring ballad and came down from her quarters by the steep staircase, not alone, but with two waiting-women in attendance. When she came near her Suitors the great lady drew a fold of her shining veil across her cheeks and took her stand by a pillar of the massive roof, with one of her faithful maids on either side.

Book 1, workbook lines 286–293

Unpacking the passage

“In her room upstairs”
Penelope’s place is in the women’s quarters, apart from the hall.
“not alone, but with two waiting-women”
A queen appears in the hall properly attended, with a maid on either side.
“drew a fold of her shining veil across her cheeks”
She covers her face with her veil before she approaches the men in the hall.

She then bursts into tears and asks Phemius to choose another song, because this one “never fails to wring my heart” (workbook lines 295–302). This is her one public act in Book 1.

Scene 5 · Penelope sent upstairs

‘So go to your quarters now and attend to your own work, the loom and the spindle, and tell the servants to get on with theirs. Making decisions must be men’s concern, and mine in particular; for I am master in this house.’

Penelope was taken aback, but she retired to her own apartments, for she took her son’s sensible words to heart. Attended by her maids, she went upstairs to her bedroom, and there she wept for Odysseus, her beloved husband, till bright-eyed Athene closed her eyes in sweet sleep.

Book 1, workbook lines 311–319

Unpacking the passage

“the loom and the spindle”
Telemachus sends his mother back to weaving, the defining women’s work.
“Making decisions must be men’s concern”
He states the rule that public authority in the hall belongs to men.
“for I am master in this house”
His first act of authority is exercised over his mother.
“she took her son’s sensible words to heart”
The poem calls his words sensible, and she obeys. Her request is refused, and she can only weep.

Scene 6 · Eurycleia at bedtime

He was escorted by the faithful Eurycleia, who carried a blazing torch. This Eurycleia was the daughter of Ops and granddaughter of Peisenor. Laertes had procured her at his own cost long ago, when she was still a girl, for the price of twenty oxen. He had treated her in his home with all the respect due to a loyal wife, though for fear of his wife’s displeasure he had not slept with her. It was she who now served as torch-bearer to his grandson; and she who of all the household women loved him most, for she had nursed him as a child.

Book 1, workbook lines 372–379

Unpacking the passage

“the faithful Eurycleia”
Her defining quality is loyalty.
“for the price of twenty oxen”
She is a slave, bought as a girl, and the poem records her price.
“all the respect due to a loyal wife, though for fear of his wife’s displeasure”
Even her history is described through marriage and its rules: how she was honoured, and why Laertes never took her to his bed.
“loved him most, for she had nursed him”
Her standing in the household comes from service to its men.

She then folds and hangs Telemachus’s tunic and draws the door shut behind her (workbook lines 380–385). A slave is one of the household’s most trusted members, and her standing rests entirely on service and loyalty.

What Book 1 establishes

  • Goddesses act freely: Calypso keeps a man, and Athene argues in council and gets her way.
  • A mortal woman’s marriage is arranged by men, and her one public request is refused by her son.
  • A woman servant’s standing is defined by loyalty and by the men who own her.

Books 2–4

Book 2

  • Antinous tells the assembly how Penelope deceived the suitors for three years and calls her the cleverest of women, meaning it as an accusation. He demands that Telemachus send her back to her father. The men debate her future in a meeting she cannot attend.
  • Telemachus makes Eurycleia promise not to tell Penelope he has gone for eleven or twelve days. Mistress and servant are kept in different states of knowledge by the young master of the house.

Book 3

  • Nestor recalls that Clytemnestra resisted Aegisthus at first, being a woman of sense, until he wore her down. The poem gives its worst wife a moment of sympathy before condemning her.

Book 4

  • Helen enters to a description that compares her to a goddess, recognises Telemachus at once, drugs the wine against grief, and tells her own story of Troy. She is more present and more articulate than any mortal woman in the Telemachy except Penelope, and her past hangs over every word.
  • Penelope discovers that Telemachus has sailed and that the suitors plan to kill him. She collapses in grief and reproaches the women of the house for not waking her. She cannot act and can only pray, and Athene answers with a dream. The poem’s most powerful mortal woman ends the Telemachy with no move left to make.

Sympathetic, or of its time?

The strongest answers argue that the poem is both. It never questions the rules: men speak in the hall, wives wait, widows are sent home. Within those rules, though, it gives its women intelligence, dignity and inner life, and it lets Penelope’s cleverness match her husband’s.

It also shows a double standard without comment. Odysseus has spent seven years with Calypso and the poem never treats this as unfaithfulness. Penelope’s fidelity is the whole point of her character, and Clytemnestra’s failure of it is the poem’s deepest condemnation.

Remember. Notice the double standard without excusing it, and without pretending the poem is unaware of it.

Scholarly views

These are positions for you to test against the text. Decide how far the text supports each one, and mark your verdict.

Marilyn Katz
Penelope’s Renown (1991)
Penelope’s reputation rests on more than patience. Her intelligence and the deliberate ambiguity of her motives make her a character the audience has to interpret, in the same way it interprets Odysseus.
For
The web trick is mētis of the highest order; Antinous calls her the cleverest of women even as he complains; the poem never tells us what she privately believes about Odysseus’s return.
Against
In Books 1–4 the poem gives Penelope very few words and shows her mostly through men talking about her. Is the ambiguity Katz values a deliberate depth, or simply the result of the poem looking elsewhere?
How far does the text support this?
Helene Foley
“Reverse Similes” and Sex Roles in the Odyssey (1978)
The Odyssey repeatedly compares its women to men and its men to women, and uses this role-reversal to explore what a good marriage and a well-run household require of both partners.
For
Penelope defends the household by strategy while its master is absent; Antinous complains that she outwits a hundred men; it is Telemachus who must learn to command the hall.
Against
The role-reversals are temporary and the poem restores the normal order. Penelope is sent upstairs and ends Book 4 in prayer. A reader could argue the poem admires her competence only until a man is available to take over.
How far does the text support this?
Moses Finley
The World of Odysseus (1954)
The world of the poem is a man’s world. Women, even queens, are defined by their relationship to men, and their economic and political standing is derivative.
For
Penelope’s remarriage is a matter for her father and her son; her future is debated in an assembly she cannot attend; Eurycleia’s standing depends entirely on Laertes and Odysseus.
Against
Helen, Athene and Calypso all act on their own account, and Penelope’s web is the household’s only effective defence for three years. Finley describes the rules accurately; the poem may be more interested in what women achieve inside them.
How far does the text support this?

Summary

Key points
  • Public life in the poem is male: the assembly, speech in the hall and hosting all belong to men. Women’s sphere is the oikos, with its stores, servants, weaving and children.
  • Marriage is arranged between men, and a widow is sent back to her father with a dowry 1.243–244. Fidelity is expected of wives and not of husbands.
  • Penelope is the constrained centre, cleverer than a hundred men and still sent upstairs. Helen is blamed and forgiven at once. Clytemnestra is the wife who did not wait. Eurycleia is a slave whose standing rests on her loyalty.
  • Goddesses act freely: Athene speaks in council and drives the plot, and Calypso keeps the hero. Female power in the poem is either divine or indirect.
  • In Book 1 the audience meets Calypso 1.14 and Athene in council from 1.44, hears Athene advise sending Penelope home 1.243–244, sees Penelope overruled by her son, and learns Eurycleia’s history from 1.372.
  • In Books 2–4 Antinous praises the web trick as a complaint, Nestor gives Clytemnestra one line of sympathy, Helen recognises Telemachus, drugs the wine and tells her version of Troy, and Penelope is left only able to pray.
  • Katz argues that Penelope must be interpreted, Foley that the poem explores role reversal, and Finley that it is a man’s world. Each view can be tested against how little Penelope speaks and how much is said about her.
