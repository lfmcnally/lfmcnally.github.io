How to use this book
Part One · Reading the Telemachy

This is a plain, page-by-page guide to what actually happens in the first four books of the Odyssey. It is here so that you can follow the story clearly before moving on to the themes, such as kleos, timē and the gods, all of which assume you already know who everyone is and what happens to them.

What’s prescribed
  • Book 1 Prescribed text is the set text for this topic and is examined in detail.
  • Books 2–4 Context are context: you need to know the story, but not to the same depth.

Each book is broken into short scenes, in the order they happen. Characters are introduced the first time they appear, with a short box explaining who they are.

Remember. You do not need to memorise any exact wording from the poem for this section. Focus on the story, the people, and why things happen.

The world of the Odyssey
Before the poem begins

Ten years ago, a huge Greek army finally captured the city of Troy, ending a war that had lasted a decade. One of the Greek commanders was Odysseus, king of the small island of Ithaca, famous among the Greeks above all for his cunning. It was Odysseus’s idea, a giant wooden horse left as a fake gift, that finally let the Greeks get inside Troy’s walls and win the war.

The war itself took ten years, and it ended ten years ago. Odysseus has now been away from home for twenty years in total, and for the last several of those years nobody on Ithaca has had any news of him at all. Most people assume he is dead.

A note on the gods. The Greek gods look and act like people: they argue, play favourites and hold grudges. They are, however, immortal and vastly more powerful. They watch human affairs closely and often intervene, sometimes openly and more often in disguise.

Back home, this absence has created a crisis. Odysseus’s wife, Penelope, has not remarried, because she still hopes he is alive. His son, Telemachus, was a baby when his father left and is now a young man, old enough to rule but with no real authority in his own house.

Meanwhile, a large group of young noblemen from Ithaca and the surrounding islands, the suitors, have moved into the royal household uninvited. They are trying to pressure Penelope into choosing one of them to marry, and in the meantime they are living in the palace at the family’s expense: eating their food, drinking their wine, and slowly using up everything Telemachus should one day inherit.

This is the situation the poem opens on. The Odyssey begins on Ithaca with Odysseus’s son, and Odysseus himself does not appear until Book 5.

About Homer
Who made the poem

The Odyssey is credited to a poet named Homer, but almost nothing certain is known about him. No reliable dates, birthplace or biography survive from his own time. Everything written about “Homer” as a person comes from centuries later, and much of it is legend. Several Greek cities claimed to be his birthplace, which itself suggests that no one really knew.

Key idea. “Homer” may represent one poet, several poets, or a whole tradition. The uncertainty is real, and the debate about it is usually called the Homeric Question.

An oral tradition

The poems were composed within an oral tradition. Stories about the Trojan War and its heroes were told and retold by professional performers (bards, like Phemius in Book 1) for generations before anything was written down, changing slightly with each performance. Scholars now generally think the Iliad and the Odyssey took something close to their current shape around the 8th century BC, drawing on material that was already very old by then, and were only written down some time after that.

Iliad and Odyssey. The Iliad covers a few weeks near the end of the Trojan War itself. The Odyssey picks up ten years later, after the war has ended, and follows the difficult journeys home.

This matters for how you read the poem. Repeated phrases, standard descriptions and familiar scene-types (a hero arriving as a guest, a feast, a recognition) are tools a performer relied on to compose and remember an enormously long story out loud, in front of a live audience, without a written script. They should not be read as signs of lazy writing.

A twenty-year gap
Where Books 1–4 sit in time

One thing beginners often miss is that Odysseus has been away for twice as long as the Trojan War lasted. The war itself was only half of it.

10 years · The Trojan WarOdysseus leaves Ithaca; Troy falls at the end of the war.
10 years · Odysseus tries to get homeRoughly three years of adventures (the Cyclops, Circe, the Sirens), then seven years stuck on Calypso’s island.
The TelemachyBooks 1–4 begin here, near the very end of the second stretch, with Odysseus still on Calypso’s island and no news of him at home.

Common mistake. Don’t assume Odysseus has spent this whole time on his journey home having adventures we simply haven’t read yet. By the time Books 1–4 take place, he has been stuck, unmoving, on Calypso’s island for seven of those ten years. His famous adventures (the Cyclops, the Sirens and so on) all happened earlier, in the first three years after Troy fell, and are only told in flashback much later in the poem.

How the poem is put together
Story order and telling order

This is one of the most important things to grasp before reading, and one of the easiest to get wrong. The Odyssey does not tell Odysseus’s story in the order it happened. It opens near the very end of his twenty-year absence, then jumps around in time. The famous adventures on the list of stops, numbers 2 to 11, are not narrated as they happen at all. They are told later, by Odysseus himself, as a long flashback to an audience in Phaeacia.

Key idea. Story order and telling order are different things. The poem deliberately scrambles the first to create the second.

Starting a story in the middle of events, instead of at the beginning, is called starting in medias res, a Latin phrase meaning “into the middle of things”. It is one of the poem’s most influential features, and storytellers have copied it ever since.

Remember. When the poem opens, the Cyclops, Circe and the Sirens are all already in Odysseus’s past. Nothing from stops 2–10 is still to come.

Why tell it this way?

There are good reasons for Homer to arrange things like this instead of simply starting at Troy. Opening on Ithaca, with a household falling apart and a son who has never known his father, makes the stakes of the homecoming clear before we ever meet the man trying to make it.

Putting the adventures into Odysseus’s own mouth, told to a live audience, lets us hear him as a storyteller: cunning, persuasive, and possibly not always reliable. This tells us as much about him as the adventures themselves do.

Holding back the most famous episodes until the middle of the poem keeps the audience’s attention on the question that drives the whole story, which is whether Odysseus will get home.

Common mistake. Don’t describe Books 1–4 as “the start of Odysseus’s journey”. By the time the poem begins, his journey is almost over: nineteen of the twenty years have already passed, and he has been sitting on one island for seven of them.

Cast of characters
The people and gods you need before Book 1

These are the core people and gods to know before starting Book 1. More characters are introduced as they appear in each book.

O
Odysseus
King of Ithaca, currently absent. He fought at Troy and has been trying to get home ever since. He is famous for his cunning (mētis) as much as his courage. He does not appear in person in Books 1–4; everyone else is either searching for him or talking about him.
A
Athene
Goddess of wisdom and strategy, and Odysseus’s long-standing divine patron. She spends most of Books 1–4 disguised as a mortal, guiding Telemachus without revealing who she really is.
Z
Zeus
King of the gods, ruling over gods and mortals alike. He appears mainly in Book 1, where he sets out his view that humans usually bring suffering on themselves and are not being unfairly punished by the gods.
Pe
Penelope
Odysseus’s wife and Queen of Ithaca. She has waited twenty years for her husband, refusing to accept that he is dead, and is under constant pressure from the suitors to remarry. She is clever in her own right: she famously delayed the suitors for three years using a weaving trick.
Po
Poseidon
God of the sea. He holds a personal grudge against Odysseus (for reasons explained later in the wider poem) and is the one god working against his return. He is conveniently absent for the events of Book 1.
T
Telemachus
Son of Odysseus and Penelope, about twenty years old. He has grown up without his father and has no real power in his own house. The Telemachy (Books 1–4) follows his journey from a passive, uncertain young man into someone willing to act.
M
Mentor / Mentes
Two names, both really Athene in disguise. Mentes is an old family friend Athene pretends to be in Book 1. Mentor is a trusted friend of Odysseus whose appearance Athene borrows later. Neither is a real, separate character.
S
The suitors
Roughly a hundred young noblemen from Ithaca and nearby islands, occupying Odysseus’s hall and pressuring Penelope to marry one of them. Their two ringleaders, Antinous and Eurymachus, do most of the talking.

Common mistake. Don’t treat Mentes and Mentor as real, independent characters. Both names refer to disguises Athene uses. When you see either name in Books 1–2, it is Athene acting, and no mortal adviser is involved.
