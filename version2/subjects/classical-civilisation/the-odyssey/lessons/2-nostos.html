Lesson 2 · Nostos

The Odyssey announces homecoming as its subject in its very first lines, and everything the poem does is measured against that goal. The Telemachy shows what a homecoming involves by setting several returns from Troy side by side, while the one that matters most has still not happened.

What you need to know
  • what nostos means and why it is the organising idea of the whole poem
  • the difference between arriving home and achieving a homecoming
  • the range of returns the Telemachy shows, from complete to catastrophic
  • how the poem handles homecoming from the point of view of those left behind

Where it happens in Book 1

1.1–21 the proem   1.60–79 the divine council   1.194–205 “your father is alive”   1.211–212 the great name lament   1.239–305 Athene’s plan

Have your Book 1 workbook open alongside this page. Quotations on this page give the workbook’s own line numbers.

What nostos is
Section 1

Nostos means return or homecoming. It covers the journey and the arrival, and, as this lesson will show, it also covers what has to happen after the arrival. The Odyssey announces it as its subject in the first lines: a man who wandered far and suffered at sea while trying to get home. Everything the poem does is measured against that goal.

This makes the Odyssey a different kind of poem from the Iliad, which is about winning glory in war and the cost of doing so. The Odyssey asks what happens afterwards: whether a man can get back to the life he left, and what he finds when he does.

The words
nostos
return, homecoming; also the struggle to achieve one
the Nostoi
a lost epic about the homecomings of the Greek heroes after Troy. The Odyssey is the one surviving member of a family of such poems, and refers to the others constantly
oikos
the household a returning man must recover for his homecoming to count
kleos and nostos
glory or home. Achilles chose glory over a long life; Odysseus wants both, and the tension runs under the whole poem

Nostos and xenia

Nostos also depends on xenia. No one in the poem gets home alone. Menelaus says outright in Book 4 that he and Helen made it back only because strangers fed and sheltered them on the way. Every homecoming is a chain of hospitality scenes, and if one link breaks the traveller does not arrive.

What a homecoming actually requires
Section 2

A completed nostos has four parts, and the Telemachy shows each of them failing for someone.

  1. Survive the journey. Ajax did not: Proteus reports that he drowned on the way home. Book 4
  2. Arrive. Odysseus has not, after ten years; he is stuck on Calypso’s island.
  3. Be received by your own household. Agamemnon arrived and was killed the same day by his wife’s lover.
  4. Recover your place: wife, property, authority. This is the part still ahead of Odysseus, and the suitors are the obstacle.

Agamemnon

Agamemnon is the poem’s proof that arriving and coming home are two different things. He survived the war and the voyage and reached his own hall, yet he never came home. His story is told again and again in Books 1–4 because it separates the two so brutally.

It also shows why nostos is bound up with the household and with timē. Odysseus’s return will have to deal with the suitors, reclaim his wife and restore his son’s inheritance. The Telemachy shows the household falling apart so that the audience understands how much the homecoming will have to repair.

Common mistake. Nostos is more than reaching Ithaca. Agamemnon reached Mycenae and was murdered before the day was out, and the poem makes it very clear that reaching the door is a different thing from coming home.

The homecoming that has not happened
Section 3

The Telemachy’s peculiar power comes from the fact that its central nostos has not occurred. Odysseus is alive but stuck, and no one at home knows this. For four books the poem shows what a missing homecoming does to the people waiting for it:

  • a wife who can neither remarry nor mourn;
  • a son who can neither inherit nor grieve;
  • a household that cannot be defended, because no one knows whether its master is dead.

Telemachus says as much in Book 1. If his father had died at Troy there would at least be a grave and a name 1.211–212. For the family, not knowing is worse than a known loss.

What the audience knows

This is also a matter of technique. The audience knows from the divine council that the return is fated 1.60–79; the characters do not. The Telemachy works in the gap between what the audience knows and what Ithaca knows, and this is why the suitors’ dismissal of the eagle omen in Book 2 is so pointed: the audience knows that they are wrong.

Kinds of return
Section 4

Books 1–4 line up several homecomings from the war side by side. Read as a set, they make the poem’s argument: there are many ways for a nostos to succeed or fail, and Odysseus’s is the one still in the balance.

WhoKind of returnWhat it shows
NestorComplete and promptSailed straight home and kept his household and honour intact. The untroubled model, told by the man himself in Book 3.
MenelausComplete but delayedBlown to Egypt and stranded for years; home only after seizing Proteus for a way back. Wealthy, but he says he would give it up to have his dead comrades back.
AgamemnonArrival without homecomingMurdered in his own hall by Aegisthus and Clytemnestra; avenged by Orestes. The anti-nostos the poem returns to repeatedly.
AjaxNo returnDied at sea, as Proteus tells Menelaus. A homecoming that never began.
AchillesReturn refusedChose glory at Troy over a long life at home. This is the Iliad’s choice, and the one Odysseus does not make.
OdysseusPendingAlive, detained by Calypso, longing for home. The whole plot is the working-out of this one unfinished return.
TelemachusA journey in reverseLeaves home to find news that might bring his father back, and by Book 4 faces an ambush on his own return.
Nostos in Book 1
Section 5 · Prescribed text

Book 1 announces nostos as the poem’s subject, settles on Olympus that Odysseus will return, and then shows Ithaca learning for the first time that a return is even possible.

The homecoming scenes in order

Scene 1
The proem
Workbook lines 1–20

The poem defines its hero by his journey home, names Calypso as the one detaining him and Poseidon as the god opposing him.

Scene 2
The divine council
Workbook lines 59–80

Zeus agrees that the gods will bring Odysseus home; Poseidon may delay the return but cannot stop it.

Scene 3
“Your father is alive”
Workbook lines 172–181

“Mentes” tells Telemachus that Odysseus is alive, held on an island, and resourceful enough to find a way back.

Scene 4
The great name lament
Workbook lines 203–216

Telemachus wishes his father had died at Troy, where he would have had a burial mound and a famous name.

Scene 5
Athene’s plan
Workbook lines 237–263

Athene tells Telemachus to call an assembly, then sail to Pylos and Sparta for news of his father’s return.

Scene 6

Phemius sings of the Greeks’ return from Troy; Penelope comes down in tears and asks for another song.

Scene 1 · The proem

Tell me, Muse, the story of that resourceful man who was driven to wander far and wide after he had sacked the holy citadel of Troy. … He suffered great anguish on the high seas in his struggles to preserve his life and bring his comrades home. But he failed to save those comrades, in spite of all his efforts.

… Odysseus alone was prevented from returning to the home and wife he yearned for by that powerful goddess, the Nymph Calypso, who longed for him to marry her, and kept him in her vaulted cave.

Book 1, workbook lines 1–15

Unpacking the passage

“driven to wander far and wide”
The hero is introduced through his journey before he is even named. Wandering is the first thing we learn about him.
“to preserve his life and bring his comrades home”
His goal is stated as a homecoming for himself and for his men, and the next sentence tells us that half of it failed.
“Odysseus alone was prevented from returning”
Every other survivor of the war is home. Odysseus is singled out as the one whose nostos is unfinished.
“the home and wife he yearned for”
Home is defined as a household and a wife, which is exactly what the suitors are threatening on Ithaca.

Nostos is announced as the poem’s subject before anything else happens. The proem also names Poseidon as the god who pursued Odysseus “with relentless malice till the day when he reached his own country” (workbook lines 18–20), so the audience already knows who is delaying the return.

Scene 2 · The divine council

‘That is why, ever since Polyphemus was blinded, Poseidon the Earth-shaker has kept Odysseus in exile, though he stops short of killing him. But come now, let all of us here together contrive a plan to bring him home. Poseidon will relent. For he will not be able to struggle on alone against the united will of the immortal gods.’

‘… tell Calypso, the Nymph with the plaited tresses, of our unalterable decision that the patient Odysseus must now set out for home.’

Book 1, workbook lines 68–80

Unpacking the passage

“stops short of killing him”
Poseidon can make the return long and painful, but he will not end it by killing Odysseus.
“contrive a plan to bring him home”
Zeus turns Odysseus’s homecoming into the business of all the gods together.
“our unalterable decision”
Athene’s words to be carried to Calypso describe the return as fixed. The only questions left are when it will happen and at what cost.

From the first scene on Olympus the audience knows that the return will happen. The people on Ithaca do not, and the rest of the Telemachy depends on that difference.

Scene 3 · “Your father is alive”

‘… the gods must be hindering his return, because the good Odysseus is not dead, but alive somewhere on this earth. He must be on some distant island out in the sea, in the hands of enemies, savages no doubt, who keep him there by force. … Your father will not be exiled much longer from the land he loves so well, not even if he’s kept in iron chains. He will think of a way to return – he is endlessly resourceful.’

Book 1, workbook lines 173–181

Unpacking the passage

“the gods must be hindering his return”
Athene, speaking as Mentes, explains the delay as the work of the gods, which matches what the audience has just seen on Olympus.
“alive somewhere on this earth”
This is the first word anyone on Ithaca has had that Odysseus is still alive.
“who keep him there by force”
Odysseus is presented as a prisoner, held on the island against his will.
“He will think of a way to return”
The return will depend partly on Odysseus’s own resourcefulness, the quality the proem gave him in its first line.

A homecoming now becomes possible in the minds of the people waiting for it, and the news changes Telemachus’s whole posture for the rest of the book.

Scene 4 · The great name lament

‘… they have made him vanish. His death itself, if he had fallen among his men at Troy or died in friendly arms at home with all his fighting done, would have caused me less distress. For in that case the whole Achaean nation would have joined in building him a mound, and he would have left a great name for his son to inherit. But there was to be no famous end for him; the Storm-Fiends have snatched him away. He has gone where he cannot be seen or found and to me has left nothing but sorrow and tears.’

Book 1, workbook lines 207–215

Unpacking the passage

“they have made him vanish”
Telemachus’s complaint is that his father has disappeared without trace. There is no news, and so no ending.
“would have caused me less distress”
He says plainly that a known death would be easier to bear than the uncertainty of a failed homecoming.
“building him a mound”
A death at Troy would have given the family a grave, a funeral and a place to grieve.
“where he cannot be seen or found”
A missing man cannot be mourned or replaced, which is why the household is stuck.

For Telemachus a failed homecoming with no body is worse than a heroic death. The moment is also about kleos, since a death at Troy would have left a great name, and so it hurts twice.

Scene 5 · Athene’s plan

‘Choose your best ship, man her with twenty oarsmen, and set out to inquire after your long absent father. … Go to Pylos first and question the excellent Nestor; then on to Sparta to see auburn-haired Menelaus, since he was the last of the bronze-armoured Achaeans to reach home. If you hear that your father is alive and on his way back, you should reconcile yourself to a year more of this wastage. But if you hear that he is dead and gone, return to your own country, build a mound with all the proper funeral rites, and give your mother in marriage to a new husband.’

Book 1, workbook lines 246–256

Unpacking the passage

“set out to inquire after your long absent father”
Athene’s plan is itself a journey in search of a homecoming. Telemachus must leave home so that his father’s return becomes possible.
“the last of the bronze-armoured Achaeans to reach home”
Menelaus is chosen because of his own long nostos, which makes him the best source of news about returns.
“build a mound with all the proper funeral rites”
Either answer would end the waiting: news of life means patience, news of death means a funeral and a remarriage.

Scene 6 · The song of the returns

He found them listening in silence to a song which their famous bard was singing to them about the Achaeans’ return from Troy and the disasters that Pallas Athene made them suffer.

‘… give us no more of your present song. It is too sad; it never fails to wring my heart. For in that catastrophe no one was dealt a heavier blow than I, who pass my days in mourning for the best of husbands, the man whose name rings through the land from Hellas to the heart of Argos.’

Book 1, workbook lines 284–302

Unpacking the passage

“the Achaeans’ return from Troy”
Phemius is singing a song of the returns, the same subject as the lost Nostoi poems.
“the disasters that Pallas Athene made them suffer”
The returns are sung as suffering, and the goddess who is now helping Odysseus caused some of it.
“It is too sad; it never fails to wring my heart”
For Penelope the song is about her own husband’s missing return, so she cannot bear to hear it.
“pass my days in mourning”
She mourns a husband who may still be alive, which is the position the delayed nostos has left her in.

Here homecoming is literally a song sung in a hall, and one that a waiting wife cannot bear to hear. Telemachus answers that “Odysseus is not the only one who has never returned from Troy” (workbook line 310).

What Book 1 establishes

  • Nostos is the poem’s subject from its first lines, and Odysseus is the one survivor whose return is unfinished.
  • The gods have settled that he will return, so the audience knows something the people on Ithaca do not.
  • Ithaca hears for the first time that a return is possible, and Telemachus sets out on a journey of his own to find out more.
Nostos in Books 2–4
Section 6 · Context

Book 2 · The omen and the departure

  • Two eagles fight overhead. Halitherses reads the sign as meaning that Odysseus’s return is near and that the suitors’ ruin will come with it. The suitors laugh.
  • Telemachus sails by night. His departure is the mirror image of his father’s situation: he leaves home deliberately so that a homecoming becomes possible.

Book 3 · Nestor at Pylos

  • Asked for news, Nestor gives a history of homecomings instead. The leaders quarrelled after Troy fell and sailed separately; some got back quickly, some slowly and some never.
  • He dwells on Agamemnon, murdered on arrival and avenged by Orestes, and holds Orestes up to Telemachus as a model of what a son does when a homecoming goes wrong.
  • Nestor’s own return was smooth. His settled household and grand public sacrifice show what a completed nostos looks like, and quietly measure how far Ithaca has fallen from it.

Book 4 · Menelaus at Sparta

  • Menelaus was blown to Egypt and stranded for years. He got home only by seizing the shape-shifting Proteus and holding on through every transformation until Proteus told him what to do. His nostos was earned by endurance, and it took nearly as long as Odysseus’s.
  • From Proteus he learned three fates at once: Ajax drowned on the way home; Agamemnon was murdered on arrival; Odysseus was alive but weeping on Calypso’s island with no ship and no crew. This is the first concrete news of Odysseus that anyone brings Telemachus, and it comes wrapped in one lost return and one ruined one.
  • For all his palace’s gold, Menelaus says he would gladly have less if the men who died at Troy could have come home. A completed nostos can still be a sorrowful one.
  • Penelope learns that Telemachus has sailed and that the suitors mean to kill him on his return. She is now waiting on two homecomings and dreads losing both. Athene sends a dream in the shape of her sister Iphthime, promising that Telemachus will come safely home.

Worth noticing. The first nostos the gods explicitly guarantee to anyone on Ithaca is the son’s, in Penelope’s dream at the end of Book 4.

Homecoming from the other side
Section 7

Most stories of return are told from the traveller’s point of view. The Telemachy does something rarer: for four books it tells a homecoming entirely from the side of the people waiting for it.

Penelope’s weaving trick, Telemachus’s silence among the suitors, the household’s wealth draining away and the suitors’ growing confidence that the master is dead are all what a delayed nostos looks like from inside the house.

When Odysseus finally does return, the audience already knows exactly what he is returning to, and how much has been at stake in every year of his absence. In this way the Telemachy earns the homecoming before it happens.

Scholarly views to argue with
Section 8

These are positions for you to test. Decide how far the text supports each one, and mark your verdict.

Gregory Nagy
Greek Mythology and Poetics (1990)
Nostos is more than travel. It is a return to light and to life, out of darkness, death and forgetting, and so coming home is the opposite of dying.
For
Telemachus treats his father’s missing return as worse than death; Agamemnon reaches home and dies anyway; Odysseus weeps on Calypso’s island as if already dead to the living.
Against
Menelaus is fully alive, fully home, and still grieving. If nostos is a return to life, his should be a happy ending, and the poem declines to make it one.
How far does the text support this?
Anthony Edwards
Achilles in the Odyssey (1985)
The Odyssey deliberately sets Odysseus’s values against Achilles’. Where the Iliad prizes glory won by death in battle, the Odyssey prizes survival and return, and quietly criticises the older ideal.
For
Achilles chose glory over home and is dead; Odysseus will refuse Calypso’s offer of immortality to go home; the returns of the heroes are sung as sorrow, with no sense of triumph.
Against
Odysseus does not give up glory to get home. His homecoming becomes his glory, sung by bards like Phemius. The poem may be trying to hold on to both values instead of choosing between them.
How far does the text support this?
A common student view
It is tempting to read the Telemachy as marking time until Odysseus appears.
Test it
Test that view against Book 4. The first hard news of Odysseus, the first divine guarantee of a safe return and the household’s worst fear all arrive there, before he has said a word. Ask whether the poem could have earned his return without these four books.
Summary
Lesson 2 · Nostos
Key points
  • Nostos is homecoming. It includes the journey, the arrival and the recovery of your place at home, and Agamemnon managed only two of its four parts.
  • The Nostoi were a whole family of poems about the heroes’ returns from Troy. The Odyssey is the one that survives, and it refers to the others constantly.
  • In Book 1 the proem announces the subject 1.1–21, Zeus fixes the return as fated 1.60–79, Athene tells Telemachus that his father is alive 1.194–205, Telemachus delivers the great name lament 1.211–212, and Phemius sings of the returns while Penelope weeps.
  • In Books 2–4 the eagle omen predicts the return; Nestor gives a catalogue of returns and holds up Orestes as a model; Menelaus tells of Proteus and of three fates in one speech, and grieves for the comrades who never came home; Penelope fears for her son and is comforted by Athene’s promise.
  • The returns can be grouped as complete (Nestor), delayed (Menelaus), arrival only (Agamemnon), none (Ajax), refused (Achilles), pending (Odysseus) and in reverse (Telemachus).
  • The Telemachy tells a homecoming from inside the house, and the audience knows from the divine council what the characters do not.
  • Nagy reads nostos as a return to life, and Edwards reads the poem as an answer to the Iliad. Both views can be tested against Menelaus, who is home and alive but still grieving.
