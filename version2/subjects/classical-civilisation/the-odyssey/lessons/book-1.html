Before you read
The world of the Odyssey

Ten years ago, a huge Greek army finally captured the city of Troy. One of its commanders was Odysseus, king of the small island of Ithaca, famous among the Greeks for his cunning rather than his strength.

That was ten years ago. The war itself took ten years. Odysseus has now been away from home for twenty years in total, and for the last several of those years nobody on Ithaca has had any news of him at all. Most people assume he is dead.

A twenty-year gap

10 years · The Trojan WarOdysseus leaves Ithaca; Troy falls at the end of the war.
3 years · AdventuresThe Cyclops, Circe, the Sirens. Told later, in flashback (Books 9–12).
7 years · Calypso’s islandBooks 1–4 begin here, near the very end, with no news of him at home.

Common mistake. Don’t describe Books 1–4 as “the start of Odysseus’s journey”. By the time the poem begins, his journey is almost over: nineteen of the twenty years have already passed, and he has been sitting on one island for seven of them.

Back home, this absence has created a crisis. Penelope has not remarried. Telemachus, a baby when his father left, is now old enough to rule but has no real authority in his own house. And a large group of young noblemen, the suitors, have moved into the palace uninvited, eating through everything Telemachus should one day inherit.

This is the situation the poem opens on. It begins on Ithaca with Telemachus, and Odysseus himself does not appear until Book 5.

Scene 1 · On Olympus
1.1–95

The poem opens not on Ithaca but on Mount Olympus, home of the gods. Zeus is thinking about a man named Aegisthus, who years earlier murdered a king called Agamemnon and was, in turn, killed in revenge by Agamemnon’s son, Orestes. Zeus uses this as an example: mortals like to blame the gods for their misfortunes, but often their suffering is really the result of their own bad choices. Aegisthus was warned in advance, and did it anyway.

Why this scene matters. It tells the audience, before anyone on Ithaca knows it, that Odysseus is alive and that his return is only a matter of time. Everything that follows is watched with that knowledge in mind.

Athene, who favours Odysseus, uses this moment to raise his situation. He is not being punished for wrongdoing like Aegisthus. He is trapped on a remote island, held by the nymph Calypso, who wants him as a husband. Zeus agrees that Odysseus deserves help, though Poseidon, who has a personal grudge against him, will keep making the journey difficult. Athene decides to act at once: she will go to Ithaca herself, in disguise, to encourage Telemachus.

Z
Zeus
King of the gods. Appears mainly in Book 1, where he sets out his view that humans usually bring suffering on themselves rather than being unfairly punished by the gods.
P
Poseidon
God of the sea. The one god working against Odysseus’s return. Conveniently absent for the events of Book 1.
Scene 2 · A stranger arrives
1.96–279

Athene arrives on Ithaca disguised as Mentes, a chieftain from a nearby land and an old friend of the family. She finds the suitors lounging in the courtyard, and Telemachus sitting miserably among them, imagining his father returning and driving them all out.

Hospitality (xenia). Homeric society placed enormous importance on how a host treated a guest: correct food, a seat, and no rushing to business first. Telemachus does this instinctively; the suitors never do. More in Lesson 1 →

Telemachus is the first to notice the stranger and is embarrassed that a guest has been left standing at the door. He welcomes “Mentes” properly, leads him inside away from the suitors’ noise, and makes sure he is seated, fed and given wine before any business is discussed. Once they are settled, Telemachus explains the situation: his father has been missing for years, the suitors are eating through the family’s wealth, and he feels powerless to stop them.

“Mentes” reassures him. Odysseus is not dead, and will find a way home soon. She encourages Telemachus to act rather than wait: call a public assembly, tell the suitors openly to leave, then travel to Pylos and Sparta to ask the older Greek leaders for news. As she finishes speaking, she vanishes, flying off like a bird. Telemachus realises, with a shock, that his visitor was a god all along.

Key moment. This is the advice that sets the whole Telemachy in motion: call an assembly, then travel to find news. Everything in Books 2–4 follows directly from it.

M
Mentes / Mentor
Two names, both really Athene in disguise. Mentes is the old family friend she pretends to be in Book 1; Mentor is the friend whose appearance she borrows in Book 2. Neither is a real, separate character.
Scene 3 · The feast and the bard
1.280–365

Inside the hall the suitors are feasting, and a bard named Phemius is singing about the disaster-filled journeys of the Greek heroes trying to get home after Troy. Penelope hears the song from upstairs and comes down, upset: the subject is too close to her own situation, and she asks him to sing something else.

Ph
Phemius
The court bard, forced to perform for the suitors even though it clearly distresses Penelope. He has no real power in the situation himself.

Telemachus, newly encouraged, surprises everyone. He tells his mother, respectfully but firmly, that the choice of song is his to make, since he is the head of the household now, and sends her back upstairs. It is a small moment, but a real change: the first time in the poem Telemachus asserts any authority at all.

He then announces to the suitors that he will call an assembly the next day and formally order them to leave. Antinous responds mockingly; Eurymachus, more smoothly, asks who the mysterious visitor was. Telemachus, now suspecting the truth, keeps it to himself.

A&E
Antinous & Eurymachus
The two most prominent suitors. Antinous is blunt and openly hostile to Telemachus; Eurymachus is smoother and more calculating. Both reappear throughout the Telemachy.
Scene 4 · Night
1.366–387

As the suitors finally leave for the night, Telemachus goes up to his room, accompanied by his old childhood nurse, Eurycleia, who has looked after him since he was a baby. Book 1 ends with Telemachus lying awake, turning over in his mind everything “Mentes” told him, and thinking about the journey he is now planning to make.

Eu
Eurycleia
A loyal old servant who raised Telemachus. A minor presence here, but she becomes far more important later in the wider poem.
