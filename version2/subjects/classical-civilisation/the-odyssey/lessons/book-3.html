Book 3 at a glance
Context

Telemachus reaches Pylos, the home of old King Nestor. Nestor has no direct news of Odysseus, but he has a great deal to say about what happened to the Greek leaders after Troy, and he sends Telemachus on to Sparta.

Remember. Book 3 is context. You need to know the story and the people, but it is not examined in the same detail as Book 1.

Scene 1 · Arrival at Pylos
Context

Telemachus and Mentor arrive at Pylos in the middle of a huge public sacrifice to Poseidon, led by the elderly king Nestor, one of the Greek commanders who fought at Troy alongside Odysseus. Nestor and his sons welcome the visitors warmly and include them in the feast, which is a strong contrast to the suitors’ behaviour back on Ithaca.

N
Nestor
An elderly, experienced king known for his long speeches and his store of memories from the Trojan War. He has been safely home for years and has no direct news of Odysseus.
Scene 2 · Nestor’s stories
Context

Nestor has no direct news of Odysseus, but he talks at length about what happened to the Greek leaders after Troy fell. The army split up and left separately, and their journeys home went very differently. Nestor spends particular time on the story of Agamemnon, the most powerful of the Greek commanders, who returned home only to be murdered by his wife’s lover, Aegisthus, and was later avenged by his own son, Orestes.

Orestes as an example

Which shows what a good thing it is, when a man dies, for a son to survive him, as Orestes survived to take revenge on the treacherous Aegisthus who had killed his noble father. So you, my friend … be as brave as Orestes. Then future generations will sing your praises.’ The thoughtful Telemachus replied: … ‘Ah, if the gods would only give me strength like his to exact revenge for the Suitors’ vicious crimes … But they have no such happiness in store for me, nor for my father. I must just endure.’

Book 3, workbook lines 176–187

Unpacking the passage

“a good thing … for a son to survive him”
Nestor praises Orestes for what he did as a son, and Telemachus is a son in the same position.
“be as brave as Orestes”
Nestor makes the comparison directly and tells Telemachus to follow the example.
“future generations will sing your praises”
The reward Nestor offers is fame in song for later generations.
“I must just endure”
Telemachus wishes he had Orestes’ strength, but he does not yet believe he can act.

Nestor holds Orestes up as an example of exactly the kind of decisive action a son should take.

Agamemnon and Orestes. This story is repeated more than once across the Telemachy. It works as a model: Telemachus is being encouraged, again, to think about what decisive action of his own might look like.

Telemachus reveals who he is and explains his own troubles with the suitors. Nestor sympathises, comments on how much Telemachus resembles his father, and suggests that Menelaus, another Greek commander who has only recently made it home himself, might have more recent news, since he travelled further and took longer to return.

Athene revealed

As the visit ends, Athene suddenly reveals her divine nature, taking the form of a bird and flying away in front of the astonished court. Nestor, realising that Telemachus has been travelling with a god all along, offers a sacrifice in thanks. The next morning, Telemachus continues his journey overland by chariot to Sparta, accompanied by Nestor’s son, Pisistratus.

Pi
Pisistratus
Nestor’s son, who accompanies Telemachus to Sparta. A minor character, mainly present to keep Telemachus company on the road.
