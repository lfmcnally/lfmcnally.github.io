Book 2 at a glance
Context

The morning after Athene’s visit, Telemachus does what “Mentes” told him to do. He calls an assembly, speaks out against the suitors in public, and then sails away in secret to look for news of his father.

Remember. Book 2 is context. You need to know the story and the people, but it is not examined in the same detail as Book 1.

Scene 1 · The assembly
Context

The next morning, Telemachus calls the first public assembly on Ithaca in twenty years. In front of the gathered islanders, he explains the crisis and formally asks the suitors to leave his father’s house, warning that the gods will eventually punish them if they refuse.

Antinous responds by shifting the blame onto Penelope. He reveals that she has been delaying her decision for three years using a trick: she told the suitors she would choose a husband once she had finished weaving a burial shroud for Telemachus’s grandfather, Laertes, but secretly unpicked her work every night so that it was never finished. A servant eventually betrayed her, and she was forced to complete it. Antinous insists the suitors will not leave until Penelope finally chooses one of them.

The web trick

So by day she used to weave at the great web, but every night had torches set beside it and undid the work. For three years she took us in by this trick. A fourth began, and the seasons were slipping by, when one of her women who knew all about it gave her mistress away. We caught her unravelling her beautiful work, and she was forced reluctantly to complete it.

Book 2, workbook lines 94–99

Unpacking the passage

“by day … every night”
Penelope works in public during the day and undoes the work in private at night, so the suitors see only the weaving and never the unpicking.
“For three years she took us in”
Antinous admits that the suitors were fooled for years. The complaint is his, but it shows how long her plan succeeded.
“one of her women … gave her mistress away”
The trick ends only because someone inside the household betrays her.

The web trick. This is one of Penelope’s most famous moments in the whole poem: three years of buying time through cunning instead of force. It shows that she is just as resourceful, in her own way, as Odysseus.

Telemachus refuses to send his mother away against her will. At that moment, two eagles appear overhead, fighting fiercely before flying off.

The eagle omen

In answer to his words, Zeus the Thunderer urged two eagles into flight from the mountain-top. … But as soon as they were directly over the meeting-place, where the sound of voices filled the air, they began to flap their wings and wheel about, glancing down at the faces of the crowd with looks foreboding death. Then with their talons they clawed at each other’s cheeks and neck, and so swooped eastward over the house-tops of the busy town.

Book 2, workbook lines 130–137

Unpacking the passage

“Zeus the Thunderer urged two eagles”
The narrator tells us plainly that the birds are sent by Zeus, so the audience knows the omen is real before anyone interprets it.
“In answer to his words”
The omen comes straight after Telemachus speaks, as if Zeus is responding to him.
“looks foreboding death”
The eagles look down at the faces in the assembly, and the phrase points towards deaths still to come.

An old man named Halitherses interprets this as a sign from the gods that Odysseus is close to returning and that disaster awaits the suitors. The suitors laugh the omen off: Eurymachus tells him to “Run home and read omens to your children” (workbook line 159). Telemachus then asks for a ship and crew so that he can travel to the mainland for news, as “Mentes” advised. The suitors mock the idea, assuming nothing will come of it.

H
Halitherses
A respected older Ithacan who reads the eagle omen correctly. The suitors’ refusal to take him seriously is a small early sign of their overconfidence.
Scene 2 · Slipping away
Context

Athene, now disguised as Mentor, an old friend of Odysseus, helps Telemachus prepare in secret: borrowing a ship, gathering a crew and collecting supplies, all without the suitors finding out. That night, while the suitors are unaware, Telemachus and his small crew set sail from Ithaca, heading first for Pylos.

Remember. The Mentor who helps Telemachus prepare is Athene in disguise, like Mentes in Book 1. The name is different because she has chosen to look like a different old friend this time.
