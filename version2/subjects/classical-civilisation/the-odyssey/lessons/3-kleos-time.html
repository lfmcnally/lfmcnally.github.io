Lesson 3 · Kleos and timē

In the world of the Odyssey a person’s worth is confirmed in public, through how they are treated and what is said about them. Kleos is the fame that outlives you, and timē is the honour shown to you now; Telemachus begins the poem with neither.

What you need to know
  • what a shame culture is, and why it makes reputation central to Homeric behaviour
  • the difference between kleos and timē, and a reliable test for telling them apart
  • how each is earned, shown and lost across Books 1–4
  • why avenging a father is presented as the one action that wins both

Where it happens in Book 1

1.97–108 the suitors’ disrespect   1.113–135 Telemachus as host   1.211–212 the great name lament   1.253–263 Athene’s promise   1.258–263 Orestes as the model

A shame culture
Section 1

Homeric society is often described as a shame culture, as opposed to a guilt culture.

  • In a guilt culture behaviour is regulated mainly by an internal, private sense of right and wrong: a person feels bad even if no one ever finds out.
  • In a shame culture behaviour is regulated by how a person is seen and judged by others. What matters is doing the right thing and also being seen to do it, and avoiding public disgrace.

This is why so much of the Odyssey is about reputation, treatment and appearances in front of an audience. A character’s worth is confirmed publicly, through speech, treatment and story, and it is never settled privately in their own mind.

The words
kleos
glory or fame that survives a person; what others hear said about them. From the verb kluō, to hear
timē
honour or status shown to a person while alive, through how they are treated. Literally worth or value
aretē
excellence: the practical quality that makes someone good at what they are. Aretē is what you have; kleos and timē are how it is recognised
aidōs
a sense of shame; respect for how others will judge you. The internal mechanism that makes a shame culture work
geras
a specific gift or prize of honour, publicly awarded: timē made into an object
oikos
the household: family, land, servants and wealth together. Timē is largely a matter of standing within it
Kleos
Section 2

Kleos comes from the verb kluō, to hear. It is what others hear about a person, so it exists in other people’s mouths and ears, outside the person themselves. Three things follow.

  1. It cannot be self-awarded. Kleos has to be given by an audience and carried forward by stories, songs and retelling. This is why bards matter so much in the poem: the mechanism that creates kleos is the same one the poem uses to exist.
  2. It is mostly experienced after death. A person has kleos when they are dead and others are still telling their story. It requires a death worth telling: visible, witnessed and story-worthy. A hero who vanishes without witnesses gains none, however brave the act.
  3. It can be infamous. Aegisthus has kleos of a kind, since his name survives as a warning. Kleos means that a story survives, whether or not the story is one of approval.

The Iliad supplies the classic case. Achilles is offered a long life with no kleos or a short life at Troy with fame that lasts forever, and he chooses the fame. Scholars sometimes call the strongest form kleos aphthiton, imperishable fame.

Timē
Section 3

Timē means honour, but more precisely worth or value: the value placed on a person by their community. Unlike kleos, which is a legacy, timē is experienced directly, in the moment, through how a person is actually treated, and because it depends on treatment it can rise and fall. It is shown through concrete, visible things.

How timē is shown

  • the land and livestock a person owns
  • a geras received: a choice cut of meat, a share of plunder, a fine object
  • the seat a person is given, and the portion of food and wine
  • the deference shown to them in speech
  • the gifts offered on departure

The gods’ timē

Gods have timē too. Each god has a sphere of honour, and mortals maintain it through sacrifice, prayer and correct behaviour. Poseidon’s anger at Odysseus is partly a matter of timē: blinding Polyphemus injured Poseidon’s honour through his son.

Moses Finley argued that timē among mortals was maintained above all through gift-exchange, in which no gift was free and each created an obligation to return.

Telling them apart
Section 4

A reliable test: is the person receiving this now, in front of people, or being talked about after the fact? Present and physical is timē; future and told is kleos.

The two are related, since honour shown now can become the detail a storyteller repeats later, but they can also come apart.

  • The suitors have a kind of timē while they live, and are heading for a shameful death with no kleos to follow.
  • Odysseus has enormous latent kleos as the sacker of Troy and, trapped and unrecognised, almost no timē at all.
  • Telemachus at the start of Book 1 has neither, and his arc across the Telemachy is the slow construction of both.

Kleos and timē in Book 1
Section 5 · Prescribed text

Book 1 shows timē being denied and conferred in the same hall, and gives Telemachus his first reasons to think about kleos: his father’s missing fame, and the fame that Athene says he can win for himself.

The scenes in order

Scene 1
The suitors’ disrespect
Workbook lines 97–102

The suitors sit at the door on hides of Odysseus’s oxen while his servants wait on them.

Scene 2
Telemachus as host
Workbook lines 113–130

Telemachus gives “Mentes” the best chair, a footstool, food and wine, served from gold and silver.

Scene 3
The great name lament
Workbook lines 203–216

Telemachus grieves that his father vanished, when a death at Troy would have left a mound and a great name.

Scene 4
Athene’s promise
Workbook lines 80–86

On Olympus Athene plans to send Telemachus for news of his father, so that he may win the praise of men.

Scene 5
Orestes as the model
Workbook lines 256–263

Athene holds up Orestes, who won a name by killing his father’s murderer, and tells Telemachus to be as brave.

Scene 6
“I am master in this house”
Workbook lines 303–319

Telemachus overrules Penelope over the bard’s song and sends her upstairs.

Scene 1 · The suitors’ disrespect

She found the insolent Suitors sitting in front of the door on hides of oxen they themselves had slaughtered, playing with counters; their squires and pages were busy round them, some blending wine and water in the mixing-bowls, and others carving meat in lavish portions and wiping down the tables with sponges before they set them ready.

Book 1, workbook lines 97–102

Unpacking the passage

“insolent”
Insolence is a failure of deference, one of the ways timē is shown. The suitors are judged by it before they speak.
“oxen they themselves had slaughtered”
Livestock is one of the visible forms of timē. The suitors are using up the household’s wealth, and with it the heir’s standing.
“carving meat in lavish portions”
Portions of meat are a sign of honour, and here the household’s servants are giving them to the suitors.

The suitors occupy Telemachus’s hall and consume his household’s wealth without any deference to him as heir. His timē is denied daily, in front of him, in his own house. Later he tells his guest that the suitors are “courting my mother and wasting my property” and “eating me out of house and home” (workbook lines 219–222). Timē is lost here through treatment, exactly as it is gained.

Scene 2 · Telemachus as host

He then conducted her to a beautiful carved chair, over which he spread a linen cloth, and seated her there with a stool for her feet. …

Presently a maid came with water in a fine golden jug and poured it out over a silver basin for them to rinse their hands. … Meanwhile a steward served them plates of various meats he had selected from his board, and put gold cups beside them, which a steward filled with wine as he passed on his frequent rounds.

Book 1, workbook lines 115–130

Unpacking the passage

“a beautiful carved chair … a stool for her feet”
The seat a person is given is one of the clearest signs of timē, and the guest receives the best one.
“a fine golden jug … a silver basin”
The household’s best objects are used for the guest, so the honour is visible and material.
“plates of various meats he had selected”
The guest’s portion is chosen for them, another way of showing honour through food.

This is timē conferred: the guest is present and physically receiving honour. It is also a hospitality scene, as the Xenia lesson explained, and the two themes overlap here because correct hospitality is one of the main ways timē is shown.

Scene 3 · The great name lament

‘His death itself, if he had fallen among his men at Troy or died in friendly arms at home with all his fighting done, would have caused me less distress. For in that case the whole Achaean nation would have joined in building him a mound, and he would have left a great name for his son to inherit. But there was to be no famous end for him; the Storm-Fiends have snatched him away. He has gone where he cannot be seen or found …’

Book 1, workbook lines 208–214

Unpacking the passage

“fallen among his men at Troy”
A death in battle would have been witnessed by the army, which is what kleos requires.
“the whole Achaean nation would have joined in building him a mound”
The burial mound is a public monument that keeps a name alive for those who come after.
“a great name for his son to inherit”
Kleos can be passed on. Telemachus has lost his father and also the fame he would have inherited.
“no famous end … where he cannot be seen or found”
A disappearance at sea has no witnesses and no story, so it brings no kleos.

This is grief specifically about missing kleos. A death at Troy would have been witnessed and told; a disappearance at sea is neither.

Scene 4 · Athene’s promise

‘Meanwhile I myself will go to Ithaca to instil more spirit into Odysseus’ son and encourage him to call the long-haired Achaeans to an assembly and speak his mind to that crowd of Suitors … After that I shall send him to Sparta and to sandy Pylos to seek news of his father’s return. It is possible that he may hear of him, and so win the praise of men.’

Book 1, workbook lines 80–86

Unpacking the passage

“speak his mind to that crowd of Suitors”
The assembly will be a public stand in front of the community, the setting in which timē is claimed.
“to seek news of his father’s return”
The journey is the action that will earn the reputation.
“and so win the praise of men”
The praise lies in the future, after the journey, and it is something other people will say about him.

Kleos is promised in advance, for after the journey. It is future-facing, and it is a story other people will tell, which is what makes it kleos.

Scene 5 · Orestes as the model

‘You are no longer a child: you must put childish thoughts away. Have you not heard what a name Orestes made for himself in the world when he killed the cunning Aegisthus for murdering his noble father? You, my friend – and what a tall and splendid young man you have grown! – must be as brave as Orestes. Then future generations will sing your praises.’

Book 1, workbook lines 258–263

Unpacking the passage

“what a name Orestes made for himself in the world”
Orestes’s act is already a story that people have heard, which is kleos in the literal sense of the word.
“for murdering his noble father”
The name was won by avenging a father, the one action the poem presents as earning both kinds of honour.
“Then future generations will sing your praises”
Athene promises Telemachus kleos in the form of song, carried by people not yet born.

Avenging a father earns both immediate respect for the act and a story retold across generations as an example. This is the first of several times the poem uses Orestes to show Telemachus what earning both at once looks like.

Scene 6 · “I am master in this house”

‘… So go to your quarters now and attend to your own work, the loom and the spindle, and tell the servants to get on with theirs. Making decisions must be men’s concern, and mine in particular; for I am master in this house.’

Penelope was taken aback, but she retired to her own apartments, for she took her son’s sensible words to heart.

Book 1, workbook lines 311–316

Unpacking the passage

“go to your quarters now”
Telemachus gives an order in front of the household and the suitors, so his authority is seen by everyone present.
“I am master in this house”
He claims his standing within the oikos aloud, which is where timē is largely located.
“Penelope was taken aback”
His mother obeys, and the claim is accepted by the household.

When Penelope asks the bard to change his song, Telemachus tells her that the choice is his and sends her upstairs. It is a live assertion of timē in front of the household, and his first act of authority in the poem.

Kleos and timē in Books 2–4
Section 6 · Context

Book 2 · The assembly

  • Telemachus calls the first assembly in twenty years and publicly demands that the suitors leave. This is a live, public bid to reclaim timē in front of the whole community.
  • Antinous reveals Penelope’s three-year web trick. The deception protected the household’s standing by delaying a decision that would have damaged it either way.

Book 3 · Nestor at Pylos

  • Nestor’s grand public sacrifice and feast display his own timē: wealth and piety performed in front of his community.
  • Nestor tells Telemachus that he talks exactly as his father did. Personal recognition given now is timē, and the link to Odysseus’s continuing story is kleos by association, so this is one of the poem’s clearest moments of both at once.

Book 4 · Menelaus at Sparta

  • Menelaus’s palace, described in gold, silver and ivory, shows status through visible material display.
  • Helen and Menelaus each tell a different version of a Troy episode. Telling your own story in a particular way is an act of managing your future kleos.
  • Agamemnon, murdered on his return, has the wrong kind of kleos: he is remembered as a warning, closer to Aegisthus than to Orestes.
Earning both together
Section 7

Most actions earn one kind of honour. Hosting a guest correctly earns timē. Dying visibly in a heroic cause earns kleos but, since the person is gone, no timē. A small number of actions earn both, and these tend to be the poem’s most significant moments.

  • Winning through mētis or force earns timē if witnessed and kleos if later retold.
  • Avenging a murdered father, Orestes’s act, earns immediate respect and becomes a retold example.

That is why Orestes is held up twice in Books 1–3, and why Telemachus is being pushed towards the same double outcome. The comparison recurs because each time it is a live measurement of what Telemachus should already be doing.

Scholarly views to argue with
Section 8

These are positions for you to test. Decide how far the text supports each one, and mark your verdict.

E. R. Dodds
The Greeks and the Irrational (1951)
Homeric society is a shame culture. The highest good is public esteem and the worst evil is public disgrace; conscience in the modern sense hardly figures.
For
Telemachus’s shame that a guest has been kept waiting; the great name lament; the suitors’ indifference to how their behaviour looks. Everything is judged by an audience.
Against
Telemachus welcomes an unknown stranger with no one important watching, and Menelaus grounds hospitality in memory of his own need. Both look like internal motivation. Is the poem really without conscience?
How far does the text support this?
A. W. H. Adkins
Merit and Responsibility (1960)
Homeric values are competitive and results-based. Aretē means success, and a hero is judged by outcomes and standing, with intentions counting for little.
For
Timē is measured in visible things: land, gifts, seats, portions. Odysseus without them has almost no standing despite his fame. Agamemnon’s intentions do not save his reputation.
Against
The poem repeatedly credits intention and restraint: Telemachus’s tact over gifts, Athene’s indirect method, Penelope’s patience. Does the Odyssey share the Iliad’s competitive ethic, or soften it?
How far does the text support this?
Gregory Nagy
The Best of the Achaeans (1979)
Kleos is inseparable from poetry. The word means both the glory and the song that carries it, so a hero’s fame exists only as long as bards sing it; the poem itself is the instrument of kleos.
For
Phemius sings the returns in Book 1; Helen and Menelaus shape their stories at Sparta; Telemachus’s lament is precisely that no one will tell his father’s story.
Against
If kleos depends entirely on poets, it is out of the hero’s hands and depends on the poets’ honesty. Helen’s and Menelaus’s competing versions suggest that kleos can be managed and distorted, which weakens it as a measure of worth.
How far does the text support this?
Summary
Lesson 3 · Kleos and timē
Key points
  • Homeric society is a shame culture, in which a person’s worth is confirmed publicly, through how they are treated and the stories told about them.
  • Kleos is fame that survives death. It is given by others, carried by song, and can be infamous. Timē is honour shown now through treatment; it is visible, material and can change.
  • The test is simple: receiving honour now, in front of people, is timē, while being talked about afterwards is kleos.
  • In Book 1 the suitors deny Telemachus his timē 1.97–108, and Telemachus confers timē on his guest as host 1.113–135. The great name lament is about missing kleos 1.211–212, Athene promises Telemachus kleos 1.253–263, Orestes is the model of a man who earned both 1.258–263, and “I am master in this house” is Telemachus’s assertion of timē.
  • In Books 2–4 the assembly and Penelope’s web concern timē; Nestor’s sacrifice displays timē, and his recognition of Odysseus in his son gives both; Menelaus’s palace displays timē; Helen’s and Menelaus’s stories manage kleos; and Agamemnon is left with an infamous kleos.
  • Most acts earn one kind of honour. Avenging a father earns both, which is why the example of Orestes keeps recurring.
  • Dodds (shame culture), Adkins (results-based merit) and Nagy (kleos as song) can each be tested against Telemachus’s unrewarded welcome of a stranger and against Helen’s managed story.
