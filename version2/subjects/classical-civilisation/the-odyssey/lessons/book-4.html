Book 4 at a glance
Context

Telemachus reaches the wealthy palace of Menelaus and Helen at Sparta. Here he hears two stories about his father at Troy and, at last, real news that Odysseus is alive. Meanwhile, back on Ithaca, the suitors discover he has gone and plan to kill him.

Remember. Book 4 is context. You need to know the story and the people, but it is not examined in the same detail as Book 1.

Scene 1 · Arrival at Sparta
Context

Telemachus and Pisistratus arrive at Sparta during a double wedding celebration in the palace of Menelaus. The palace is described as dazzlingly wealthy, full of gold, silver and fine craftsmanship. Menelaus’s wife is Helen, whose earlier departure to Troy with the prince Paris was the original cause of the Trojan War; she has since been reconciled with Menelaus and has returned home with him.

M&H
Menelaus & Helen
King and queen of Sparta. Menelaus fought at Troy to win Helen back. Both now rule together, wealthy and, on the surface, at peace, though the war still shadows their conversation.

Helen recognises Telemachus almost immediately, struck by how closely he resembles his father. Menelaus agrees, and the young man is warmly welcomed once his identity is confirmed.

Scene 2 · Stories about Troy
Context

Over dinner, Helen and Menelaus each tell a story about an episode from the war involving Odysseus, and the two versions do not entirely agree. Helen describes recognising Odysseus when he slipped into Troy in disguise to spy, and claims she kept his secret. Menelaus instead tells the story of the Wooden Horse, and how Odysseus had to physically restrain the Greek soldiers hiding inside it from answering when Helen, walking around the horse, imitated the voices of their wives to trick them into giving themselves away.

Menelaus’s version

Three times you circled round our hollow lair, feeling the outside with your hands, and you called out the name of all the Argive captains in turn, altering your voice to mimic each man’s wife. Diomedes and I, who were sitting right in the middle with the good Odysseus, heard you calling and were both tempted to jump up and come out or give an instant answer from within. But Odysseus held us back and checked our impetuosity.

Book 4, workbook lines 244–250

Unpacking the passage

“Three times you circled round”
Menelaus speaks to Helen directly, and the “you” in his story is the person working against the Greeks.
“altering your voice to mimic each man’s wife”
Helen is shown using a trick to draw the hidden soldiers out, which sits awkwardly beside her own claim to have helped Odysseus.
“Odysseus held us back”
Odysseus is the one man who keeps control, even though Menelaus himself was tempted to answer.

Both stories present Odysseus as resourceful and clever. The fact that Helen and Menelaus tell such different versions is worth noticing, because each is subtly shaping the story to cast themselves in a better light.

Worth noticing. Neither version can simply be taken as the plain truth. Both Helen and Menelaus have reasons to tell the story their own way.

Scene 3 · Proteus and the news of Odysseus
Context

Menelaus then tells the long story of his own journey home, which took years longer than expected. Stranded by bad winds, he was forced to capture and hold on to Proteus, a shape-shifting sea-god known as the Old Man of the Sea, who could see the future but had to be physically restrained through a series of transformations before he would answer questions honestly.

Pr
Proteus
A minor sea-god who is not one of the major Olympians. His knowledge of the future has to be earned by force, by holding on to him through his shape-changes.

From Proteus, Menelaus learned several important pieces of news: that the hero Ajax had died on his way home, that Agamemnon had been murdered by Aegisthus on his return, and that Odysseus was alive but trapped on an island, held by the nymph Calypso and unable to continue his journey home.

“The third,” said Proteus, “is Odysseus, Laertes’ son, whose home is in Ithaca. I caught a glimpse of him on an island, in the Nymph Calypso’s home, with tears streaming down his cheeks. She keeps him captive there, for without a ship and crew to carry him so far across the sea it is impossible for him to reach his home.”

Book 4, workbook lines 481–486

Unpacking the passage

“The third”
Odysseus comes last in a list of Greek leaders, after Ajax and Agamemnon, both of whom are dead.
“with tears streaming down his cheeks”
Odysseus is alive, but he is unhappy where he is.
“without a ship and crew”
Proteus explains why Odysseus cannot leave: he has no means of making the journey.

Key moment. This is the most concrete, direct news of Odysseus that Telemachus receives anywhere in the Telemachy.

Scene 4 · Meanwhile, on Ithaca
Context

The action briefly shifts back to Ithaca. The suitors discover that Telemachus has secretly left the island, and they are furious and alarmed, since his journey suggests he is finally acting against them. Antinous proposes an ambush: a group of suitors will lie in wait with a ship and kill Telemachus at sea on his way home, before he can cause any more trouble.

Remember. This ambush plot is left unresolved at the end of Book 4. It sets up real danger for Telemachus’s return journey, which is still hanging over the story.

Penelope learns both that her son has left without telling her and that the suitors are now plotting to murder him. She is overwhelmed with grief and fear. Athene takes pity on her and sends a comforting dream in the shape of Penelope’s own sister, Iphthime, reassuring her that Telemachus is under the gods’ protection and will come to no harm.
