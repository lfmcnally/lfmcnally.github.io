Hunting and the Elite

What the Mycenaeans hunted, how and why, and what the evidence (above all the dagger blade from Grave Circle A) can and cannot tell us.
Practise this →
Lesson 2.2

At a glance

At a glance
  • the spec asks for the evidence for hunting and the nature of hunting: be ready to say what hunting was like and how we know
  • almost all the evidence is elite art (an inlaid dagger, frescoes, gold rings and cups), so it shows hunting as the rich wanted it seen
  • the big idea: for the ruling class, hunting was a display of courage and power, linked to war and kingship

Why they hunted

Food, protection and status

Food and materials
  • most meat came from farm animals (sheep, goats, pigs and cattle), but bones of deer and wild boar from Mycenaean sites show that game was eaten too
  • hunting also supplied materials: hides and skins, antler, and boar tusks for the boar's tusk helmet (2.3)
Protecting the flocks
  • wolves and lions were a threat to sheep and cattle, so killing predators protected the herds the palaces depended on (the Linear B tablets record tens of thousands of sheep)
  • Homer's similes are full of lions raiding the farmyard and herdsmen driving them off, which suggests this was a familiar danger
Status, courage and kingship
  • hunting large, dangerous animals at close quarters with a spear showed courage, the quality a warrior-ruler most needed
  • the lion hunt was the ultimate test; in Egypt and the Near East kings were shown hunting lions to show their power over wild, dangerous forces, and the Mycenaean elite borrowed the image
  • hunting in a group with hounds, spears and shields was also practice for war: the same weapons, teamwork and nerve
  • only the wealthy could afford hounds, horses, chariots and time to hunt, so hunting itself marked a man as elite

How they hunted

Weapons, hounds, nets and chariots

Weapons
  • spears: long, bronze-tipped, used for thrusting at close range, the main hunting weapon
  • bows and arrows: an archer appears on the dagger blade, and arrowheads of bronze, flint and obsidian were buried in the shaft graves
  • shields: on the dagger the hunters carry huge body-shields, the same ones used in battle
Hounds, nets and chariots
  • hounds: the boar hunt fresco from Tiryns shows dogs with collars leaping at a boar; in the Odyssey, Odysseus' old dog Argos was once used to hunt wild goats, deer and hares
  • nets and traps: one of the gold Vapheio cups shows a wild bull caught in a net strung between trees, so some animals were trapped and caught alive
  • chariots: a gold signet ring from Shaft Grave IV shows an archer shooting at a stag from a moving chariot, and a Tiryns fresco shows women driving out in a chariot, perhaps to watch a hunt
What a hunt was like
  • hunts were group events: beaters and hounds drove the animal out, then hunters closed in with spears
  • they were dangerous: the dagger shows a hunter already down under the lion, and Odysseus is gashed above the knee by a boar's tusk (Odyssey 19)
  • women of the elite seem to have taken part as spectators or drivers (the Tiryns chariot fresco), so a hunt could also be a social occasion

The dagger blade

Prescribed source · Grave Circle A, Mycenae

Prescribed source
Object
bronze dagger blade decorated on both sides
Date
c.1550 to 1500 BC
Found
Shaft Grave IV, Grave Circle A, Mycenae, excavated by Heinrich Schliemann in 1876
Now in
National Archaeological Museum, Athens
Technique
inlay: figures cut from gold and silver set into a strip of black niello in the bronze
Appearance and content
  • side one: a group of hunters faces a charging lion; one hunter has fallen beneath it, the others press in with long spears, and one is an archer drawing his bow; two more lions run away
  • the hunters carry the two great body-shields of the period: the tower shield (tall and rectangular) and the figure-of-eight shield
  • side two: a lion pounces on a herd of fleeing deer or antelope, a hunt in the animal world to match the human one
  • the figures are arranged to fit the tapering blade, so the scene narrows towards the point
Style and technique
  • niello is a black paste of metal and sulphur; set into the bronze, it makes a dark background so that the gold and silver figures stand out, almost like a painting in metal
  • the figures are lively and full of movement: the lion's leap, the fallen hunter and the fleeing lions make the scene dramatic
  • the technique is very rare and skilled; similar inlaid daggers come from Egypt and Crete, so the maker may have learned it abroad, or been a Cretan craftsman working for a Mycenaean lord
Purpose, and how it shaped the object
  • it was almost certainly never used: a thrust would ruin the inlay, so it was made for display, as a mark of the owner's rank
  • it was buried with its owner in a royal shaft grave, among other weapons and gold, to show his status in death
  • because it was made to impress, the maker chose a heroic subject (men facing a lion), costly materials and a slow, difficult technique
Interpretations
  • to its owner and his people: a picture of the courage expected of a ruler; burying it with him linked him to that ideal
  • to a modern historian: evidence for weapons and shields, for group hunting, for high-quality craftsmanship and for contact with Crete and Egypt (the gold, silver and the technique itself)
  • a problem: we do not know how common lions were in Greece; a few lion bones from Tiryns suggest some survived, but the lion hunt may be a borrowed artistic theme more than a record of a real event

Other evidence

Frescoes, rings, cups and Homer

Visual and material sources
  • boar hunt fresco, Tiryns (13th century BC): hounds with collars attack a boar that has been speared; it decorated the palace walls
  • women in a chariot, Tiryns (fresco): two women drive out, perhaps to the hunt
  • gold signet ring, Shaft Grave IV: an archer in a chariot shoots at a stag
  • Vapheio cups (gold, from a tholos tomb near Sparta, c.1500 BC): wild bulls are caught, one tangled in a net between trees
  • gold lion's head rhyton, Shaft Grave IV (prescribed source): a ritual pouring vessel in the shape of a lion's head; like the Lion Gate, it shows the lion as a symbol of royal power
  • boar's tusk helmets: each needed the tusks of many boars, so a helmet was proof of hunting success
Literary sources (useful for unseen questions)
  • Odyssey 19: the young Odysseus hunts boar on Mount Parnassus with his uncles; the hounds pick up the scent, the boar charges from its thicket, gashes his leg, and Odysseus kills it with his spear
  • Odyssey 17: the dog Argos, once a fine hunting hound, lies neglected on a dung heap and dies when he recognises his master
  • Iliad 9: the story of the great boar of Calydon, sent by Artemis, hunted by a band of heroes
  • these were composed centuries later, so they show what later Greeks believed about heroic hunting; treat them as supporting evidence only

Usefulness & interpretation

What the sources can and cannot show

Every source is evidence of something; the skill is saying exactly what, and what it cannot show.

Source
Useful for
Limits
the dagger blade
weapons, shields, group hunting, elite values, craftsmanship and trade

Homer
how a hunt felt: hounds, danger, wounds
composed about 400 years later, and it is poetry, written to entertain
Almost all our evidence for hunting was made for the elite and shows hunting as they wanted it remembered: brave, glamorous and dangerous. It tells us more about values than about the everyday business of getting food.
Link to Homer
  • Odysseus' boar hunt (Odyssey 19) matches the art closely: group hunting, hounds, spears and real danger
  • Homer's heroes are constantly compared to lions in similes, so the lion hunt sits at the heart of the heroic ideal, just as on the dagger
  • the body-shields on the blade recall Ajax's shield “like a tower” in the Iliad, a memory of equipment long out of use by Homer's day

Exam skills

Describing a source, an 8-mark answer and a 15-mark plan

Describing a source (short questions)
  • say what it is, what it is made of, and where it was found
  • describe the content in detail: who, doing what, with what weapons
  • for “explain” questions, add why: link each detail to purpose or context (“the costly materials show...”)
Modelled 8-mark answer

How useful is the dagger blade from Grave Circle A for understanding Mycenaean hunting? Aim for about three pairs from the source and three from your own knowledge, each pair a fact (AO1) and what it shows (AO2), then a judgement.

What you know (AO1)
What it shows (AO2)
the hunters carry long spears and one has a bow
useful for the weapons used: hunters fought at close range with spears, supported by archers

the Tiryns fresco shows hounds hunting boar, and bones show deer were eaten
the dagger shows only one, glamorous kind of hunt, so we need other sources for everyday hunting

Judgement: very useful for the weapons and the values of the elite hunter, but less useful for how ordinary people hunted, because it was a luxury object made to impress.

Planning a 15-mark essay

“Hunting was more about status than survival.” How far do you agree? Use a range of sources.

  • status: the dagger blade (gold, a royal grave, a heroic lion hunt); the gold rhyton and Lion Gate (the lion as royal symbol); the chariot ring (only the rich had chariots)
  • survival: animal bones (deer and boar eaten); protecting flocks from lions and wolves; materials such as hides and tusks
  • both: the boar's tusk helmet (a practical helmet that also showed off hunting success)
  • judgement: for the elite, mostly status, since that is what nearly all the evidence shows; but the evidence was made for the elite, so it may hide the practical hunting of ordinary people
