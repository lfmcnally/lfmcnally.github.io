Linear B

The palace records of the Mycenaeans: how the clay tablets were made and preserved, what they record, how they were deciphered, and why they matter.
Practise this →
Lesson 2.5

At a glance

At a glance
  • the spec asks for how the tablets were preserved, what they record, and their significance: learn all three
  • the tablets were everyday palace paperwork, never meant to last; they survive only because the palaces burned
  • they are our only writing from the Mycenaean world, and they prove its people spoke Greek

The script

Signs, scribes, and the loss of writing

The script
  • Linear B was adapted from Linear A, the script of Minoan Crete, which is still undeciphered
  • it has about 90 syllable signs, each standing for a syllable such as ti, ri or po, so ti-ri-po spells “tripod”
  • it also uses ideograms: small pictures of the thing being counted (a tripod, a sheep, a chariot, a jar)
  • and a system of numbers (in tens, like ours) and signs for weights and measures
  • it is clumsy for Greek: final consonants are left out, and some sounds share signs, so words can be hard to read
Who wrote them
  • trained scribes; different handwriting shows dozens worked at Knossos and Pylos
  • there is no sign that most people could read; writing seems to have been a specialist palace skill
  • when the palaces fell around 1200 BC, writing disappeared from Greece for about four hundred years, until the Greeks adopted the Phoenician alphabet

How they survived

Damp clay, and a lucky fire

How the tablets were made
  • scribes pressed signs into damp clay with a pointed tool
  • two main shapes: long, narrow “palm-leaf” tablets for short notes and larger “page” tablets for longer lists
  • the clay was left to dry in the air, never fired; it was meant for records that lasted a season or a year, then thrown away, or the clay reused
  • tablets were stored in wicker baskets or wooden boxes, with clay labels saying what was inside
  • the information was probably copied onto something else for permanent records (perhaps leather or wood), which has not survived
How they survived
  • when the palaces were destroyed by fire, the heat baked the clay hard, as if in a kiln
  • so they were preserved by accident, in the very fire that destroyed the palace
  • so the tablets record only the palace's last months, a snapshot of the moment it fell, much like Pompeii in AD 79
  • at Pylos, the American archaeologist Carl Blegen found the archive room on the first day of digging, in 1939; at Knossos, Arthur Evans had found thousands of tablets from 1900

What they record

Goods, people, gods and war

Goods and the economy
  • livestock: huge flocks of sheep (around 100,000 in the Knossos records), and goats, pigs and cattle
  • wool, linen and cloth: targets for weavers, and finished cloth
  • food: grain, figs, olive oil, wine and spices, collected from the region and handed out as rations
  • land: who held which plots, and on what terms
  • craft: bronze-smiths given metal to work, perfume-makers given oil and spices
People
  • titles: the wanax (king), the lawagetas (perhaps a war-leader), and local officials called basileus, a word that later meant “king”
  • workers: women spinners and weavers, with their children, paid in rations; some women came from Miletus, Knidos and Lemnos, probably as captives
  • slaves, craftsmen, shepherds and soldiers
Religion and war
  • offerings to gods, many of them known later: Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hermes, Artemis and even Dionysus, and a goddess called Potnia (“Mistress”)
  • one Pylos tablet sends gold vessels to gods at a shrine, and some scholars think it lists people as offerings too; the reading is disputed
  • military records: chariots, wheels, horses, corslets, spears and arrows; at Pylos, rowers sent to the coast and watchers posted along the shore, perhaps just before the palace fell
What they do not record
  • no history, letters, laws, stories, poems or prayers
  • no dates in our sense, no names of the kings, no account of why the palaces fell

The tripod tablet

Prescribed source · Palace of Pylos

Prescribed source
Object
a “page” tablet listing vessels
Date
about 1200 BC, baked in the fire that destroyed the palace
Found
the palace of Pylos, by Carl Blegen's team in 1952
Now in
National Archaeological Museum, Athens
Material
clay, inscribed with a pointed tool
Appearance and content
  • three lines of signs, each word followed by an ideogram (a small picture of the vessel) and a number
  • the first line lists tripod cauldrons (three-legged bronze pots), spelled ti-ri-po or ti-ri-po-de, with a picture of a tripod; one is described as Cretan work, and one as burnt at the legs
  • the other lines list jars or goblets called di-pa (later Greek depas), described by size and by the number of handles: four, three or none
  • it belongs to a set of tablets recording an inspection of the palace's precious furniture and vessels
Why this tablet matters
  • Ventris had published his decipherment in 1952, arguing the language was Greek; many scholars were unconvinced
  • when this tablet was read in 1953, using his values for the signs, the words matched the pictures: ti-ri-po-de sat beside a tripod, and the words for “four-handled”, “three-handled” and “no-handled” sat beside jars with four, three and no handles
  • so it was the clearest proof that the decipherment was right and that the Mycenaeans wrote Greek
  • it also shows both kinds of sign (syllables and ideograms) side by side, and the palace's careful checking of its wealth, down to a damaged tripod
Purpose, and how it shaped the tablet
  • it was a working record for palace officials, so it is plain and practical: a list, with no decoration
  • it was never meant to last, so its survival is pure chance; that is true of every tablet

Decipherment

How Ventris cracked the script

How Linear B was deciphered
  • Michael Ventris, an English architect who had been fascinated by the script since boyhood, worked on it for years using grids of signs
  • he expected a lost, non-Greek language, but when he tested Cretan place names (Knossos, Amnisos) the words came out as Greek
  • in 1952 he announced that Linear B was an early form of Greek, and worked with the scholar John Chadwick to publish the proof
  • the tripod tablet confirmed it in 1953; Ventris died in a car crash in 1956, aged 34

Significance & interpretation

Why the tablets matter, and their limits

Why the tablets matter
  • they are our only written evidence from the Mycenaean world; without them we would know almost nothing about how it was run
  • they prove the Mycenaeans spoke Greek, so they are the ancestors of later Greek culture
  • they show a highly organised palace economy, controlling land, flocks, craft and labour through careful records
  • they name gods worshipped later in Greece, showing religion carried on through the Dark Age
  • they give glimpses of people usually invisible in the evidence: women workers, children, slaves
Limits
  • they are administrative lists: little about beliefs, feelings, events or everyday life outside the palace
  • they record only the last months before the fires
  • many words are still uncertain, and the script's spelling makes some readings guesses
  • they show what the palace cared about, so ordinary people appear only as workers and numbers
Link to Homer
  • the clearest place where Homer and the evidence disagree: his heroes cannot read or write; the only mention of writing, the “deadly signs” Bellerophon carries in Iliad 6, is treated as strange
  • the poems know nothing of the palace bureaucracy, its scribes and its records
  • but the tablets also support Homer: Greek speakers, many of the same gods, the titles wanax (Homer's anax, “lord”) and basileus, and Pylos as a great palace, the home of Homer's King Nestor
A pile of accidentally baked palace lists is our only writing from this world, and it was enough to prove that the Mycenaeans were Greeks.

Exam skills

An 8-mark answer and a 15-mark plan

Modelled 8-mark answer

How useful is the tripod tablet for understanding the Mycenaean world?

What you know (AO1)
What it shows (AO2)
it lists tripods and jars, counted and described
shows a palace that recorded and checked its wealth in detail
one tripod is “burnt at the legs”
shows how careful the inspections were, noting even damage
it uses syllable signs with ideograms
shows how the script worked and how scribes made records clear
the words matched the pictures when read with Ventris's values
it proved the language was Greek, linking the Mycenaeans to later Greeks
it was baked by the fire that destroyed the palace
it shows only the last moment of the palace, by accident
it is a plain list, with no events or beliefs
it tells us little about everyday life, ideas or history, so other sources are needed

Judgement: very useful for the language of the Mycenaeans and the workings of the palace economy; much less useful for what people thought or how most of them lived.

Planning a 15-mark essay

“The Linear B tablets are the most important evidence for life in the Mycenaean age.” How far do you agree? Use a range of sources.

  • agree: the only writing; prove Greek; show the economy, workers, gods, titles, military preparations (use the tripod tablet, sheep and wool lists, rowers tablets)
  • other evidence matters as much: sites (Mycenae, Tiryns: walls, palaces); grave goods (Grave Circles A and B: wealth, trade, warrior identity); art (the dagger blade, Warrior Vase, frescoes: values, dress, warfare)
  • judgement: the tablets are unique for how the palaces were run, but they are silent on beliefs, art and death, so they are most powerful combined with the archaeology
