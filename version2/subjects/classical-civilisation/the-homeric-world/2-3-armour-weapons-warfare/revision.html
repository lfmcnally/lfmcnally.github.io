Armour, Weapons and Warfare

How Mycenaean warriors were armed and protected, how that changed over four centuries, how they fought, and what the Warrior Vase can tell us.
Practise this →
Lesson 2.3

At a glance

At a glance
  • the spec asks for the evidence for and nature of armour and weapons: know each piece of equipment, how it was used, and which source shows it
  • equipment changed over time, from huge body-shields and long swords to smaller shields and shorter swords, so always say when a source comes from
  • warfare runs through Mycenaean art, walls and records, and the elite were buried with their weapons

Weapons

Spears, swords, bows and slings

Spears
  • the main weapon: a long wooden shaft with a bronze head, held in both hands or one hand to thrust at close range
  • later, shorter spears and javelins were also thrown (the warriors on the back of the Warrior Vase seem about to throw)
Swords and daggers
  • early swords (16th and 15th centuries BC) were very long and narrow, made for thrusting; the shaft graves held dozens, some with gold-covered hilts
  • later swords (13th and 12th centuries BC) were shorter and sturdier, for cutting as well as thrusting
  • daggers were carried too, some richly decorated (the dagger blade in 2.2)
Bows and slings
  • arrowheads of bronze, flint and obsidian were buried in the shaft graves
  • the silver Siege Rhyton shows archers and slingers defending a city
  • Linear B tablets from Knossos record thousands of arrows in the palace stores

Armour

Shields, helmets and body armour, and how they changed

Shields
  • tower shield: tall, rectangular and curved, covering a man from neck to ankle
  • figure-of-eight shield: pinched in at the middle, so it looks like an 8 from the front
  • both were made of layers of ox-hide on a wooden frame, and hung from a strap over the shoulder, so the warrior could swing them round to cover his body
  • heavy and awkward, they gave way to smaller round shields held by a handle, which allowed faster movement
Helmets
  • boar's tusk helmet: a leather or felt cap covered with rows of plates cut from boars' tusks; one helmet needed the tusks of many boars (estimates run from about ten to forty), so it showed its owner's wealth and hunting success
  • later helmets were made of bronze or of leather with bronze fittings; the Warrior Vase shows horned helmets and spiky (“hedgehog”) helmets
Body armour
  • the Dendra panoply (c.1400 BC): a complete suit of bronze plates covering the body from neck to thighs, with shoulder guards and a collar, found in a chamber tomb at Dendra near Mycenae with a boar's tusk helmet
  • it weighs around 15 kg and restricts movement, so it was probably worn by a warrior who fought from, or near, a chariot
  • greaves (shin guards) of bronze have been found, and the Warrior Vase shows them worn by ordinary soldiers
  • Linear B tablets list corslets (body armour) issued by the palace, so the palace equipped its fighters

The Warrior Vase

Prescribed source · House of the Warrior, Mycenae

Prescribed source
Object
a krater, a large bowl for mixing wine with water
Date
c.1200 BC, the very end of the palace period
Found
the House of the Warrior, inside the citadel of Mycenae near Grave Circle A, excavated by Schliemann in 1876
Now in
National Archaeological Museum, Athens
Material
clay, painted in dark paint on the pale surface
Appearance and content
  • front: six warriors march in step to the left, all dressed and armed alike
  • each wears a horned helmet, a corslet over a short fringed tunic and greaves, and carries a round shield cut away at the bottom and a long spear
  • a small bag hangs from each spear, perhaps rations for a march
  • on the far left a woman raises her arm towards them
  • back: five warriors in spiky “hedgehog” helmets hold their spears up, as if about to throw or charge
  • the handles of the bowl are decorated with bulls' heads
Style
  • simple, repetitive figures in profile, painted as a band (frieze) round the bowl
  • big noses and eyes, stick-like legs: a stylised look, far from the fine detail of earlier palace frescoes
  • the repetition makes the soldiers look like a disciplined unit, all equal
Purpose, and how it shaped the object
  • a krater was used at feasts, so men drinking together would see these warriors as the wine was served
  • the scene therefore celebrates warriors and military values to an audience of warriors
  • the band running round the bowl suits a marching line, so the shape of the pot shaped the scene
Interpretations
  • departure: soldiers setting off for war, with the woman waving farewell
  • mourning: her raised arm may be a gesture of grief, a common gesture in Greek art for mourning the dead
  • a parade or the men of one household or town; and some read it as a sign of a world under threat, since it dates to the time the palaces were falling
  • to the original audience, a proud picture of their own warriors; to us, our clearest picture of late Mycenaean infantry kit

Warfare & other evidence

How they fought, and how we know

How they fought
  • sieges and defence: the Cyclopean walls of Mycenae and Tiryns, and the Siege Rhyton from Shaft Grave IV, which shows a city on a hill under attack, with defenders, archers and slingers, and women watching from the walls
  • raids by land and sea, for cattle, metal and captives; Linear B tablets from Pylos list women workers from the coast of Asia Minor, probably captives
  • chariots carried elite warriors to and around the battlefield (see 2.4)
  • the Pylos battle fresco: Mycenaean warriors in boar's tusk helmets and greaves fight men dressed in animal skins
  • the end: Pylos tablets that send rowers to the coast and set watchers along the shore suggest a palace preparing for attack, perhaps just before it burned
Other evidence
  • shaft graves (Grave Circles A and B): rulers buried with swords, daggers, spearheads and arrowheads, so being a warrior was part of royal identity
  • the dagger blade (2.2): tower and figure-of-eight shields in use
  • surviving boar's tusk helmets and plates from many tombs
  • the Dendra panoply
  • Linear B: chariots, corslets, spears and arrows counted and issued by the palace
Homer (useful for unseen questions)
  • Iliad 10: Odysseus is given a boar's tusk helmet, described in detail; it was an antique by Homer's day, so the description is a genuine memory of the Bronze Age
  • Iliad 7: Ajax carries a shield “like a tower” made of seven layers of ox-hide and one of bronze
  • Iliad 6: as Hector walks, the rim of his huge shield knocks against his neck and his ankles, exactly the body-shield of the early Mycenaeans
  • Homer's heroes fight with bronze weapons, though iron was in use in his own time
  • but they often throw their spears and use round shields too, details from later centuries, so the poems mix periods

Judgement: very useful for the kit and organisation of ordinary soldiers around 1200 BC; to understand Mycenaean warfare across the whole age, it must be combined with earlier sources.

Planning a 15-mark essay

“The Mycenaeans were a warlike people.” How far do you agree? Use a range of sources.

  • agree: Cyclopean walls; weapons in the shaft graves; the Siege Rhyton and Pylos battle fresco; the Warrior Vase; Linear B arms lists and rowers; boar's tusk helmets
  • challenge: much of the evidence shows trade, craft, farming and religion (storerooms, Linear B sheep and wool lists, imported amber and ivory); much of the “war” art is display for the elite, made for graves and palace walls
  • judgement: war and the image of the warrior mattered greatly to the ruling class; whether ordinary people were “warlike” is harder to say, since the evidence comes mostly from the elite
