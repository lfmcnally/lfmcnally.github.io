Chariots, Clothing and Trade
At a glance
Key facts
  • Chariots: light, two-wheeled, two horses; for war, hunting, travel and display
  • Clothing: wool and linen; women in flounced skirts and short-sleeved jackets, men in tunics and kilts
  • Trade: by sea; copper, tin, gold, ivory, amber and glass in; olive oil, perfume, wine and pottery out
  • Prescribed source: fresco of a lady holding a necklace, House of the Chief Priest, Mycenae
At a glance
  • for each topic the spec asks for the evidence for it and its nature: be ready to describe what it was like and name the sources that show it
  • cloth, wood and leather rot, so very little survives: we rely on art, Linear B, and objects that travelled
  • the big idea: an elite who displayed wealth through chariots and fine dress, and who needed trade to get the metals Greece lacked
Chariots
What they were like, what they were for, and the evidence
What a chariot was like
  • a light, two-wheeled vehicle pulled by two horses, carrying a driver and one passenger, usually a warrior
  • wheels usually had four spokes; the body was a light frame of wood or wicker, covered with leather
  • designs changed: an early box chariot (on the shaft grave stelae), then a chariot with curved side wings, and later a lighter, open rail chariot
What chariots were used for
  • war: to carry elite warriors to and around the battlefield; Greece is rocky and hilly, so huge chariot charges like those of Egypt and the Near East are unlikely, and warriors probably got down to fight on foot
  • hunting: a gold ring from Shaft Grave IV shows an archer shooting a stag from a chariot
  • travel and display: a Tiryns fresco shows two women driving a chariot, so elite women used them too
  • races and funerals: Homer's heroes race chariots in the funeral games for Patroclus (Iliad 23)
  • status: horses and chariots were very expensive to keep, so owning one marked a man as elite
Roads and the palace
  • Mycenaean roads linked the citadels, with stone kerbs and drains, and bridges such as the Arkadiko bridge on the road from Mycenae towards Epidaurus, built of Cyclopean blocks and still standing
  • the roads were wide enough for chariots, and their building shows a palace able to organise large projects
  • Linear B tablets from Knossos list chariots, wheels, horses and armour issued to named men, sometimes noting the condition of the wheels, so the palace supplied and checked chariots
Evidence for chariots
  • stelae (grave markers) over the shaft graves in Grave Circle A: carved chariot scenes, the earliest images
  • the gold ring from Shaft Grave IV (a chariot hunt)
  • frescoes from Tiryns and Pylos
  • “chariot kraters”: painted mixing bowls showing chariots, many exported to Cyprus
  • Linear B tablets and the chariot ideogram (a picture-sign)
  • clay figurines of horses and chariots
  • no complete chariot survives, since wood and leather rot, so reconstructions rely on these images
Clothing
Making cloth, women's and men's dress, and the evidence
Making cloth
  • the two main fibres were wool (from sheep) and linen (from flax)
  • Linear B tablets from Knossos record flocks of around 100,000 sheep, kept for wool; Pylos tablets record flax, so cloth was a major palace industry
  • groups of women spun and wove for the palace and were paid in rations of grain and figs, with their children listed alongside them
  • some of these women came from Miletus, Knidos and Lemnos, across the Aegean, and were probably captives or slaves
  • the tablets name types of cloth and garments, including the chiton (tunic, written ki-to) and pharea (cloths or cloaks, pa-we-a)
  • dyes came from plants and from murex shellfish, which gave an expensive purple
Women's dress
  • a long skirt, often flounced (in tiers of frills) and patterned
  • a close-fitting jacket or bodice with short sleeves, sometimes cut open at the front, a style taken from Minoan Crete and perhaps worn for ritual
  • a belt at a narrow waist
  • jewellery: necklaces, bracelets, rings, hair ornaments; long hair in curls and ringlets, tied with ribbons and beads
  • evidence: the lady with a necklace; a procession fresco from Tiryns of women carrying offerings in this dress; clay phi and psi figurines in long dresses painted with stripes
  • in Grave Circle A, Grave III, the women were buried in clothes covered with hundreds of gold discs and ornaments sewn onto the cloth
Men's dress
  • a short tunic to the knee or mid-thigh, belted; or a kilt or loincloth, as on the hunters of the dagger blade
  • soldiers on the Warrior Vase wear a short fringed tunic under a corslet, with greaves
  • long robes for ceremonies: the lyre-player in the Pylos throne room wears one
  • cloaks for warmth and travel; Homer's heroes wear a cloak (pharos) fastened with a pin
  • hair often long; beards appear on some gold masks from the shaft graves; many figures in art are barefoot
Clothing in Homer (useful for unseen questions)
  • Penelope weaves and unweaves a shroud for Laertes to delay the suitors (Odyssey 2 and 19)
  • Nausicaa and her maids wash the palace clothes in the river (Odyssey 6)
  • Queen Arete recognises the cloak and tunic Odysseus wears because she made them herself (Odyssey 7)
  • fine clothes are gifts in guest-friendship, given to departing guests
  • like the Linear B tablets, Homer shows weaving as women's work and fine cloth as wealth
The lady holding a necklace
Prescribed source · House of the Chief Priest, Mycenae
Prescribed source
Object
part of a wall painting (fresco)
Date
13th century BC
Found
the House of the Chief Priest, in the Cult Centre on the slope of the citadel of Mycenae
Technique
painted on lime plaster, with mineral colours
Appearance and content
  • a woman seen in profile, holding up a necklace of beads in one hand
  • she wears a short-sleeved jacket decorated with patterned bands, and bracelets on her wrists
  • her hair is long and dark, falling in curls down her back, with a band or ornaments in it
  • her skin is painted pale: in Mycenaean painting, as in Egyptian and Cretan, women were shown pale and men a darker red-brown
  • her eye is drawn large and almond-shaped, as if seen from the front, though her face is in profile, another convention of the style
Purpose, and how it shaped the painting
  • it decorated a room in the Cult Centre, a group of shrines, so it is probably a religious scene
  • the necklace may be an offering to a goddess, or the woman may be a goddess receiving it, or a priestess
  • because it was made for a sacred building, the woman is shown in her finest, perhaps ceremonial, dress
What it tells us about clothing
  • useful for: elite women's dress and jewellery, hairstyles, the skill of painters and the colours available
  • limits: a single fragment; an idealised religious image; it shows the richest women in special dress, and tells us nothing about everyday clothes or ordinary people
Trade
What was traded, with whom, how, and the evidence
Why the Mycenaeans needed trade
  • bronze, the metal of weapons and tools, is made from copper and tin; Greece had little copper and almost no tin
  • Greece also lacked gold, ivory, amber, glass and precious stones, all of which the elite used to show their status
  • it could offer olive oil, perfumed oil, wine, fine pottery and cloth

What came in and what went out.

Imports (and where from)
Exports
copper: Cyprus, cast into “ox-hide” ingots shaped like a stretched hide
olive oil and perfumed oil, carried in stirrup jars (closed jars with a spout)
tin: from far to the east, through the Near East; perhaps also from western Europe
wine
gold: Egypt and Nubia
fine painted pottery, found from Italy to the Levant
ivory: elephant and hippopotamus, from Syria and Egypt
cloth, probably, though it does not survive
amber: the Baltic, in northern Europe (found in Grave Circles A and B)
metalwork and crafted goods
glass, faience and lapis lazuli: Egypt and the Near East
Where Mycenaean goods went, and how we know
  • Mycenaean pottery has been found in Cyprus, the Levant (such as Ugarit in Syria), Egypt (at Amarna, the capital of the pharaoh Akhenaten), the coast of Asia Minor (Miletus and Troy), Sicily, southern Italy and Sardinia
  • Egyptian objects at Mycenae include plaques of faience bearing the name of the pharaoh Amenhotep III (14th century BC)
  • an Egyptian statue base of the same pharaoh lists Aegean place names, apparently including Mycenae, Nauplion and Kythera, so the Egyptians knew the Mycenaean world
  • Hittite letters from Anatolia mention a land called Ahhiyawa, probably the Mycenaean Greeks (Homer's “Achaeans”), whose king the Hittite king addresses as a fellow great king
  • Linear B uses words borrowed from the Near East for gold (ku-ru-so), sesame, cumin and the tunic (ki-to), and labels some goods and people ku-pi-ri-jo, “Cypriot”
How trade worked
  • by sea, mostly: land travel over mountains was slow and costly; ships with oars and a sail hugged the coast and island-hopped
  • sailing was safe only from about late spring to early autumn; winter storms closed the sea
  • there was no coinage: goods were bartered, and metal measured by weight served as a standard of value; gold balance pans were buried in Grave III of Grave Circle A
  • much exchange was probably gifts between rulers, as in Homer and in Egyptian royal letters; there were probably private merchants too, and historians debate how far the palaces controlled trade
The Uluburun shipwreck
  • found in 1982 off the south coast of Turkey by a sponge diver; it sank around 1300 BC
  • it carried about ten tonnes of copper (over 350 ox-hide ingots) and about one tonne of tin, the right mix to make bronze
  • also: glass ingots, elephant and hippo ivory, ebony, amber beads, jars of resin, a gold scarab of the Egyptian queen Nefertiti, and Mycenaean pottery
  • personal objects from several cultures, including some Mycenaean ones, suggest a mixed crew or passengers
  • it is the best evidence for the scale and range of Bronze Age sea trade, and the ship was probably sailing towards the Aegean; but the ship itself was probably from the Levant or Cyprus, so it shows the trade the Mycenaeans were part of, more than Mycenaean shipping
Trade in Homer (useful for unseen questions)
  • Iliad 7: ships from Lemnos bring wine, and the Greeks barter for it with bronze, iron, hides, cattle and slaves
  • Odyssey 1: Athena, in disguise, says she is sailing to trade iron for copper
  • Odyssey 4: Helen and Menelaus have gifts from Egyptian Thebes, including a silver basket on wheels
  • Odyssey 15: the swineherd Eumaeus was kidnapped as a boy by Phoenician traders and sold as a slave
  • Odyssey 8: a young Phaeacian insults Odysseus by saying he looks like a merchant who cares only about cargo and profit, showing aristocratic contempt for trade

Together the sources show a rich, connected world: chariots and fine dress displayed the elite's status, and trade brought in the metals and luxuries that made that display possible.
Link to Homer
  • chariots in Homer work much as the evidence suggests: heroes ride to battle and fight on foot, and Telemachus drives by chariot from Pylos to Sparta (Odyssey 3 to 4), which fits the Mycenaean road network
  • clothing as women's work and fine cloth as wealth and gifts match the Linear B tablets
  • Homer's world of gift-exchange and suspicion of merchants matches what we can infer about elite exchange
  • but Homer's heroes live without palace records, and many of his traders are Phoenicians, a people of his own day, so the poems mix the Bronze Age with later times

How useful is the fresco of a lady holding a necklace for understanding Mycenaean clothing?

shows elaborate hairstyles, which took time and servants to arrange

Planning a 15-mark essay

“Trade was the key to the wealth of the Mycenaean rulers.” How far do you agree? Use a range of sources.

  • agree: gold, amber and ivory in Grave Circles A and B; bronze needed imported copper and tin; the Uluburun wreck; Mycenaean pottery across the Mediterranean; Egyptian objects at Mycenae
  • other sources of wealth: land and farming (Linear B sheep, wool, flax, oil, grain); craft production in palace workshops (perfume, cloth); war and raiding (captive women at Pylos, weapons in graves)
  • judgement: trade turned local wealth (oil, cloth) into the luxuries and metals that showed power, so it was essential; but that local wealth came from the land and labour the palace controlled
