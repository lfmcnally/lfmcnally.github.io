The Palace and the Megaron
At a glance
Key facts
  • Best examples: Pylos, Tiryns and Mycenae
  • Date: mostly 14th and 13th centuries BC; destroyed by fire around 1200 BC
  • The heart: the megaron: porch, anteroom and a great hall with a round hearth and a throne
  • Run by: the wanax (king), with officials and scribes
  • Functions: home, government, storehouse, workshop, archive, shrine and feasting hall
At a glance
  • the spec asks for the typical palace complex and megaron and their functions and use: learn the layout and what each part was for
  • a palace was far more than a royal home: it was the political, economic and religious centre of its region
  • Pylos is the best evidence: it was never built over, and the fire that destroyed it baked its Linear B tablets
The palace complex
Setting, layout and decoration
Setting
  • at Mycenae and Tiryns the palace sat on the highest point of the citadel, inside the Cyclopean walls, looking down over the town and plain
  • Pylos stood on a low hill and had no great walls, so not every palace was a fortress
The typical layout
  • you entered through a propylon, a formal gateway with columns on each side
  • this led into an open courtyard, where visitors gathered and, at Tiryns, an altar stood facing the megaron
  • the megaron opened onto the courtyard, the grandest building in the complex
  • around it ran corridors to storerooms, workshops, an archive, shrines, bathrooms and living rooms; stairways show there was an upper floor, which has not survived
How it was built and decorated
  • walls of rubble and mud brick held in a timber frame, faced with plaster; only the stone footings survive
  • wooden columns on stone bases, tapering downwards in the Cretan style
  • walls covered in frescoes (processions, hunts, battles, animals) and floors of painted plaster divided into squares
  • the decoration was there to impress visitors with the ruler's wealth and power
The megaron
The great hall at the heart of the palace
The three rooms
  • the porch, open to the courtyard, with two columns across its front
  • the anteroom (vestibule), a waiting space between porch and hall
  • the hall itself: a large, nearly square room, the most important space in the palace
  • the plan is long and narrow, entered from one end; later Greek temples kept the same shape, with a porch and an inner room
Inside the hall
  • a large, round hearth in the centre: at Pylos it is about four metres across, its plaster rim painted with flame and spiral patterns
  • four columns around the hearth held up the roof; above the hearth an opening, or a raised section of roof, let out the smoke and let in light
  • the throne stood against the right-hand wall as you entered (its base survives at Pylos and Tiryns)
  • at Pylos a channel in the floor beside the throne seems to have been for pouring libations (drink offerings), so the king had a religious role
  • the Pylos throne room had a fresco of griffins and lions on either side of the throne and a lyre-player seated on a rock, a bard entertaining a feast
What the megaron was used for
  • government: the king sat in state and received visitors, envoys and officials
  • religion: the hearth and libation channel suggest rituals led by the king
  • feasting and hospitality: banquets, music and poetry, binding the king's followers to him
  • display: every visitor walked through gateway, courtyard, porch and anteroom before reaching the throne, so the plan itself built up a sense of the king's power
The palaces on the ground
Pylos, Mycenae and Tiryns
Pylos: a palace in detail

The best-preserved palace, and the best evidence for how a palace worked.

  • excavated by the American archaeologist Carl Blegen from 1939; destroyed by fire around 1200 BC, then left unbuilt-on, so its plan is unusually clear
  • an archive room by the entrance held most of the palace's Linear B tablets, where officials could check goods coming in and out
  • two pantries held nearly 3,000 drinking cups, evidence for feasts on a huge scale
  • storerooms with large jars (pithoi) set into benches held olive oil and wine; tablets and sealings record what was in them
  • a bathroom still has its painted clay bathtub in place
  • a smaller hall (often called the “queen's megaron”), workshops and shrines lay around the main building
Mycenae and Tiryns
  • Mycenae: the palace crowns the citadel, reached by a grand staircase from the west; its megaron had a painted hearth and throne room, though part of it has collapsed down the ravine
  • Tiryns: a great propylon leads to a colonnaded courtyard with a round altar facing the great megaron, whose throne base survives; there is also a smaller megaron
  • Tiryns bathroom: its floor is a single slab of limestone weighing about 20 tonnes
  • the same basic plan at all three shows a shared Mycenaean culture of kingship
Functions and use
What the palace was for, and how we know

A Mycenaean palace did the work that later Greek cities split between many buildings.

Function
Evidence
ruling: the king's seat of power
throne in the megaron; Linear B names the wanax (king) and other officials
economic: collecting, storing and handing out goods
storerooms of oil and wine jars; tablets listing goods collected from the region and rations paid to workers
record-keeping
archive room and thousands of Linear B tablets
craft production
workshops for perfumed oil, metal and ivory; tablets listing bronze-smiths given metal by the palace
religion
hearth, libation channel, shrines, and offerings to gods recorded on tablets
feasting and hospitality
nearly 3,000 cups at Pylos; the lyre-player fresco
home
living rooms, bathrooms, an upper floor
defence
at Mycenae and Tiryns, the palace sits inside Cyclopean walls
The palace was the centre of a redistribution system: produce and goods flowed in, were counted and stored, and flowed out again as rations, raw materials and gifts, all under the king's control.
Homer and the problems of the evidence
Literary sources and their limits
Homer's palaces (useful for unseen questions)
  • Odyssey 22: Odysseus kills the suitors in his megaron, with a high threshold, a great doorway and a hearth; the plan matches the archaeology well
  • Odyssey 3: at Nestor's palace in Pylos, Telemachus is given a bath by Nestor's daughter, and in the real palace of Pylos a bathtub survives
  • Odyssey 7: Odysseus enters the palace of Alcinous and supplicates the queen, then sits by the hearth among the ashes, showing the hearth as a sacred place
  • Odyssey 7 also describes walls of bronze, gold and silver doors, and gold dogs at the gate: an exaggerated, fairy-tale palace
  • Odyssey 4: Menelaus' palace at Sparta gleams with bronze, gold, silver and ivory, and Telemachus thinks it must be like the palace of Zeus
How far does Homer match?
  • matches: a central hall with a hearth, feasting with a bard, hospitality to guests, an upper floor where Penelope has her rooms
  • differs: Homer has no archive, no scribes and no storerooms full of records; his palaces are simpler households, and some are wildly exaggerated
  • the poems were composed around 750 to 700 BC, some four to five hundred years after the palaces burned, so they mix memory, later life and imagination
Problems with the evidence
  • we see foundations: walls, upper floors and roofs are gone, so reconstructions involve guesswork
  • room uses are worked out from what was found in them (cups, jars, tablets), which shows only their final use
  • the fires that preserved the tablets give a snapshot of the last months, so we see little of the palace's earlier history
Exam skills
Describing, explaining, an 8-mark answer and a 15-mark plan
Describing and explaining
  • for “describe a feature” questions: name it and give two details (the hearth: round, about four metres across, painted, surrounded by four columns)
  • for “explain the function” questions: link a feature to a use (“the archive room by the entrance shows the palace checked goods coming in”)
  • for unseen plans or photos: find the courtyard, porch and hall first, then look for hearth, columns and throne
Modelled 8-mark answer

How useful is the megaron at Pylos for understanding the power of a Mycenaean king?

What you know (AO1)
What it shows (AO2)
the throne stood against the wall of the hall, flanked by painted griffins and lions
the king was shown as powerful and almost sacred, guarded by fierce creatures
visitors passed through a gateway, courtyard, porch and anteroom to reach him
the plan built up awe before the king was seen, so it was designed to display power
a channel beside the throne was probably for pouring libations
the king had a religious role as well as a political one
the archive room near the entrance held Linear B tablets
the king controlled the economy through records, so his power was administrative too
nearly 3,000 drinking cups were found in the pantries
feasting on a huge scale bound followers to the king through hospitality
we have only the foundations, and finds show only the last months before the fire
we cannot be sure how rooms were used throughout the palace's history, so it is useful but incomplete

Judgement: very useful, because the layout, decoration and finds all point to a king with political, religious and economic power; but it shows his power as he wished it displayed, and only at the very end.

Planning a 15-mark essay

“The palace was the centre of every part of Mycenaean life.” How far do you agree?

  • agree: government (throne, wanax), economy (storerooms, tablets, rations), religion (hearth, libations, shrines), craft (workshops), feasting (cups)
  • challenge: farmers and herders lived in villages across the region, and much of their life never reached the records; houses outside the citadel at Mycenae (such as the House of the Oil Merchant) show work and trade outside the palace walls; the tablets record only what the palace cared about
  • judgement: the palace was the centre of organised life, but our evidence comes mostly from palaces, so it may exaggerate how far they controlled everyone
