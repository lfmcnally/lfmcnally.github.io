Dating the Mycenaean Age
When it was, and the gap to Homer
At a glance
  • the Mycenaean age ran from roughly 1600 to 1150 BC, in the Late Bronze Age
  • it was at its height around 1350 to 1200 BC, then collapsed
  • Homer composed his poems around 750 to 700 BC, about four hundred years later

The age and the gap to Homer
What “Mycenaean” means, and the four-hundred-year gap
What “Mycenaean” means
  • the age is named after Mycenae, the richest city, though it was never a single kingdom
  • it was many separate cities that shared a culture: similar palaces, similar art, and the same way of running things
  • each city was run from a palace, with a ruler, scribes who kept records in Linear B, and a steep social pyramid below
  • “Mycenaean” is a modern label that links these cities together
The collapse and the gap to Homer
  • around 1200 to 1100 BC this world fell apart, part of a wider breakdown across the eastern Mediterranean: the palaces were destroyed and the art of writing was lost
  • Greece entered a poorer “Dark Age”, and by Homer's day the great citadels were ancient ruins
  • their walls seemed so huge that later Greeks believed the Cyclopes had built them
  • this four-hundred-year gap is why the poems and the archaeology never quite match: Homer remembers a grander past through centuries of retelling, mixed with his own later world
The most important date in this topic is the gap. Homer wrote about four hundred years after the world he describes, so he is its distant echo rather than its witness.
How the age is dated
Pottery, layers, Egypt and science
No dates were written down
  • the Mycenaeans left no dated records: the Linear B tablets are lists, with no years and no names of kings
  • so archaeologists build dates in two steps: first put things in order (relative dating), then fix that order to years BC (absolute dating)
Putting things in order
  • stratigraphy: at a site, layers build up over time, so lower layers are older; at Troy, for example, the city is numbered in layers from Troy I (oldest) upwards
  • pottery styles: Mycenaean pots changed in shape and painted decoration over time; broken pottery is found everywhere and survives well, so it is the main tool for dating a layer, a building or a grave
  • the pottery sequence gives the age its modern name, Late Helladic (“Helladic” means mainland Greek Bronze Age), divided into LH I, II and III, with LH III split into IIIA, IIIB and IIIC
Fixing it to years BC
  • links with Egypt: Egyptian history is dated by lists of pharaohs, so where Egyptian and Mycenaean objects are found together, each dates the other; faience plaques with the name of Amenhotep III (14th century BC) were found at Mycenae, and Mycenaean pots were found at Amarna, a city lived in for only about twenty years, around 1350 to 1330 BC, so pots found there must date from then
  • scientific dating: radiocarbon dating of seeds, bone and charcoal, and tree-ring dating of timber, give independent dates
  • these methods agree roughly, but dates are usually given with “c.” (circa, “about”), since each can be decades out

Ancient dates
  • later Greeks also tried to date the age: the scholar Eratosthenes (3rd century BC) put the fall of Troy in 1184 BC
  • the historian Herodotus (5th century BC) thought Homer lived about four hundred years before his own time, so about 850 BC
  • these are ancient interpretations, worked out from family trees and stories, so they are useful for what the Greeks believed, but less reliable than the archaeology
